# 1. Module 1 — Foundational Topics

## 1.1 Supply Chain Overview

### Introduction

A supply chain is a network of organizations, people, activities, resources, information, and processes involved in producing and delivering products or services from the original supplier to the final customer.

Supply Chain Management (SCM) coordinates these interconnected activities to maximize overall value, improve operational efficiency, control costs, and satisfy customer demand.

A supply chain is not limited to transportation or purchasing. It includes the complete lifecycle of a product, beginning with raw material acquisition and ending with customer delivery and potentially product returns.

### The Three Primary Flows

**1. Material Flow**

The physical movement of products and materials throughout the supply chain.

Examples:
- Raw materials moving from suppliers to manufacturers.
- Components moving between production facilities.
- Finished goods moving into distribution centers.
- Products moving from warehouses to retailers and customers.
- Returned products moving through reverse logistics.

**2. Information Flow**

The exchange of information required to coordinate supply chain activities.

Examples:
- Customer orders.
- Demand forecasts.
- Inventory availability.
- Production schedules.
- Purchase orders.
- Shipment tracking.
- Supplier delivery notifications.

**3. Financial Flow**

The movement of money and financial resources throughout the supply chain.

Examples:
- Customer payments.
- Supplier payments.
- Procurement expenses.
- Transportation costs.
- Inventory carrying costs.
- Credit arrangements and payment terms.

### Supply Chain Participants

A typical supply chain includes:

1. Raw material suppliers.
2. Component manufacturers.
3. Manufacturers and assemblers.
4. Wholesalers and distributors.
5. Retailers.
6. End customers.
7. Logistics and transportation providers.
8. Third-party logistics providers (3PL).

### Supply Chain Objectives

The primary objective is to maximize total supply chain value.

Supply chain value can be represented as:

**Supply Chain Surplus = Customer Value − Total Supply Chain Cost**

The objective is not necessarily to minimize every individual operational cost. Instead, the organization must optimize the entire system.

For example, selecting a cheaper transportation method might increase delivery time and inventory holding costs. An efficient supply chain evaluates the combined impact rather than optimizing transportation independently.

### Push and Pull Systems

**Push-Based Supply Chain**

Operations are initiated based on forecasts and anticipated customer demand.

Characteristics:
- Forecast-driven production.
- Inventory positioned before actual orders.
- Useful for stable and predictable demand.
- Greater exposure to forecasting errors.

**Pull-Based Supply Chain**

Operations are initiated in response to actual customer demand.

Characteristics:
- Demand-driven replenishment.
- Reduced unnecessary inventory.
- Greater responsiveness to customer requirements.
- Requires reliable information and operational coordination.

**Push-Pull Boundary**

The point separating forecast-driven activities from customer-order-driven activities.

Many modern supply chains use hybrid models.

### Learning Outcomes

After completing this topic, the learner should be able to:
- Explain the structure of an end-to-end supply chain.
- Identify the different supply chain participants.
- Distinguish material, information, and financial flows.
- Explain push and pull operating models.
- Understand the relationship between operational decisions and total supply chain performance.

## 1.2 Supply Chain Strategy

### Introduction

Supply chain strategy defines how an organization configures and manages its supply chain to support its competitive business objectives.

An organization must determine how it will balance cost efficiency, operational responsiveness, product availability, delivery speed, and flexibility.

The supply chain strategy should be aligned with the organization's overall business strategy.

### Competitive Strategy

Organizations typically compete through different dimensions:

**Cost Leadership**
- Reduce procurement expenses.
- Optimize manufacturing costs.
- Improve transportation efficiency.
- Minimize unnecessary inventory.
- Increase operational productivity.

**Differentiation**
- Offer unique products.
- Provide customized services.
- Improve product quality.
- Deliver superior customer experiences.

**Responsiveness**
- Respond quickly to customer demand.
- Reduce order fulfillment time.
- Maintain flexible production capacity.
- Improve supply chain visibility.

### Efficient vs. Responsive Supply Chains

| Dimension | Efficient Supply Chain | Responsive Supply Chain |
|---|---|---|
| Primary objective | Cost minimization | Speed and flexibility |
| Demand | Predictable | Variable |
| Inventory | Optimized and relatively lean | Strategic buffers |
| Production | Stable schedules | Flexible capacity |
| Suppliers | Cost and reliability | Speed and flexibility |
| Product variety | Limited or standardized | High or customized |

An organization should choose its supply chain configuration according to demand characteristics and customer expectations.

### Strategic Fit

Strategic fit means aligning supply chain capabilities with customer requirements and competitive strategy.

For example:

- A manufacturer of standardized household products may prioritize efficiency.
- A fashion company dealing with rapidly changing trends may prioritize responsiveness.
- A pharmaceutical distributor may require high availability, strict quality controls, and reliable delivery.

### Push/Pull Strategy

The organization determines which activities should be forecast-driven and which should be initiated by actual customer demand.

Strategic considerations include:
- Demand uncertainty.
- Product variety.
- Production lead time.
- Supplier reliability.
- Customer delivery expectations.
- Inventory investment.

### Supply Chain Design Decisions

Strategic decisions include:
- Facility locations.
- Supplier network configuration.
- Manufacturing capacity.
- Distribution network design.
- Transportation infrastructure.
- Inventory positioning.
- Outsourcing decisions.

These decisions influence long-term operating costs and supply chain responsiveness.

### Learning Outcomes

- Understand the relationship between business strategy and supply chain strategy.
- Differentiate efficiency and responsiveness.
- Identify strategic trade-offs.
- Explain push, pull, and hybrid supply chain models.
- Evaluate how strategic decisions influence operational performance.

## 1.3 Supply Chain Framework & Costing

### Introduction

A supply chain framework provides a structured approach to understanding, analyzing, and evaluating supply chain activities.

It connects operational processes with financial performance and helps organizations understand how decisions in one department affect the entire business.

### Supply Chain Performance Drivers

The major operational drivers include:

**Facilities**

Physical locations where inventory is stored, products are manufactured, or services are delivered.

Important considerations:
- Facility capacity.
- Location.
- Utilization.
- Operating expenses.
- Flexibility.

**Inventory**

Materials and products maintained throughout the supply chain.

Important considerations:
- Inventory levels.
- Replenishment policies.
- Safety stock.
- Holding costs.
- Availability.

**Transportation**

The movement of products between supply chain locations.

Important considerations:
- Transportation modes.
- Delivery speed.
- Freight costs.
- Reliability.
- Shipment consolidation.

**Information**

Data used to coordinate planning and operations.

Important considerations:
- Forecast accuracy.
- Data quality.
- System integration.
- Demand visibility.
- Real-time tracking.

**Sourcing**

Decisions concerning suppliers and external resources.

Important considerations:
- Supplier reliability.
- Procurement costs.
- Lead times.
- Quality.
- Supply risk.

**Pricing**

Commercial decisions that influence demand and supply chain profitability.

Important considerations:
- Price sensitivity.
- Promotional activity.
- Demand variability.
- Revenue management.

### Supply Chain Costing

Supply chain costing evaluates the financial resources consumed throughout procurement, manufacturing, storage, transportation, and distribution.

Typical cost categories include:

1. Procurement costs.
2. Manufacturing costs.
3. Transportation costs.
4. Warehousing costs.
5. Inventory carrying costs.
6. Order processing costs.
7. Packaging costs.
8. Returns and reverse logistics costs.

### Financial and Operational Relationships

Supply chain decisions influence financial indicators.

Examples:

- Excessive inventory increases working capital requirements.
- Slow inventory turnover may indicate inefficient stock management.
- Long payment cycles affect cash flow.
- Poor transportation planning increases logistics expenses.
- Production inefficiency increases unit manufacturing costs.

### Cash-to-Cash Cycle

The Cash-to-Cash Cycle Time measures how long an organization takes to recover cash invested in operations.

**Cash-to-Cash Cycle = DIO + DSO − DPO**

Where:

- DIO = Days Inventory Outstanding.
- DSO = Days Sales Outstanding.
- DPO = Days Payables Outstanding.

### Learning Outcomes

- Identify major supply chain performance drivers.
- Understand supply chain cost structures.
- Connect operational decisions with financial performance.
- Explain the relationship between inventory, revenue, costs, and working capital.
- Understand the importance of end-to-end supply chain optimization.

# 2. Module 2 — Planning and Strategy

## 2.1 Fundamental Issues

### Introduction

Supply chain planning coordinates demand, supply, production, inventory, and organizational resources to ensure that products and services are available when required.

Planning involves managing uncertainty, understanding demand patterns, evaluating supply capabilities, and coordinating different operational functions.

### Demand Planning

Demand planning estimates future customer requirements using historical data, market information, customer behavior, and business assumptions.

Demand planning considers:

- Historical sales.
- Seasonal patterns.
- Market trends.
- Promotional campaigns.
- Customer orders.
- Economic conditions.
- Product life cycles.

### Demand Forecasting Methods

**Moving Average**

Uses the average of recent observations to estimate future demand.

**Simple Moving Average:**

Forecast = Sum of Demand During Selected Periods / Number of Periods

**Weighted Moving Average**

Assigns different weights to historical observations.

**Exponential Smoothing**

Uses previous forecasts and actual demand to update future estimates.

**Regression Analysis**

Identifies statistical relationships between demand and explanatory variables.

### Forecast Evaluation

Forecast accuracy is important because poor forecasts can create excessive inventory or product shortages.

Common metrics include:

- Mean Absolute Error (MAE).
- Mean Squared Error (MSE).
- Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE).
- Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE).
- Forecast Bias.

A forecast should be evaluated according to its intended use, demand characteristics, and data quality.

### Supply Planning

Supply planning determines how an organization will satisfy forecasted and actual demand.

It considers:
- Available inventory.
- Production capacity.
- Supplier capabilities.
- Procurement lead times.
- Workforce availability.
- Manufacturing constraints.
- Transportation capacity.

### Sales and Operations Planning (S&OP)

S&OP is a cross-functional planning process that aligns demand, supply, production, inventory, and financial objectives.

Typical participants:
- Sales.
- Marketing.
- Operations.
- Procurement.
- Finance.
- Supply chain management.

The objective is to establish one coordinated operational plan.

### Learning Outcomes

- Understand demand and supply planning.
- Explain the forecasting process.
- Identify sources of demand uncertainty.
- Evaluate forecast accuracy.
- Understand the role of S&OP in organizational coordination.

## 2.2 Aggregate Planning

### Introduction

Aggregate planning is a medium-term planning process that determines how an organization will balance expected demand with available production capacity and resources.

It usually focuses on product families or overall production volumes rather than individual products.

The objective is to establish a feasible production and workforce plan while controlling operational costs.

### Planning Horizon

Aggregate planning typically covers several months up to approximately one year, depending on the industry.

It bridges long-term strategic planning and short-term operational scheduling.

### Key Planning Variables

**Production Rate**

The quantity of products manufactured during a specific period.

**Workforce Level**

The number of employees available for production activities.

**Inventory Level**

The amount of finished goods maintained to satisfy future demand.

**Backorders**

Customer orders that cannot be fulfilled immediately.

**Overtime**

Additional working hours used to increase production capacity.

**Subcontracting**

Outsourcing part of the production requirements to external manufacturers.

### Aggregate Planning Strategies

**1. Level Strategy**

Maintains relatively stable production and workforce levels.

Demand fluctuations are managed through inventory and, where necessary, backorders.

Advantages:
- Stable workforce.
- Predictable production.
- Easier resource planning.

Disadvantages:
- Potentially higher inventory.
- Additional holding costs.

**2. Chase Strategy**

Adjusts production capacity to match changing demand.

This may involve changing workforce levels, working hours, or subcontracting.

Advantages:
- Lower inventory requirements.
- Production closer to actual demand.

Disadvantages:
- Workforce instability.
- Capacity adjustment costs.
- Operational complexity.

**3. Hybrid Strategy**

Combines level and chase strategies.

Organizations may maintain a stable core workforce while using overtime, subcontracting, or inventory buffers to manage fluctuations.

### Aggregate Planning Costs

Important cost categories include:

- Regular production costs.
- Overtime costs.
- Hiring and training costs.
- Layoff costs.
- Inventory holding costs.
- Backorder costs.
- Subcontracting costs.

### Example

A manufacturer expects the following monthly demand:

| Month | Expected Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 1,000 |
| February | 1,300 |
| March | 1,600 |
| April | 1,200 |

The organization must determine whether to:

- Maintain constant production and accumulate inventory.
- Increase and decrease production to match demand.
- Combine regular production with overtime and inventory.

The best decision depends on total operating costs, capacity, customer requirements, and workforce constraints.

### Learning Outcomes

- Understand medium-term production planning.
- Differentiate level, chase, and hybrid strategies.
- Identify aggregate planning variables.
- Evaluate capacity and inventory trade-offs.
- Understand the financial implications of production decisions.

## 2.3 Role of Inventory in the Supply Chain

### Introduction

Inventory represents materials and products maintained by an organization to support production, distribution, and customer demand.

Inventory management seeks to balance product availability against the financial and operational costs of holding stock.

Too much inventory increases storage costs and ties up working capital. Too little inventory increases the risk of stockouts, production interruptions, and lost sales.

### Types of Inventory

**Raw Materials**

Materials purchased from suppliers and used in manufacturing.

**Work in Progress (WIP)**

Products currently undergoing manufacturing or processing.

**Finished Goods**

Completed products ready for sale or distribution.

**Maintenance, Repair and Operations (MRO)**

Materials and supplies used to maintain operational activities.

### Functions of Inventory

1. Meet anticipated customer demand.
2. Protect against demand uncertainty.
3. Protect against supplier delays.
4. Support continuous manufacturing.
5. Benefit from economies of scale.
6. Separate dependent operational processes.
7. Support seasonal demand.

### Inventory Classification

**ABC Analysis**

Classifies inventory according to its relative annual consumption value.

- Category A: High-value items requiring close control.
- Category B: Medium-value items requiring regular monitoring.
- Category C: Lower-value items requiring simpler controls.

Annual Consumption Value:

Annual Demand × Unit Cost

ABC analysis commonly applies the Pareto principle, although actual category distributions depend on the inventory population.

**Cycle Counting**

A continuous inventory verification process that checks selected items periodically rather than relying exclusively on full physical inventory counts.

### Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)

EOQ determines the order quantity that minimizes the combined annual ordering and holding costs under simplified assumptions.

Formula:

EOQ = √(2DS / H)

Where:

- D = Annual demand.
- S = Ordering cost per order.
- H = Annual holding cost per unit.

### Safety Stock

Safety stock is additional inventory maintained to reduce the probability of stockouts caused by demand or supply uncertainty.

The required amount depends on:
- Demand variability.
- Lead-time variability.
- Desired service level.
- Replenishment reliability.

A simplified model with constant lead time and normally distributed demand is:

Safety Stock = Z × σd × √L

Where:

- Z = Service-level factor.
- σd = Standard deviation of demand per period.
- L = Lead time in the same demand periods.

### Reorder Point (ROP)

The reorder point indicates when a replenishment order should be placed.

ROP = Average Demand During Lead Time + Safety Stock

### Inventory Performance Metrics

**Inventory Turnover**

Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory

**Days Inventory Outstanding**

Average Inventory / Cost of Goods Sold × 365

**Stockout Rate**

Measures the frequency or proportion of demand that cannot be satisfied from available inventory.

**Inventory Carrying Cost**

Includes storage, insurance, handling, obsolescence, capital, and related holding expenses.

### Inventory Management Trade-Offs

Inventory decisions require balancing:

- Ordering costs.
- Holding costs.
- Stockout costs.
- Supplier lead times.
- Service-level requirements.
- Demand uncertainty.

### Learning Outcomes

- Identify inventory types and functions.
- Apply ABC classification.
- Understand EOQ and safety stock.
- Calculate reorder points.
- Interpret inventory turnover.
- Evaluate inventory availability and cost trade-offs.

## 2.4 Material Requirements Planning (MRP)

### Introduction

Material Requirements Planning is a production planning and inventory control system that calculates the materials and components required to manufacture products.

MRP determines:

- What materials are needed?
- How much is required?
- When are materials required?
- When should purchase or production orders be released?

It translates the production schedule into time-phased material requirements.

### MRP System Inputs

**1. Master Production Schedule (MPS)**

Specifies the quantities of finished products that must be manufactured and when they are required.

**2. Bill of Materials (BOM)**

Defines the components, materials, and quantities required to manufacture a finished product.

**3. Inventory Records**

Contain information about:
- Available inventory.
- Scheduled receipts.
- Open purchase orders.
- Existing production orders.
- Safety stock.

### MRP Processing

MRP follows a structured process:

1. Identify finished product requirements.
2. Examine the bill of materials.
3. Calculate gross material requirements.
4. Determine available inventory.
5. Calculate net requirements.
6. Consider scheduled receipts.
7. Offset requirements according to lead times.
8. Generate planned production and purchasing orders.

### Gross Requirements

The total quantity of a material needed before considering available inventory.

### Net Requirements

The actual additional quantity required after considering inventory and scheduled receipts.

A simplified calculation is:

Net Requirements = Gross Requirements − Available Inventory − Scheduled Receipts

If the result is negative, no additional quantity is required for that period, subject to safety-stock and lot-sizing rules.

### Lead-Time Offsetting

MRP schedules orders backward from the date materials are required.

If a component is needed in week 8 and its procurement lead time is two weeks, the purchase order should generally be released in week 6.

### Lot-Sizing Methods

Common lot-sizing approaches include:

- Lot-for-Lot (L4L).
- Fixed Order Quantity.
- Economic Order Quantity.
- Period Order Quantity.

### Capacity Planning

MRP identifies material requirements, but available production capacity must also be evaluated.

Capacity Requirements Planning (CRP) helps determine whether planned production can be completed using available labor and equipment.

### MRP vs. MRP II vs. ERP

| System | Main Purpose |
|---|---|
| MRP | Material requirements planning |
| MRP II | Manufacturing resource planning |
| ERP | Integration of enterprise-wide business processes |

### Learning Outcomes

- Explain the MRP process.
- Understand MPS, BOM, and inventory records.
- Calculate gross and net material requirements.
- Understand lead-time offsetting.
- Identify lot-sizing approaches.
- Explain the relationship between MRP and capacity planning.

# 3. Module 3 — Tactical Considerations

## 3.1 Sourcing

### Introduction

Sourcing is the strategic process of identifying, evaluating, selecting, and managing suppliers to obtain the materials, products, and services required by an organization.

It involves analyzing supply markets, evaluating supplier capabilities, assessing risk, and developing appropriate supplier relationships.

### Sourcing Objectives

- Ensure reliable supply.
- Achieve competitive costs.
- Improve material quality.
- Reduce procurement risk.
- Maintain supplier performance.
- Support organizational strategy.
- Build long-term supplier relationships.

### Sourcing Process

1. Identify business requirements.
2. Analyze supply market conditions.
3. Identify potential suppliers.
4. Evaluate supplier capabilities.
5. Compare supplier costs and risks.
6. Select appropriate suppliers.
7. Establish contractual arrangements.
8. Monitor supplier performance.

### Supplier Evaluation

Supplier selection should consider multiple dimensions:

**Cost**
- Unit price.
- Transportation expenses.
- Payment terms.
- Total Cost of Ownership (TCO).

**Quality**
- Product conformity.
- Defect rates.
- Certification.
- Quality control procedures.

**Delivery**
- Lead time.
- On-time delivery.
- Capacity.
- Delivery reliability.

**Risk**
- Financial stability.
- Geographic exposure.
- Political risk.
- Supplier dependency.
- Business continuity.

### Supplier Scoring

Organizations can use weighted scoring models to compare suppliers.

Example:

| Evaluation Criteria | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Cost | 30% |
| Quality | 30% |
| Delivery | 25% |
| Reliability | 15% |
| Total | 100% |

Each supplier receives a score for every criterion.

Weighted Supplier Score = Sum of (Criterion Weight × Criterion Score)

### Single vs. Multiple Sourcing

**Single Sourcing**

Purchasing a particular product or service from one supplier.

Advantages:
- Stronger supplier relationships.
- Potential volume discounts.
- Simplified coordination.

Risks:
- Higher dependency.
- Greater exposure to disruptions.

**Multiple Sourcing**

Using multiple suppliers for the same requirement.

Advantages:
- Reduced supplier dependency.
- Greater supply flexibility.
- Competitive pressure.

Risks:
- More complex management.
- Potentially lower purchasing volumes per supplier.

### Total Cost of Ownership

TCO evaluates the complete cost associated with acquiring and using a product or service.

It may include:
- Purchase price.
- Transportation.
- Inspection.
- Storage.
- Maintenance.
- Defects.
- Disposal.

### Learning Outcomes

- Understand strategic sourcing.
- Evaluate suppliers using structured criteria.
- Apply supplier scoring models.
- Distinguish single and multiple sourcing.
- Understand total cost of ownership.
- Identify supplier-related risks.

## 3.2 Purchasing

### Introduction

Purchasing is the operational process of acquiring goods and services from external suppliers.

While sourcing focuses on supplier selection and supply strategy, purchasing focuses on executing procurement transactions.

### Purchasing Process

1. Purchase requisition.
2. Supplier identification or approved supplier selection.
3. Request for quotation (RFQ).
4. Supplier quotation evaluation.
5. Purchase order creation.
6. Order confirmation.
7. Delivery and receipt.
8. Inspection and verification.
9. Invoice matching.
10. Supplier payment.

### Purchase Requisition

An internal document requesting the purchase of specific goods or services.

It usually contains:
- Item description.
- Required quantity.
- Required delivery date.
- Cost center.
- Requesting department.
- Approval information.

### Purchase Order (PO)

A formal document issued to a supplier specifying the agreed purchasing requirements.

Typical information:
- Supplier details.
- Product descriptions.
- Quantities.
- Prices.
- Delivery terms.
- Payment terms.
- Delivery location.

### Procurement Documentation

Common purchasing documents include:

- Purchase Requisition.
- Request for Information (RFI).
- Request for Quotation (RFQ).
- Request for Proposal (RFP).
- Purchase Order.
- Goods Receipt Note (GRN).
- Supplier Invoice.
- Credit Note.

### Three-Way Matching

An internal financial control that compares:

1. Purchase Order.
2. Goods Receipt.
3. Supplier Invoice.

The objective is to verify that the organization ordered, received, and was invoiced for the correct products and quantities.

### Electronic Data Interchange (EDI)

EDI enables organizations to exchange standardized business documents electronically.

Examples:
- Purchase orders.
- Shipping notices.
- Invoices.
- Order confirmations.

EDI reduces manual processing and improves transaction accuracy.

### Purchasing Performance Metrics

- Purchase Price Variance.
- Supplier On-Time Delivery.
- Purchase Order Cycle Time.
- Procurement Cost Savings.
- Supplier Defect Rate.
- Invoice Accuracy.

### Learning Outcomes

- Understand the purchasing lifecycle.
- Distinguish sourcing from purchasing.
- Identify procurement documentation.
- Explain purchase order management.
- Understand three-way matching.
- Describe EDI and purchasing automation.

## 3.3 Manufacturing

### Introduction

Manufacturing transforms raw materials and components into finished products through production processes.

Manufacturing management coordinates materials, labor, machinery, production schedules, and quality requirements to achieve the required output.

### Manufacturing Strategies

**Make-to-Stock (MTS)**

Products are manufactured based on forecasts and stored as finished goods before customer orders are received.

**Make-to-Order (MTO)**

Production begins after receiving a confirmed customer order.

**Assemble-to-Order (ATO)**

Standard components are prepared in advance and assembled after customer orders are received.

**Engineer-to-Order (ETO)**

Products are designed or engineered according to individual customer requirements.

### Facility Layout Strategies

**Product Layout**

Equipment is arranged according to the sequence of production operations.

Suitable for high-volume standardized manufacturing.

**Process Layout**

Similar machines or activities are grouped together.

Suitable for lower-volume production with greater product variety.

**Fixed-Position Layout**

The product remains stationary while workers, equipment, and materials move around it.

Suitable for large projects such as shipbuilding and construction.

**Cellular Layout**

Machines are grouped into manufacturing cells designed for families of similar products.

### Manufacturing Technologies

Modern manufacturing can involve:

- Computer Numerical Control (CNC).
- Robotics.
- Automated production lines.
- Manufacturing Execution Systems (MES).
- Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT).
- Computer-Aided Design (CAD).
- Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM).

### Production Performance

Important indicators include:

**Production Throughput**

The number of units produced during a given period.

**Capacity Utilization**

Actual Output / Available Capacity × 100

**Production Yield**

Good Units Produced / Total Units Produced × 100

**Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE)**

OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality

OEE measures the effectiveness of manufacturing equipment.

### Manufacturing Bottlenecks

A bottleneck is a resource or process that limits the output of the entire production system.

Identifying and managing bottlenecks improves throughput and reduces delays.

### Learning Outcomes

- Understand manufacturing processes.
- Differentiate MTS, MTO, ATO, and ETO.
- Identify facility layout strategies.
- Understand production capacity.
- Evaluate manufacturing performance.
- Recognize bottlenecks and operational constraints.

## 3.4 Transportation

### Introduction

Transportation is the movement of raw materials, components, and finished products between supply chain locations.

It directly affects delivery time, logistics costs, inventory positioning, customer satisfaction, and supply chain responsiveness.

### Transportation Modes

**Road Transportation**

Suitable for flexible regional and local distribution.

Advantages:
- Door-to-door delivery.
- Route flexibility.
- Suitable for short and medium distances.

Limitations:
- Traffic congestion.
- Fuel costs.
- Road capacity restrictions.

**Rail Transportation**

Suitable for large quantities over medium and long distances.

Advantages:
- High freight capacity.
- Economies of scale.
- Suitable for bulk materials.

Limitations:
- Fixed routes.
- Terminal handling requirements.
- Limited door-to-door flexibility.

**Air Transportation**

Suitable for urgent, high-value, or time-sensitive shipments.

Advantages:
- High speed.
- International reach.
- Reduced transit time.

Limitations:
- High transportation costs.
- Capacity restrictions.
- Weight and size limitations.

**Water Transportation**

Suitable for international freight and large-volume shipments.

Advantages:
- High carrying capacity.
- Low cost per unit for many bulk shipments.
- Suitable for international trade.

Limitations:
- Longer transit times.
- Port dependency.
- Weather and maritime disruptions.

**Pipeline Transportation**

Suitable for specific liquids and gases.

Advantages:
- Continuous transportation.
- High-volume movement.
- Low labor requirements during operation.

Limitations:
- High initial investment.
- Limited product applicability.
- Fixed infrastructure.

### Transportation Network Design

Organizations must determine:

- Which transportation mode to use.
- Which routes to follow.
- Where distribution facilities should be located.
- How shipments should be consolidated.
- How transportation capacity should be allocated.

### Transportation Trade-Offs

A faster transportation mode may increase freight expenses but reduce inventory holding costs.

For example, air freight may be more expensive than ocean freight, but it can reduce the time products spend in transit.

The optimal decision should consider total logistics cost and customer service requirements.

### Transportation Performance Metrics

- Freight Cost per Unit.
- Cost per Kilometer.
- On-Time Delivery.
- Transit Time.
- Vehicle Utilization.
- Load Factor.
- Damage Rate.
- Delivery Reliability.

### Learning Outcomes

- Understand transportation's role in supply chains.
- Compare transportation modes.
- Evaluate transportation network decisions.
- Understand shipment consolidation.

## 4.2 Lean & Continuous Improvement Tools

### Introduction

Lean management focuses on maximizing customer value while eliminating activities that consume resources without creating value.

Continuous improvement involves systematically identifying operational problems, analyzing their causes, implementing improvements, and monitoring results.

### The Eight Common Categories of Waste

The Lean framework commonly identifies eight forms of waste:

**1. Defects**

Errors requiring correction, replacement, or rework.

**2. Overproduction**

Producing more than required or earlier than needed.

**3. Waiting**

Idle time caused by delays, missing materials, or unavailable resources.

**4. Non-Utilized Talent**

Failing to use employees' knowledge, skills, and capabilities.

**5. Transportation**

Unnecessary movement of products and materials.

**6. Inventory**

Excessive inventory that increases holding costs and hides operational problems.

**7. Motion**

Unnecessary movement of employees and equipment.

**8. Extra Processing**

Activities that do not add value from the customer's perspective.

### Lean Tools

**5S Methodology**

A workplace organization method:

- Sort.
- Set in Order.
- Shine.
- Standardize.
- Sustain.

**Value Stream Mapping (VSM)**

A visual technique used to understand material and information flows and identify waste.

**Kaizen**

Continuous, incremental improvement involving employees throughout the organization.

**Kanban**

A visual signaling mechanism used to control workflow and replenish materials according to demand.

**Just-in-Time (JIT)**

An approach that aims to provide materials and products when they are needed, reducing unnecessary inventory.

**Standard Work**

Documented and repeatable procedures designed to improve consistency and operational performance.

### Six Sigma

Six Sigma focuses on reducing process variation and defects through structured, data-driven improvement.

The DMAIC methodology includes:

1. Define.
2. Measure.
3. Analyze.
4. Improve.
5. Control.

### Root Cause Analysis

Root cause analysis identifies the underlying reasons behind operational problems.

Common tools:

**5 Whys**

Repeatedly asks why a problem occurred to uncover its underlying causes.

**Fishbone Diagram**

Organizes potential causes into categories such as people, methods, machines, materials, measurement, and environment.

**Pareto Analysis**

Prioritizes the causes contributing most significantly to a problem.

### Theory of Constraints (TOC)

TOC focuses on identifying and managing the primary constraint limiting system performance.

The five focusing steps are:

1. Identify the constraint.
2. Exploit the constraint.
3. Subordinate other activities to it.
4. Elevate the constraint.
5. Repeat the process.

### Learning Outcomes

- Identify operational waste.
- Understand Lean principles.
- Apply 5S, Kaizen, and Kanban.
- Understand Value Stream Mapping.
- Explain DMAIC.
- Identify root causes of operational problems.
- Understand bottleneck management.

# 5. Quantitative Methods & Analytical Techniques

Supply chain analysis uses quantitative methods to support operational planning and decision-making.

The following techniques are especially relevant.

| Technique | Main Application |
|---|---|
| ABC Analysis | Inventory classification |
| EOQ | Optimal order quantity |
| Safety Stock | Protection against uncertainty |
| Reorder Point | Replenishment decisions |
| Moving Average | Demand forecasting |
| Exponential Smoothing | Demand prediction |
| Regression Analysis | Identifying relationships |
| Time-Series Analysis | Trend and seasonal analysis |
| Pareto Analysis | Problem prioritization |
| Weighted Scoring | Supplier evaluation |
| Cost-Benefit Analysis | Evaluating alternatives |
| Scenario Analysis | Risk and uncertainty |
| Capacity Analysis | Production planning |
| Optimization | Resource allocation |
| Root Cause Analysis | Operational problem solving |

## Essential Formulas

### Inventory

**Economic Order Quantity**

EOQ = √(2DS / H)

**Reorder Point**

ROP = Demand During Lead Time + Safety Stock

**Inventory Turnover**

Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory

**Days Inventory Outstanding**

DIO = Average Inventory / Cost of Goods Sold × 365

### Forecasting

**Mean Absolute Error**

MAE = Σ|Actual − Forecast| / n

**Mean Squared Error**

MSE = Σ(Actual − Forecast)² / n

**Mean Absolute Percentage Error**

MAPE = (100 / n) × Σ|Actual − Forecast| / |Actual|

MAPE requires special care when actual demand is zero or close to zero.

### Operations

**Capacity Utilization**

Actual Output / Available Capacity × 100

**Production Yield**

Good Units / Total Units Produced × 100

**OEE**

Availability × Performance × Quality

### Financial Performance

**Cash-to-Cash Cycle**

DIO + DSO − DPO

These formulas are simplified representations. Real operational models must account for the assumptions, measurement periods, and business context behind each calculation.

## Supply Chain Performance Dimensions

A useful performance framework evaluates five broad dimensions:
