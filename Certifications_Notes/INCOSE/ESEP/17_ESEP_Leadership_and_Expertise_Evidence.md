# 17. ESEP Leadership and Expertise Evidence

## 1. Purpose

This module helps organize evidence of senior-level systems engineering experience, technical leadership, and professional expertise. It focuses on demonstrating how engineering decisions influenced systems, teams, organizations, stakeholders, and lifecycle outcomes.

These notes are preparation material, not official INCOSE eligibility criteria. Confirm current ESEP requirements, experience definitions, and evidence expectations against the official INCOSE application guidance.

## 2. Learning Objectives

* Explain the difference between participation, technical responsibility, and leadership.
* Identify evidence that demonstrates systems engineering expertise.
* Structure professional examples around context, actions, decisions, and outcomes.
* Connect experience to lifecycle processes and systems engineering competencies.
* Document technical influence without exaggerating personal responsibility.
* Prepare a traceable portfolio of professional evidence.
* Identify gaps that require further experience or documentation.

## 3. Understanding Leadership and Expertise

### 3.1 What Is Systems Engineering Expertise?

Systems engineering expertise involves applying engineering principles, technical judgment, lifecycle knowledge, and systems thinking to complex problems.

Evidence may include:

* Resolving conflicting stakeholder needs.
* Establishing or reviewing system requirements.
* Making architecture trade-offs.
* Managing system interfaces.
* Leading integration or verification activities.
* Assessing technical risk.
* Resolving cross-disciplinary engineering conflicts.
* Improving engineering processes.
* Mentoring engineers and developing organizational capability.

Expertise is demonstrated by the quality and impact of engineering judgment, not simply by familiarity with terminology or tools.

### 3.2 Participation Versus Responsibility Versus Leadership

**Participation** means contributing to an activity.

Example: Contributing test results during an integration review.

**Responsibility** means being accountable for a defined work product or technical activity.

Example: Owning the verification plan for a subsystem and ensuring that its requirements are adequately covered.

**Leadership** means influencing direction, coordinating decisions, resolving difficult issues, and taking responsibility for broader technical outcomes.

Example: Leading a cross-functional team to resolve architecture conflicts affecting multiple subsystems and securing agreement on a system-level solution.

All three can be valuable experience. They should be described accurately rather than presented as equivalent.

### 3.3 Dimensions of Leadership Evidence

| Dimension             | Evidence to collect                                               |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Technical depth       | Difficult engineering problems, analysis, and justified decisions |
| Systems thinking      | Consideration of interfaces, dependencies, and lifecycle effects  |
| Decision-making       | Alternatives, trade-offs, assumptions, and rationale              |
| Influence             | Stakeholder alignment and resolution of technical disagreements   |
| Delivery              | Verified outcomes, milestones, and corrective actions             |
| Risk management       | Identification, analysis, mitigation, and escalation              |
| Organizational impact | Improved processes, standards, or engineering capability          |
| Mentoring             | Guidance, knowledge transfer, and development of others           |
| Professional judgment | Ethical decisions, uncertainty management, and accountability     |

## 4. Mapping Experience to Systems Engineering

### 4.1 Lifecycle Perspective

Professional evidence should not focus exclusively on design or implementation.

Consider contributions across relevant lifecycle activities:

1. Business or mission analysis.
2. Stakeholder needs and requirements definition.
3. System requirements definition.
4. Architecture definition.
5. Design definition.
6. System analysis.
7. Implementation.
8. Integration.
9. Verification.
10. Validation.
11. Transition.
12. Operation.
13. Maintenance.
14. Disposal.

Not every role includes every process. The purpose is to understand the breadth of your experience and identify areas where you have made meaningful contributions.

### 4.2 Technical Decision Evidence

For each significant decision, record:

* The problem or opportunity.
* The system boundary and affected stakeholders.
* The requirements and constraints.
* The alternatives considered.
* The analytical methods used.
* The decision criteria.
* The selected approach and rationale.
* The risks accepted or mitigated.
* The outcome and supporting evidence.
* Your specific role and authority.

This format shows engineering judgment more clearly than simply listing technologies used.

### 4.3 Leadership Without a Formal Manager Title

Leadership does not always require direct reports or a management title.

Examples may include:

* Coordinating technical work across disciplines.
* Leading a requirements or architecture review.
* Resolving an interface issue between teams.
* Establishing a reusable engineering process.
* Mentoring junior colleagues.
* Influencing a design decision through analysis.
* Escalating a critical technical risk appropriately.

However, describe the actual scope of influence. Informal leadership should not be misrepresented as formal managerial authority.

## 5. Building an Evidence Portfolio

### 5.1 Evidence Sources

Potential sources include:

* Approved engineering documents.
* Requirements and traceability records.
* Architecture decisions.
* Review minutes and action logs.
* Risk registers.
* Verification and validation reports.
* Change requests and impact assessments.
* Project plans and responsibility assignments.
* Performance reports.
* Training and mentoring records.
* Reference letters from people with direct knowledge of the work.

Use only materials you are authorized to retain or share. Remove confidential, proprietary, personal, and security-sensitive information when necessary.

### 5.2 Evidence Quality

Strong evidence is:

**Specific:** It identifies a concrete situation and contribution.

**Verifiable:** It can be corroborated through appropriate records or references.

**Relevant:** It relates to systems engineering expertise and responsibility.

**Accurate:** It reflects the actual role and authority held.

**Outcome-oriented:** It explains what changed because of the work.

**Contextual:** It clarifies constraints, complexity, and significance.

A long list of responsibilities is not necessarily strong evidence if it does not explain decisions or outcomes.

### 5.3 Confidentiality and Evidence

A professional application does not justify disclosing protected information.

When a project is confidential, describe the engineering problem at an appropriate level, remove sensitive identifiers, and ask the relevant organization or reference whether the evidence can be shared.

Do not fabricate metrics, invent documents, or imply that an unverified outcome was achieved.

## 6. Structuring Professional Examples

Use the following structure to prepare a professional example.

### Situation

Describe the system, stakeholders, operating context, and technical challenge.

### Responsibility

State your actual role, decision authority, and contribution.

### Engineering Actions

Explain the analysis, methods, coordination, and technical decisions.

### Outcome

Describe the result, including evidence where available.

### Reflection

Explain what you learned and how the experience improved your engineering judgment.

### Example Template

**Title:** Cross-subsystem interface resolution

* **Situation:** Two subsystems used incompatible assumptions about message timing.
* **Responsibility:** I was responsible for assessing the interface impact and coordinating the technical investigation.
* **Actions:** I reviewed interface requirements, compared alternative timing approaches, assessed downstream dependencies, and coordinated a controlled update.
* **Outcome:** The selected approach was documented and verified through the agreed integration tests.
* **Reflection:** The experience reinforced the importance of explicit interface contracts and early cross-team validation.

This is an illustrative example. Replace it with a real experience and do not claim the described actions or results unless they occurred.

## 8. Gap Analysis

After organizing the evidence, identify:

* Processes where you have direct experience.
* Areas where you contributed but did not own the decision.
* Activities where evidence is weak or unavailable.
* Competencies supported mainly by theoretical study.
* Experience that could be strengthened through future assignments.
* References who can credibly verify the work.

Do not treat reading about a process as equivalent to having performed it professionally.

## 9. Exercises

### Exercise 1: Reframe a Responsibility

Rewrite this statement as an evidence-based professional example:

"Worked on system integration and solved technical problems."

Include context, personal contribution, engineering method, and outcome.

### Exercise 2: Identify Leadership

Describe a situation in which an engineer influenced a decision without having formal managerial authority. Explain the evidence that demonstrates leadership.

### Exercise 3: Evidence Quality

A candidate claims to have reduced system failures by 40%, but has no baseline, measurement method, or supporting record. What should be done before including this claim?

## 10. Suggested Answers

**Exercise 1:** A strong answer identifies the system and integration problem, the candidate's specific role, the analysis or coordination performed, the decision made, and how the result was verified. It should avoid vague claims and unsupported impact statements.

**Exercise 2:** Evidence may include coordinating a technical review, presenting a defensible analysis, gaining agreement from affected teams, and ensuring implementation and verification. Leadership is demonstrated through actual influence and responsibility.

**Exercise 3:** Verify the baseline, time period, metric definition, measurement method, and source records. If the figure cannot be substantiated, remove it or describe the outcome qualitatively without inventing precision.

## 11. Key Takeaways

* Expertise is demonstrated through engineering judgment and credible outcomes.
* Participation, responsibility, and leadership should be distinguished accurately.
* Professional evidence should show context, personal contribution, decisions, and results.
* Lifecycle breadth matters, but experience claims must remain truthful.
* References should have direct knowledge of the experience they confirm.
* Confidentiality and professional integrity apply throughout application preparation.
