# 20. ESEP Professional Practice and Ethics

## 1. Purpose

This module covers the ethical responsibilities of systems engineering professionals. It examines public safety, honesty, competence, conflicts of interest, confidentiality, responsible decision-making, and accountability throughout the system lifecycle.

Ethics is not separate from technical engineering. Ethical judgment affects requirements, architecture, risk acceptance, verification, release decisions, operation, and retirement.

## 2. Learning Objectives

* Explain the relationship between engineering ethics and public welfare.
* Identify professional responsibilities when safety or system integrity is threatened.
* Recognize conflicts of interest and misleading technical claims.
* Understand confidentiality, intellectual property, and responsible information handling.
* Apply ethical reasoning to difficult engineering decisions.
* Explain professional accountability and appropriate escalation.
* Understand how ethical practice supports public trust and engineering quality.

## 3. Core Concepts

### 3.1 Professional Ethics

Professional ethics consists of principles and responsibilities that guide conduct when making decisions that affect clients, employers, colleagues, the public, and the environment.

Ethical engineering requires more than following minimum rules. It includes exercising sound judgment, communicating honestly, recognizing limitations, and considering the consequences of engineering decisions.

### 3.2 Public Safety and Welfare

The safety and welfare of people must receive appropriate priority in engineering decisions.

Systems engineers should identify hazards, communicate material risks, ensure that appropriate analysis is performed, and avoid concealing information that could affect safety.

Examples of unethical conduct include:

* Concealing a known safety-critical defect.
* Falsifying verification results.
* Approving a system without required evidence.
* Misrepresenting unresolved hazards as closed.
* Ignoring an unacceptable risk because a deadline is approaching.

The appropriate response depends on the facts, applicable law, professional obligations, and authorized safety processes. Where serious risks remain unresolved, they must be escalated through appropriate channels.

### 3.3 Honesty and Integrity

Professional integrity requires accurate representation of:

* Technical results.
* Engineering assumptions.
* Test coverage and limitations.
* Personal qualifications and experience.
* Project status and unresolved risks.
* Sources of data and analytical methods.
* The certainty of conclusions.

An engineer should distinguish measured results from estimates, assumptions from verified facts, and recommendations from approved decisions.

### 3.4 Competence and Professional Development

Professionals should undertake work within their competence or obtain suitable supervision, collaboration, or expert support.

Responsible practice includes:

* Recognizing knowledge gaps.
* Seeking independent review where necessary.
* Keeping technical knowledge current.
* Understanding the limitations of tools and models.
* Avoiding unsupported claims of expertise.
* Communicating when available evidence is insufficient.

Professional development should improve practical judgment, not merely increase the number of certificates held.

### 3.5 Accountability

Accountability means accepting responsibility for one's professional actions, decisions, and assigned obligations.

It includes documenting important decisions, correcting errors, reporting material issues, and participating in corrective action.

Accountability does not mean accepting blame for every team outcome. It means accurately describing one's contribution and fulfilling the responsibilities associated with one's role.

## 4. Ethical Decision-Making Framework

When facing an ethical dilemma, use a structured process.

### Step 1: Define the Problem

Describe the situation objectively. Separate observed facts from assumptions and opinions.

### Step 2: Identify Stakeholders

Consider users, operators, maintainers, customers, colleagues, suppliers, the public, and the environment where relevant.

### Step 3: Identify Obligations

Review applicable laws, regulations, contracts, professional codes, organizational policies, and technical standards.

### Step 4: Identify Risks and Consequences

Assess potential harm, uncertainty, reversibility, severity, and who may bear the consequences.

### Step 5: Develop Alternatives

Identify possible actions, including mitigation, additional analysis, independent review, delay, or escalation.

### Step 6: Evaluate the Alternatives

Compare the alternatives against safety, honesty, professional duties, stakeholder impacts, and applicable requirements.

### Step 7: Consult and Escalate

Seek advice from appropriate technical, safety, legal, compliance, or professional authorities when necessary.

### Step 8: Make and Document the Decision

Ensure that the decision is made by an appropriately authorized person and that the rationale is recorded.

### Step 9: Verify the Outcome

Confirm that agreed corrective actions occurred and that the underlying issue was addressed.

## 5. Conflicts of Interest

A conflict of interest occurs when a personal, financial, professional, or other interest could improperly influence—or reasonably appear to influence—professional judgment.

Examples include:

* Recommending a supplier because of an undisclosed financial relationship.
* Selecting a product without disclosing a relevant personal interest.
* Altering technical evaluations to favor a preferred vendor.
* Using privileged information for personal benefit.

Responsible handling includes:

1. Identifying the potential conflict.
2. Disclosing it to the appropriate authority.
3. Following the organization's conflict-management policy.
4. Withdrawing from decisions when required.
5. Documenting the agreed resolution.

A conflict does not always mean misconduct has occurred. Failure to disclose or manage a material conflict can undermine trust and decision quality.

## 6. Confidentiality and Intellectual Property

### 6.1 Confidential Information

Engineers may handle proprietary designs, customer information, security-sensitive data, personal information, or commercially sensitive records.

Professional practice requires following applicable authorization, data-handling, and disclosure rules.

Do not assume that information can be shared merely because it is technically interesting or useful in an application, portfolio, presentation, or interview.

### 6.2 Responsible Use of Engineering Artifacts

Before sharing a document, diagram, dataset, or technical example, determine:

* Who owns the information?
* Is the material confidential or restricted?
* Is external disclosure authorized?
* Does it contain personal or security-sensitive information?
* Can the example be anonymized or generalized?
* Are attribution or licensing obligations applicable?

Never remove sensitive markings and then assume the underlying information is safe to disclose.

### 6.3 Intellectual Property and Attribution

Give appropriate credit for other people's work and follow applicable licensing and ownership rules.

Do not present another person's analysis, publication, design, or software as your own. Clearly distinguish your contribution from collaborative work.

## 7. Ethical Issues in Data, Software, and AI-Enabled Systems

Modern systems engineering frequently involves data-driven software and AI-enabled components. These systems create additional ethical concerns.

### 7.1 Data Quality and Representation

Engineers should consider:

* Data accuracy and provenance.
* Missing or biased data.
* Representativeness of test datasets.
* Privacy and permitted use.
* Data retention and access control.
* The effect of data limitations on system behavior.

### 7.2 Model Limitations

An AI model's output should not be described as reliable or validated beyond the available evidence.

Responsible practice may require:

* Appropriate testing and evaluation.
* Analysis of known failure modes.
* Clear communication of uncertainty.
* Human oversight where appropriate.
* Monitoring for performance changes.
* Defined fallback behavior.
* Documentation of intended use and limitations.

### 7.3 Automation and Human Responsibility

Automation does not eliminate professional accountability.

Engineers should consider whether users can understand system behavior, recognize failures, intervene appropriately, and recover from incorrect or unexpected outputs.

The degree of oversight should be appropriate to the consequences of failure and the system's operating context.

## 8. Environmental and Lifecycle Responsibility

Ethical engineering considers consequences beyond initial delivery.

Relevant issues include:

* Energy and resource consumption.
* Repairability and maintainability.
* Waste and hazardous materials.
* Long-term environmental effects.
* Accessibility and equitable impacts.
* Safe decommissioning and disposal.
* Operational reliability and resilience.

A system that meets its immediate performance targets may still be inadequate if its lifecycle impacts are ignored.

## 9. Reporting Technical and Ethical Concerns

### 9.1 Appropriate Escalation

When a concern arises, use the applicable technical, safety, quality, compliance, or management process.

A responsible approach is to:

1. Record the concern factually.
2. Identify supporting evidence and uncertainty.
3. Assess potential consequences.
4. Notify the appropriate responsible person.
5. Request a documented assessment or corrective action.
6. Escalate if the issue remains unresolved and the risk warrants it.
7. Preserve relevant records according to policy.
8. Follow applicable reporting and legal requirements.

### 9.2 Avoiding Retaliation and Concealment

Professional environments should encourage good-faith reporting of genuine concerns.

Do not retaliate against colleagues for raising legitimate safety or integrity issues. Equally, reports should be made responsibly, with factual information and without knowingly making false allegations.

If internal channels are ineffective or inappropriate, consult the applicable law, professional code, and authorized external reporting mechanisms.

## 10. Ethical Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Incomplete Verification

A manager asks you to mark a requirement as verified even though the required test has not been performed.

**Ethical response:** Do not falsify the record. Explain the evidence gap, document the actual verification status, identify available alternatives, and escalate if necessary.

### Scenario 2: Hidden Supplier Conflict

An engineer recommends a supplier while withholding a financial relationship with that supplier.

**Ethical response:** Disclose the conflict through the appropriate process and ensure the supplier decision is evaluated impartially.

### Scenario 3: Unreliable AI Output

A team wants to market an AI-enabled monitoring feature as highly reliable despite limited validation data.

**Ethical response:** Communicate the evidence limitations, avoid unsupported performance claims, establish appropriate evaluation and monitoring, and obtain the required approvals before making claims or releasing the feature.

### Scenario 4: Confidential Portfolio Material

A candidate wants to include a proprietary architecture diagram in a professional application.

**Ethical response:** Verify disclosure authorization. If sharing is not permitted, use an approved, anonymized description or a non-confidential example that demonstrates the relevant engineering contribution.

### Scenario 5: Schedule Pressure and Safety

A project is asked to release a system while a significant hazard remains unresolved.

**Ethical response:** Ensure the hazard is assessed and communicated through the appropriate safety process. Do not conceal the issue or bypass mandatory controls. The authorized decision-maker must determine the permitted course of action under the applicable safety and regulatory framework.

## 11. Exercises

### Exercise 1: Ethical Analysis

A verification report contains a failed test. A colleague suggests omitting the result because the next software version may fix the problem.

Explain the ethical issues and identify an appropriate response.

### Exercise 2: Professional Competence

You are asked to approve a complex safety analysis outside your area of expertise. What should you do?

### Exercise 3: Data Privacy

A development team wants to use customer records to improve an AI model but has not checked the permitted purpose of the data.

Identify the questions that must be resolved before proceeding.

### Exercise 4: Accountability

A system fails because several teams misunderstood an interface requirement. Explain how to report the failure constructively without concealing your own contribution or unfairly blaming another team.

## 12. Suggested Answers

**Exercise 1:** Omitting a failed test would misrepresent the evidence and undermine verification integrity. Record the result, assess its significance, investigate the cause, and use the established defect and corrective-action process. A later fix does not erase the historical result.

**Exercise 2:** Disclose the limitation, request review by a suitably qualified expert, and avoid approving conclusions you cannot competently assess. You may contribute within your expertise while ensuring that the required independent or specialist assessment occurs.

**Exercise 3:** Verify the lawful and authorized purpose, privacy obligations, data minimization, access controls, retention rules, security measures, data provenance, and any required consent or approval. Evaluate whether anonymized or synthetic data could meet the need. Follow applicable law and organizational policy.

**Exercise 4:** Document the interface misunderstanding, the resulting failure, the decisions and assumptions involved, and the corrective actions. Identify your own role accurately, investigate the process weaknesses, and agree on preventive measures such as clearer interface specifications and earlier integration testing.

## 13. Professional Application and Evidence

When documenting ethical practice, consider examples where you:

* Reported a material technical concern.
* Refused to misrepresent engineering evidence.
* Disclosed or managed a conflict of interest.
* Protected confidential information.
* Recognized a limitation in your competence.
* Ensured appropriate review of a safety-critical decision.
* Communicated uncertainty in a technical recommendation.
* Improved a process to prevent recurrence of an ethical or technical failure.

Describe these examples factually and protect any confidential or sensitive information.

## 14. Key Takeaways

* Public safety and welfare are central responsibilities of engineering professionals.
* Honesty requires accurate reporting of evidence, uncertainty, and limitations.
* Competence includes recognizing when expert assistance is necessary.
* Conflicts of interest should be disclosed and managed.
* Confidentiality and intellectual property obligations continue during professional applications and knowledge sharing.
* AI-enabled systems require responsible treatment of data, model limitations, and human oversight.
* Ethical concerns should be documented and escalated through appropriate channels.
* Professional accountability includes learning from failures and preventing recurrence.
