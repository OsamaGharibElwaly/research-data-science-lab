# 19. ESEP Application Review and Interview Preparation

## 1. Purpose

This module prepares candidates to review their ESEP application, explain their professional experience, and respond to questions about systems engineering judgment, leadership, trade-offs, and professional practice.

The interview or review process may vary with current INCOSE procedures. Confirm whether an interview, additional assessment, or clarification is required for your application before relying on any specific process described here.

## 2. Learning Objectives

* Conduct a structured application quality review.
* Explain technical decisions using clear, evidence-based reasoning.
* Prepare examples of leadership, complex problem-solving, and stakeholder coordination.
* Respond to questions about uncertainty, risk, and engineering trade-offs.
* Demonstrate lifecycle and systems-level thinking.
* Handle questions about experience gaps and unsuccessful decisions honestly.
* Prepare a professional application review and interview checklist.

## 3. Application Review Before Submission

### 3.1 Completeness Review

Verify that the application addresses every current requirement.

Check:

* Eligibility and required experience.
* Education and any required credentials.
* Experience dates and calculations.
* Required experience descriptions.
* Leadership and professional contribution evidence.
* Reference qualifications and submission status.
* Required declarations and signatures.
* Payment and submission instructions.
* Supporting documents and their permitted formats.
* Confidentiality and authorization requirements.

Use the current official checklist as the controlling source.

### 3.2 Consistency Review

Check that the following agree throughout the application:

* Position titles and dates.
* Employer and project descriptions.
* Personal responsibility and decision authority.
* Claims about leadership and outcomes.
* Supporting records.
* Reference descriptions.
* Contributions to professional practice.

A discrepancy does not always mean a claim is wrong, but unexplained inconsistencies can make an application difficult to evaluate.

### 3.3 Evidence Review

For each significant claim, ask:

1. Is the claim accurate?
2. Is the scope of my responsibility clear?
3. Can I explain the engineering context?
4. Is there evidence or a credible reference?
5. Can I explain the result without exaggeration?
6. Have I protected confidential information?

Remove unsupported numerical claims and distinguish estimates from measured results.

## 4. Preparing Technical Experience Examples

### 4.1 Use a Structured Answer

A useful structure is:

* **Context:** What system and engineering problem were involved?
* **Responsibility:** What were you personally accountable for?
* **Analysis:** What information, constraints, and alternatives did you consider?
* **Decision:** What action or recommendation did you make, and why?
* **Outcome:** What happened, and how was the result assessed?
* **Learning:** What would you retain or improve in future work?

This structure helps reviewers understand the quality of your engineering judgment.

### 4.2 Prepare a Portfolio of Examples

Prepare genuine examples covering a range of topics:

* Conflicting stakeholder requirements.
* Architecture alternatives and trade-offs.
* Interface or integration problems.
* Technical risk identification and mitigation.
* Verification or validation challenges.
* Configuration and change control.
* Cross-disciplinary disagreement.
* A difficult decision under time or resource constraints.
* A failure, unexpected result, or lesson learned.
* Mentoring or improving an engineering process.
* Lifecycle or enterprise-level thinking.

Choose examples from actual work. Academic projects, prototypes, and training exercises should be identified accurately and not presented as commercial or operational experience.

### 4.3 Explain Your Personal Contribution

Prefer:

"I compared three architecture alternatives against performance, maintainability, and interface constraints, then recommended the option that best met the agreed decision criteria."

Avoid:

"We did architecture and selected a solution."

The first statement communicates a specific contribution. The second may be accurate but does not make individual responsibility clear.

Use "I" for your own actions and "we" when describing collective outcomes.

## 5. Systems Engineering Questions

### 5.1 Requirements and Stakeholders

Possible questions:

* How do you resolve conflicting stakeholder needs?
* How do you distinguish stakeholder needs from system requirements?
* How do you handle changing requirements?
* How do you establish bidirectional traceability?
* How do you determine whether a requirement is verifiable?

A strong answer should discuss context, measurable criteria, stakeholder agreement, impact analysis, traceability, and verification planning.

### 5.2 Architecture and Trade-Offs

Possible questions:

* How do you compare architecture alternatives?
* How do you identify critical interfaces?
* What do you do when no alternative satisfies every requirement?
* How do you balance performance, cost, reliability, and maintainability?

Explain the decision criteria, assumptions, constraints, uncertainty, and consequences. Avoid presenting a trade-off as a purely subjective preference.

### 5.3 Integration, Verification, and Validation

Possible questions:

* Why can individually tested components fail during integration?
* How do verification and validation differ?
* How do you manage interface changes?
* What evidence is required before operational release?

Discuss interface compatibility, configuration control, integration sequencing, system-level behavior, test evidence, and operational acceptance criteria.

### 5.4 Risk and Uncertainty

Possible questions:

* How do you prioritize technical risks?
* What do you do when data is insufficient?
* How do you handle a high-impact risk close to a deadline?
* When should a risk be escalated?

Explain the risk context, likelihood and consequences where estimable, assumptions, mitigation options, residual exposure, decision authority, and monitoring plan.

### 5.5 Leadership and Governance

Possible questions:

* How do you influence a technical decision without formal authority?
* How do you resolve disagreements between engineering disciplines?
* How do you know when a decision requires escalation?
* How do you ensure that engineering decisions align with organizational objectives?

Demonstrate listening, evidence-based reasoning, clear responsibilities, documented decisions, and appropriate escalation.

## 6. Advanced Scenario Practice

### Scenario 1: Conflicting Requirements

Two stakeholders demand incompatible system performance targets.

A strong response should:

1. Clarify the operational need behind each target.
2. Identify which requirements are mandatory.
3. Quantify the conflict where possible.
4. Develop feasible alternatives.
5. Assess system-level consequences.
6. Present trade-offs and risks.
7. Obtain a decision from the authorized stakeholders.
8. Update requirements and traceability records.

Do not silently choose one stakeholder's preference.

### Scenario 2: Late Interface Change

A supplier changes an interface shortly before integration testing.

A strong response should:

1. Establish the exact change and its rationale.
2. Identify affected components, requirements, and tests.
3. Assess compatibility and downstream impact.
4. Determine configuration and approval requirements.
5. Evaluate alternatives, including postponement or rollback.
6. Coordinate affected teams.
7. Authorize the change through the applicable process.
8. Verify the revised configuration.

### Scenario 3: Schedule Versus Safety

A project is behind schedule, and an unresolved hazard remains.

A strong response should explain that the hazard must be evaluated through the established safety and risk process. The authorized decision-makers must determine whether the risk is acceptable and whether further mitigation is required. Schedule pressure alone is not a technical justification for bypassing mandatory controls.

### Scenario 4: Failed Design Assumption

A prototype performs worse than expected because a key assumption was incorrect.

A strong response should distinguish the observed evidence from the original assumption, assess the cause, update the model or requirement where appropriate, evaluate alternatives, document the decision, and incorporate the lesson into future work.

## 7. Discussing Failures and Lessons Learned

Professional reviewers may be interested in how you respond to setbacks.

A constructive explanation should cover:

* What was expected.
* What actually happened.
* Which assumptions or controls proved inadequate.
* Your responsibility and actions.
* How the problem was corrected.
* What changed in the engineering process.
* How recurrence is now prevented or detected.

Do not blame other people without evidence or claim sole responsibility for a team outcome. Explain what you learned and how your practice changed.

## 8. Communicating at a Senior Professional Level

A clear professional answer should:

* Start with the central decision or principle.
* Explain the engineering context.
* Identify relevant constraints and risks.
* Describe alternatives and reasoning.
* State the outcome and evidence.
* Acknowledge uncertainty where appropriate.

Avoid:

* Reciting textbook definitions without application.
* Overusing acronyms.
* Giving excessively long answers that obscure the decision.
* Claiming certainty when the evidence is incomplete.
* Inventing experience to fit a question.
* Confusing a recommendation with an authorized decision.

## 9. Mock Review Questions

Answer these aloud using genuine professional examples.

1. Describe a complex systems engineering decision you influenced.
2. How did you resolve competing stakeholder priorities?
3. Give an example of an interface problem and its resolution.
4. Describe a technical risk that changed your engineering approach.
5. How have you improved a process or developed other engineers?
6. Explain a decision where lifecycle cost changed the preferred solution.
7. Describe an engineering outcome that did not meet expectations.
8. How do you decide when to escalate a technical concern?
9. How do you preserve traceability through major changes?
10. What evidence supports your most significant professional contribution?

For each answer, assess whether it identifies the context, your role, reasoning, outcome, and supporting evidence.

## 10. Self-Assessment Rubric

Score each dimension from 1 to 5.

| Dimension             | 1              | 3                            | 5                                       |
| --------------------- | -------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Context               | Unclear        | Adequate                     | Precise and relevant                    |
| Personal role         | Ambiguous      | Mostly clear                 | Explicit and accurate                   |
| Engineering reasoning | Unsupported    | Reasonable                   | Structured and evidence-based           |
| Systems perspective   | Component-only | Some dependencies considered | Clear lifecycle and system-level impact |
| Risk awareness        | Missing        | Basic                        | Explicit and proportionate              |
| Outcome               | Unclear        | Described                    | Supported by credible evidence          |
| Reflection            | Missing        | General lesson               | Specific improvement in practice        |

Use low scores to identify examples that need better documentation or clearer explanation, not to embellish the underlying experience.

## 11. Final Preparation Checklist

* [ ] Current eligibility criteria have been verified.
* [ ] Every required section is complete.
* [ ] Experience dates and descriptions are consistent.
* [ ] Significant claims have credible support.
* [ ] References are suitable and informed.
* [ ] Confidential information has been protected.
* [ ] Professional contribution examples are ready.
* [ ] Several technical decision examples have been rehearsed.
* [ ] At least one difficult lesson learned can be explained honestly.
* [ ] Application status and next steps are tracked.

## 12. Key Takeaways

* Strong application review combines completeness, consistency, and evidence quality.
* Technical answers should explain reasoning, not merely conclusions.
* Senior-level examples show systems thinking, trade-offs, risk awareness, and influence.
* Difficult experiences can demonstrate professional maturity when described honestly.
* Interview or review procedures should be confirmed through current official guidance.
* Never invent accomplishments to satisfy an expected answer.
