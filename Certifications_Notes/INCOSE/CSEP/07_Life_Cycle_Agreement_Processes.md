# 07 — Life Cycle Agreement Processes

## 1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the acquisition and supply processes.
2. Distinguish the acquirer, supplier, customer, and end user.
3. Describe how an organization defines an acquisition or supply strategy.
4. Explain the purpose of requests for proposals, supplier evaluation, and agreements.
5. Describe how requirements, interfaces, risks, and changes are managed across organizational boundaries.
6. Explain supplier monitoring, verification, validation, acceptance, and delivery.
7. Identify common contractual and technical risks in supplier relationships.
8. Apply agreement-process concepts to a realistic engineering project.

## 2. Overview of Life Cycle Agreement Processes

Systems are rarely developed entirely by one organization. A project may acquire commercial hardware, outsource software development, purchase engineering services, or subcontract the manufacture of components.

Agreement processes establish the relationship between organizations so that required products and services can be obtained and delivered under defined conditions.

In the ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 framework, the principal life cycle agreement processes are commonly identified as:

* **Acquisition:** Used by an organization that obtains a product or service from another organization.
* **Supply:** Used by an organization that provides a product or service to another organization.

These processes are complementary but are performed from different organizational perspectives.

### 2.1 Acquisition versus supply

| Aspect              | Acquisition                                                                                | Supply                                                                          |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary perspective | Acquirer or buyer                                                                          | Supplier or provider                                                            |
| Main concern        | Obtain the required system or service                                                      | Deliver the agreed system or service                                            |
| Typical activities  | Define needs, prepare solicitation, evaluate offers, establish agreement, monitor delivery | Assess request, prepare proposal, plan work, fulfill agreement, deliver product |
| Key risk            | Receiving an unsuitable, late, noncompliant, or excessively costly deliverable             | Accepting an unclear or unachievable commitment                                 |
| Evidence of success | Deliverables meet agreed acceptance criteria                                               | Contractual and technical obligations are fulfilled                             |

Both organizations remain responsible for their own internal management and engineering activities. An agreement does not replace systems engineering.

### 2.2 Why these processes matter

Poorly managed agreements can produce:

* Incomplete or ambiguous requirements.
* Misunderstood responsibilities.
* Incompatible hardware and software.
* Uncontrolled scope growth.
* Disputes over acceptance.
* Schedule and cost overruns.
* Supplier lock-in.
* Intellectual-property and data-rights conflicts.
* Security, safety, or regulatory gaps.
* Late discovery that a deliverable cannot integrate with the overall system.

A sound agreement provides a shared basis for planning, implementation, evaluation, delivery, and acceptance.

## 3. Organizational Roles

### 3.1 Acquirer

The acquirer is the organization that obtains a product or service.

The acquirer typically:

* Defines the need and intended outcomes.
* Identifies constraints and acceptance criteria.
* Determines whether to build, buy, lease, or outsource.
* Evaluates potential suppliers.
* Establishes and manages the agreement.
* Monitors supplier performance.
* Evaluates deliverables.
* Makes acceptance decisions according to the agreement.

The acquirer should not assume that the supplier will infer unstated operational needs.

### 3.2 Supplier

The supplier provides a product or service under an agreement.

The supplier typically:

* Reviews the request and its feasibility.
* Identifies assumptions, dependencies, and exclusions.
* Develops a proposal.
* Negotiates achievable commitments.
* Plans and performs the agreed work.
* Manages its technical solution and internal resources.
* Provides evidence of conformity.
* Reports status, risks, and issues.
* Delivers the agreed outputs and supporting information.

The supplier should not silently reinterpret ambiguous requirements or commit to impossible performance targets.

### 3.3 Other stakeholders

Other relevant parties may include:

* **Customer:** The party that purchases or receives a product or service; depending on the arrangement, the customer may be the acquirer.
* **End user:** The person or organization that uses the delivered system.
* **Subcontractor:** An organization engaged by the supplier to perform part of the work.
* **Regulator:** An authority that defines or enforces applicable obligations.
* **Independent assessor:** A party that evaluates compliance or technical evidence.
* **Integrator:** The organization responsible for combining components into the larger system.

A single organization may occupy several roles. The important task is to identify responsibilities explicitly rather than rely on titles.

## 4. Acquisition Process

Acquisition is more than purchasing an item. It includes defining the need, selecting an appropriate provider, establishing an agreement, and ensuring that the required outcome is delivered.

### 4.1 Establish the acquisition need

The acquirer identifies the operational problem or capability gap.

The need should describe:

* Intended outcomes.
* Required capabilities.
* Operational environment.
* Performance expectations.
* Constraints and assumptions.
* Safety and security considerations.
* Interfaces with existing systems.
* Required delivery dates.
* Life-cycle support expectations.
* Acceptance evidence.

**Example:** A hospital needs a monitoring platform that collects patient-device data, alerts clinical staff, integrates with existing records, and remains available during defined operating periods.

The need is not simply "buy a monitoring application." It includes operational behavior, integration, reliability, privacy, support, and acceptance requirements.

### 4.2 Define the acquisition strategy

The acquisition strategy explains how the organization intends to obtain the capability.

It may address:

* Make-or-buy analysis.
* Commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) versus custom development.
* Single-source versus competitive sourcing.
* Supplier capability and maturity.
* Contract type and commercial model.
* Schedule and budget constraints.
* Technical and organizational risks.
* Intellectual property and data rights.
* Security and supply-chain considerations.
* Verification, validation, and acceptance arrangements.
* Maintenance, upgrades, and end-of-life support.

The best strategy is not necessarily the lowest initial purchase price. The acquirer should consider total life-cycle cost and the risk of failing to meet operational needs.

### 4.3 Prepare the solicitation

A solicitation communicates the required outcome and requests an offer from potential suppliers.

Common forms include:

* **Request for Information (RFI):** Gathers market information and identifies possible approaches.
* **Request for Proposal (RFP):** Requests a proposed solution, delivery approach, schedule, cost, and other relevant commitments.
* **Request for Quotation (RFQ):** Commonly requests pricing and commercial terms for a sufficiently specified product or service.

Organizations use these terms differently, so the governing procurement rules should define their exact meaning.

A useful solicitation contains:

1. Background and business need.
2. Scope of supply.
3. System and service requirements.
4. Applicable standards and regulations.
5. Required interfaces and dependencies.
6. Deliverables and documentation.
7. Schedule and milestones.
8. Verification and validation expectations.
9. Acceptance criteria.
10. Security, safety, and quality obligations.
11. Support, maintenance, and warranty expectations.
12. Proposal format and evaluation criteria.
13. Commercial, legal, and data-rights terms.
14. Assumptions, exclusions, and questions process.

Requirements should be clear enough to support comparable proposals without unnecessarily prescribing every design detail.

### 4.4 Evaluate supplier proposals

The acquirer evaluates proposals against established criteria.

Potential criteria include:

* Technical compliance.
* Demonstrated performance.
* Architecture and integration feasibility.
* Supplier experience and relevant capability.
* Quality-management practices.
* Safety and security approach.
* Schedule credibility.
* Total cost of ownership.
* Maintainability and support.
* Delivery and supply-chain risk.
* Compliance with legal and regulatory obligations.

A weighted evaluation model can help when trade-offs are necessary.

For example:

| Criterion                   | Illustrative weight |
| --------------------------- | ------------------: |
| Technical compliance        |                 30% |
| Life-cycle cost             |                 20% |
| Delivery schedule           |                 15% |
| Supplier capability         |                 15% |
| Safety and security         |                 10% |
| Support and maintainability |                 10% |

These weights are illustrative, not prescribed by INCOSE. Mandatory safety, legal, or security requirements should normally be treated as pass/fail gates rather than requirements that can be offset by a high score elsewhere.

### 4.5 Select the supplier

Supplier selection should be traceable to the evaluation criteria and evidence.

The acquirer should document:

* Which proposals were evaluated.
* How mandatory requirements were assessed.
* How competing proposals were compared.
* Which assumptions remain unresolved.
* What risks were accepted.
* Why the selected supplier offers the best overall fit.

The cheapest proposal is not automatically the best choice. A low-cost proposal may omit integration, testing, training, support, or required documentation.

### 4.6 Establish the agreement

An agreement establishes the obligations and expectations of the parties.

Depending on the organization and jurisdiction, it may take the form of a contract, purchase order, statement of work, service-level agreement, or another recognized instrument.

Agreement content may include:

* Scope and deliverables.
* Technical and interface requirements.
* Schedule and milestones.
* Cost, payment, and invoicing arrangements.
* Roles and responsibilities.
* Verification and acceptance criteria.
* Reporting and review arrangements.
* Change-control procedures.
* Risk and issue escalation.
* Quality, safety, and security obligations.
* Intellectual-property and data rights.
* Confidentiality and privacy.
* Warranty and support.
* Subcontracting restrictions or approval requirements.
* Remedies, dispute resolution, and termination.
* Delivery format and required technical records.

Commercial and legal specialists should support the agreement where necessary. Systems engineers help ensure that technical obligations are complete, measurable, and feasible.

## 5. Supply Process

The supply process describes the supplier's activities in responding to and fulfilling an agreement.

### 5.1 Review the request

Before committing, the supplier should determine:

* What is being requested?
* Are the requirements understood?
* Are the requirements technically feasible?
* Are there contradictory or missing requirements?
* Are the interfaces sufficiently defined?
* Are the proposed schedule and resources realistic?
* Are there dependencies on the acquirer?
* Are there legal, safety, security, or regulatory obligations?
* Are assumptions and exclusions explicit?

A supplier should raise material ambiguities before accepting obligations.

### 5.2 Prepare a proposal

A proposal may describe:

* The proposed solution.
* Technical compliance and deviations.
* Architecture and implementation approach.
* Development or delivery plan.
* Schedule and milestones.
* Cost and commercial assumptions.
* Personnel and resource requirements.
* Verification and validation activities.
* Quality and risk-management approach.
* Required inputs from the acquirer.
* Dependencies, exclusions, and limitations.
* Support and maintenance arrangements.

A strong proposal makes its assumptions visible. Hidden assumptions often become disputes after work begins.

### 5.3 Plan and execute the work

After an agreement is established, the supplier plans and performs the required activities.

Depending on scope, the supplier may need to:

* Establish a project plan.
* Allocate technical and managerial responsibilities.
* Derive internal requirements from the agreed requirements.
* Develop or configure the product.
* Manage interfaces and configuration baselines.
* Conduct reviews, analyses, and tests.
* Manage risks and technical issues.
* Control subcontractors.
* Produce required documentation.
* Report progress and deviations.
* Prepare delivery and acceptance evidence.

The supplier remains responsible for managing its work even when the acquirer monitors progress.

### 5.4 Verify and validate deliverables

**Verification** asks whether the deliverable satisfies specified requirements.

**Validation** asks whether the resulting system or service meets its intended use and stakeholder needs in the relevant context.

For example, a monitoring system might pass all software unit tests (verification evidence), but clinicians may discover during realistic workflow evaluation that the alert interface is confusing (a validation concern).

The precise allocation of verification and validation responsibilities should be defined in the agreement.

### 5.5 Deliver the agreed outputs

Delivery may include more than the physical product or executable software.

Deliverables can include:

* Hardware or software.
* Source code and build artifacts.
* Configuration data.
* Interface specifications.
* Test results and compliance evidence.
* Installation and deployment procedures.
* User and administrator documentation.
* Training materials.
* Maintenance procedures.
* Spare parts and support information.
* Security documentation.
* Known limitations and unresolved issues.

A deliverable should be complete against the agreed scope, not merely ready to demonstrate.

## 6. Supplier Monitoring and Acquirer Oversight

Monitoring gives the acquirer visibility into whether the supplier is likely to fulfill the agreement.

Useful mechanisms include:

* Progress reports.
* Technical reviews.
* Milestone demonstrations.
* Requirements traceability reviews.
* Test and inspection results.
* Risk and issue registers.
* Schedule and cost performance indicators.
* Quality and defect trends.
* Configuration audits.
* Security and compliance assessments.
* Supplier-site assessments when justified.

Monitoring should be proportionate to risk. A low-risk commercial component may need limited oversight; a safety-critical control system may require formal evidence, independent reviews, and rigorous acceptance controls.

### 6.1 Leading and lagging indicators

**Leading indicators** help identify potential problems before they cause failure:

* Increasing unresolved requirements.
* Growing integration backlog.
* Missed intermediate reviews.
* Rising high-severity defect counts.
* Delayed delivery of test evidence.
* Unresolved interface decisions.

**Lagging indicators** describe outcomes that have already occurred:

* Missed delivery milestones.
* Actual cost overruns.
* Failed acceptance tests.
* Production defects.
* Contractual disputes.

Using both kinds of indicators provides better visibility than relying on schedule status alone.

### 6.2 Avoiding ineffective oversight

The acquirer should avoid:

* Accepting optimistic status reports without evidence.
* Reviewing progress only at final delivery.
* Confusing activity completion with requirement satisfaction.
* Directing supplier staff informally in ways that bypass agreed responsibilities.
* Making technical changes without assessing contractual consequences.
* Treating a demonstration as proof of full acceptance.

Oversight should be evidence-based and consistent with the agreement.

## 7. Acceptance and Closure

Acceptance is the formal determination that a deliverable satisfies the agreed conditions for acceptance.

Acceptance criteria should be established before delivery whenever possible.

They may include:

* Required functions demonstrated.
* Performance thresholds achieved.
* Interfaces successfully tested.
* Mandatory tests passed.
* Required documentation delivered.
* Safety and security obligations satisfied.
* Defects classified and handled according to agreed rules.
* Training and support obligations fulfilled.
* Required configuration and version information supplied.

### 7.1 Acceptance is not the same as verification

Verification generates evidence about compliance with requirements. Acceptance is a decision made by an authorized party using the agreed criteria and available evidence.

A deliverable can have successful test results but still be rejected if required documentation or other contractual obligations are missing.

Likewise, acceptance should not be granted merely because the delivery date has arrived.

### 7.2 Conditional acceptance

Some agreements permit conditional or provisional acceptance.

If used, the agreement should define:

* Which deficiencies may be deferred.
* Who approves the deferral.
* Required corrective actions.
* Completion dates.
* Residual risk ownership.
* Payment or warranty consequences.

Safety-critical or legally mandatory requirements should not be waived through an informal process.

### 7.3 Closure

Closure may involve:

* Confirming completion of contractual obligations.
* Resolving or transferring outstanding issues.
* Finalizing payment and records.
* Handing over operational responsibility.
* Confirming warranty and support start dates.
* Preserving configuration and acceptance evidence.
* Recording lessons learned.
* Managing remaining supplier dependencies.

The exact closure process depends on the agreement and organizational policy.

## 8. Requirements and Change Control Across Organizations

Requirements often cross organizational boundaries. The acquirer may specify a system-level outcome while the supplier designs a component that contributes to that outcome.

Traceability should connect:

**Stakeholder need → system requirement → supplier requirement → design or implementation → verification evidence → acceptance decision**

This chain helps identify gaps and determine the impact of proposed changes.

### 8.1 Change requests

A proposed change may affect:

* Scope.
* Cost.
* Schedule.
* Performance.
* Safety and security.
* Interfaces.
* Verification and validation.
* Compatibility.
* Training and documentation.
* Support obligations.
* Contractual terms.

The parties should use the agreed change-control process to assess and authorize changes.

**Key rule:** Do not assume that a technical change is automatically authorized simply because it appears beneficial.

For example, a supplier may recommend replacing a database engine with a faster alternative. Before implementation, the parties may need to assess licensing, migration, security, compatibility, performance, and acceptance-test impacts.

### 8.2 Interface agreements

When different suppliers provide connected components, interface agreements should specify:

* Data formats and protocols.
* Electrical or mechanical boundaries.
* Timing and performance constraints.
* Error handling.
* Security boundaries.
* Ownership of interface decisions.
* Integration responsibilities.
* Test methods and test environments.
* Version compatibility.

Interface ambiguity is especially dangerous when each supplier believes the other owns the integration problem.

## 9. Subcontractors and Supply-Chain Risks

A supplier may rely on subcontractors or third-party components. The acquirer should understand the resulting dependencies.

Potential risks include:

* A critical subcontractor fails.
* A component becomes unavailable.
* A third-party library contains a vulnerability.
* A supplier changes a component without notice.
* Technical information cannot be obtained.
* Intellectual-property restrictions prevent maintenance.
* A foreign or geographically concentrated supply chain creates disruption.
* A subcontractor's schedule delays system integration.

Risk controls may include:

* Supplier disclosure of critical dependencies.
* Approval or notification requirements for subcontracting.
* Quality and security flow-down requirements.
* Alternative-source planning.
* Component provenance records.
* Software bill of materials where appropriate.
* Continuity and recovery plans.
* Escrow or source-code access arrangements where justified.
* Clear rights to technical data and maintenance information.

Flow-down requirements should be consistent with the prime agreement and applicable law. The prime supplier normally remains accountable to the acquirer for the obligations it has accepted, even when some work is subcontracted.

## 10. Case Study: Industrial Monitoring System

### 10.1 Scenario

A manufacturing company wants to modernize its industrial monitoring system. It selects three suppliers:

* Supplier A provides industrial sensors.
* Supplier B provides gateway software.
* Supplier C provides the central monitoring platform.

The system must detect abnormal equipment conditions, send alerts, and integrate with the plant's existing network.

The initial project plan contains product specifications but does not clearly assign responsibility for end-to-end integration.

### 10.2 Problems discovered

During integration:

1. The sensors publish data using an undocumented field format.
2. The gateway supplier assumes that the central platform will convert units.
3. The platform supplier assumes that the gateway provides standardized units.
4. The combined system misses the required alert latency.
5. Each supplier claims that its own component meets its individual requirements.
6. No common acceptance test exists.

The individual products may pass their component tests while the overall system fails its intended operational requirement.

### 10.3 Root causes

* Interface responsibilities were unclear.
* System-level requirements were not flowed down consistently.
* No shared end-to-end performance criterion existed.
* Supplier monitoring focused on individual deliverables.
* Integration evidence and acceptance responsibilities were poorly defined.

### 10.4 Corrective actions

The acquirer should:

1. Establish a controlled interface specification.
2. Assign responsibility for unit conversion and data validation.
3. Define end-to-end latency and reliability requirements.
4. Create an integration and acceptance test plan.
5. Update the agreements through authorized change control.
6. Require each supplier to provide relevant verification evidence.
7. Test the complete system in a representative environment.
8. Document unresolved risks and corrective-action owners.

### 10.5 Lessons learned

* Component compliance does not prove system-level success.
* Interface requirements must have explicit owners.
* Acceptance criteria should be measurable and agreed early.
* Suppliers need a common understanding of dependencies.
* Contractual clarity and technical systems engineering must work together.

## 11. Practical Exercises with Suggested Answers

### Exercise 1: Identify the parties

A university hires a vendor to build a laboratory information system. The university defines the operational needs, while the vendor develops the application.

**Question:** Identify the acquirer and supplier.

**Suggested answer:** The university is the acquirer; the vendor is the supplier. Laboratory personnel are end users and may also be key stakeholders. The roles of any hosting provider or subcontractor should be defined separately.

### Exercise 2: Improve a vague requirement

The solicitation states: "The application must be fast."

**Question:** What should the acquirer do?

**Suggested answer:** Establish measurable performance requirements under defined operating conditions, such as response-time percentiles, concurrent-user load, dataset size, and availability expectations. The exact thresholds should be derived from stakeholder needs rather than invented without evidence.

### Exercise 3: Evaluate a low-cost proposal

Supplier A is 20% cheaper than Supplier B but excludes integration testing and long-term support.

**Question:** Is Supplier A necessarily the better option?

**Suggested answer:** No. Compare total life-cycle cost, integration risk, support needs, contractual exclusions, and mandatory requirements. If integration and support are necessary, obtain a comparable revised proposal or account for those costs and risks explicitly.

### Exercise 4: Handle a supplier change

A supplier wants to replace an agreed communication protocol with a proprietary protocol.

**Question:** What should happen before the change is implemented?

**Suggested answer:** Submit a formal change request, analyze technical and contractual impacts, evaluate interoperability and lock-in, update interface and verification artifacts as needed, and obtain authorization from the designated decision authority.

### Exercise 5: Distinguish verification and acceptance

The supplier's test report shows that all software functions pass, but the installation manual is missing.

**Question:** Must the acquirer accept the delivery?

**Suggested answer:** Not necessarily. If the manual is an agreed deliverable or acceptance condition, the delivery may be incomplete despite successful functional tests.

### Exercise 6: Identify an oversight indicator

A supplier reports that development is 80% complete but has not delivered its interface specification.

**Question:** Why is this a concern?

**Suggested answer:** The missing specification is a leading indicator of possible integration delay. The acquirer should assess its criticality, request a committed delivery date, and examine the downstream integration schedule.

### Exercise 7: Manage a subcontractor risk

A critical component is available from only one subcontractor.

**Question:** What should the acquirer and prime supplier consider?

**Suggested answer:** Assess supply continuity, component availability, substitution feasibility, technical-data access, lead times, and recovery options. Define ownership and mitigation actions and monitor the dependency throughout the project.

### Exercise 8: Resolve interface conflict

Two suppliers each claim that the other is responsible for data conversion.

**Question:** What is the most effective corrective action?

**Suggested answer:** Establish an explicit interface agreement that defines the data representation, conversion responsibility, error handling, and verification evidence. Resolve the responsibility gap through the authorized agreement and change-control process.

### Exercise 9: Define an acceptance test

A monitoring platform must alert operators when a machine exceeds a temperature threshold.

**Question:** What should an acceptance test specify?

**Suggested answer:** Specify the threshold, sensor input conditions, expected alert behavior, maximum allowed latency, logging requirements, failure behavior, test environment, evidence to collect, and pass/fail criteria.

### Exercise 10: Choose monitoring rigor

One agreement covers standard office keyboards; another covers a safety-critical industrial controller.

**Question:** Should both use identical oversight?

**Suggested answer:** No. Oversight should be proportionate to the consequence of failure, technical complexity, uncertainty, and applicable obligations. The industrial controller warrants more rigorous verification, traceability, configuration control, and safety evidence.

## 12. Original CSEP-Style Practice Questions

**These questions are original study questions, not official INCOSE examination items.**

### Question 1

What is the primary purpose of the acquisition process?

A. To design every supplier component internally.
B. To obtain a required product or service through a managed process.
C. To eliminate all supplier risk.
D. To replace the need for requirements engineering.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Acquisition establishes how an organization obtains a needed product or service. It does not eliminate risk or replace engineering.

### Question 2

Which activity is most appropriate before a supplier commits to an agreement?

A. Ignore unresolved requirements until delivery.
B. Review feasibility, assumptions, dependencies, and obligations.
C. Begin acceptance testing without a baseline.
D. Transfer all technical responsibility to the acquirer.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** A supplier should understand the requested work and raise material ambiguities before making commitments.

### Question 3

Which is the strongest basis for comparing proposals?

A. Supplier reputation alone.
B. Lowest initial cost alone.
C. Predetermined criteria aligned with requirements and project risks.
D. The number of pages in each proposal.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Consistent, relevant criteria make comparisons traceable and support defensible decisions.

### Question 4

A product passes its component tests but fails to communicate with another component. What is the most likely missing consideration?

A. Interface definition and integration verification.
B. Supplier advertising.
C. Employee onboarding unrelated to the product.
D. A reduction in documentation.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Component verification does not establish that interfaces and the integrated system perform correctly.

### Question 5

Which statement best describes acceptance?

A. It is always identical to a supplier demonstration.
B. It is the formal determination that agreed acceptance conditions have been met.
C. It can occur only after the warranty expires.
D. It removes the need to preserve test evidence.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Acceptance is an authorized decision against agreed conditions and evidence.

### Question 6

A supplier proposes a change that improves performance but may increase maintenance cost. What should occur first?

A. Implement the change immediately.
B. Assess impacts and follow the agreed change-control process.
C. Delete the original requirement.
D. Let the developer decide without informing the acquirer.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** A change may affect cost, compatibility, support, schedule, and contractual obligations; it needs controlled assessment and authorization.

### Question 7

Why should acceptance criteria be defined early?

A. To prevent the supplier from performing verification.
B. To reduce ambiguity about what constitutes an acceptable deliverable.
C. To guarantee that no defects will occur.
D. To remove all stakeholder involvement.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Early agreement on measurable criteria reduces disputes and helps both parties plan the necessary evidence.

### Question 8

Which is a leading indicator of potential integration failure?

A. A final acceptance test has failed.
B. A contractual penalty has been applied.
C. Required interface definitions remain unresolved near the integration milestone.
D. The project has already closed successfully.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** An unresolved interface is an early warning of downstream integration problems.

### Question 9

A supplier outsources a critical subsystem. What is an important management consideration?

A. The subcontractor automatically assumes every obligation to the acquirer.
B. The prime supplier's responsibilities disappear.
C. Dependencies, flow-down obligations, and accountability must be managed.
D. Subcontracted work never requires verification.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Subcontracting creates dependencies and may require explicit technical, quality, security, and contractual controls.

### Question 10

Which statement best distinguishes verification from acceptance?

A. Verification produces evidence of conformity; acceptance is a decision based on agreed criteria.
B. Verification is performed only by the acquirer; acceptance only by the supplier.
C. They always mean exactly the same thing.
D. Acceptance is unnecessary when tests are automated.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Verification assesses requirements conformity. Acceptance is a formal decision governed by the agreement and applicable authority.

## 13. Revision Notes

Remember these key distinctions:

* **Acquisition:** Obtain a needed product or service.
* **Supply:** Provide the agreed product or service.
* **Solicitation:** Request information, proposals, or quotations from prospective providers.
* **Agreement:** Establish the parties' obligations and expectations.
* **Verification:** Determine whether specified requirements are satisfied.
* **Validation:** Determine whether the system meets intended use and stakeholder needs.
* **Acceptance:** Formally determine whether agreed acceptance conditions have been met.
* **Change control:** Assess and authorize changes to controlled technical or contractual commitments.
* **Supplier monitoring:** Use evidence to assess performance, progress, and emerging risk.

A reliable agreement connects operational needs to measurable requirements, clear responsibilities, credible plans, appropriate oversight, and objective acceptance evidence.
