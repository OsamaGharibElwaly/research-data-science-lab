* Understand the IIBA and the Business Analysis Body of Knowledge (BABOK Guide).

## 2. What Is Business Analysis?

Business analysis is the practice of enabling change in an enterprise by defining needs and recommending solutions that deliver value to stakeholders.

Business analysis focuses on understanding:

* The current business situation.
* The problems or opportunities that justify change.
* The needs of stakeholders.
* The desired future state.
* Potential solutions.
* The value expected from implementing those solutions.

Business analysis is not limited to software development. It can be applied to business process improvement, organizational restructuring, digital transformation, operational optimization, product development, and many other areas.

### Example

A retail company experiences frequent stockouts despite having a large inventory.

A Business Analyst investigates sales patterns, purchasing procedures, supplier lead times, and inventory records. The analyst identifies the underlying causes and recommends improvements to inventory planning.

The solution might involve new software, redesigned processes, improved supplier coordination, or a combination of these changes.

The central objective is to solve the business problem rather than simply introduce a new technology.

## 3. The Purpose of Business Analysis

Business analysis helps organizations make informed decisions about change.

Its primary purposes include:

1. Identifying business needs.
2. Understanding stakeholder concerns and expectations.
3. Defining requirements.
4. Evaluating possible solutions.
5. Supporting decisions about priorities and scope.
6. Reducing uncertainty and unnecessary costs.
7. Evaluating whether a solution delivers its intended value.

A successful business analysis effort connects business needs to measurable outcomes.

### Business Need vs. Solution

A business need explains why change is necessary.

A solution describes how that need might be addressed.

Example:

* Business need: Reduce customer waiting time.
* Possible solution: Introduce an online appointment system.
* Desired outcome: Reduce average waiting time from 40 minutes to 15 minutes.

The online appointment system is only one possible solution. The analyst should investigate the underlying need before recommending it.

## 4. The Business Analyst Role

A Business Analyst acts as a bridge between business stakeholders and the people responsible for designing, delivering, or operating a solution.

Typical responsibilities include:

* Investigating business problems and opportunities.
* Identifying stakeholders.
* Conducting interviews and workshops.
* Analyzing business processes.
* Eliciting, documenting, and validating requirements.
* Evaluating alternatives.
* Prioritizing requirements.
* Communicating findings to stakeholders.
* Supporting solution evaluation.

The exact responsibilities vary according to the organization, industry, project, and delivery approach.

### Important Distinction

A Business Analyst does not necessarily make every business decision. The analyst provides evidence, analysis, recommendations, and facilitation so that authorized stakeholders can make informed decisions.

## 5. Business Analysis and Related Disciplines

### Business Analysis vs. Project Management

Business analysis focuses primarily on needs, requirements, solutions, and value.

Project management focuses primarily on organizing and directing work to achieve project objectives within agreed constraints.

A Business Analyst might determine what a new customer portal must accomplish. A Project Manager might coordinate the schedule, budget, resources, risks, and delivery activities.

The roles can overlap, and one person may perform both roles.

### Business Analysis vs. Systems Analysis

Systems analysis focuses on understanding and specifying system behavior, components, interfaces, and technical requirements.

Business analysis has a broader scope and can include organizational, operational, strategic, and technological changes.

### Business Analysis vs. Data Analysis

Data analysis examines data to discover patterns, answer questions, and support decisions.

Business analysis identifies business needs and recommends changes. Data analysis can provide important evidence within a business analysis engagement.

Example: A data analyst discovers that customer cancellations increase after delayed deliveries. A Business Analyst investigates the operational causes and recommends changes to the delivery process.

### Business Analysis vs. Software Development

Software developers implement and maintain software solutions.

Business Analysts help determine the business needs and requirements that the software should satisfy.

Collaboration between these disciplines improves alignment between business expectations and technical implementation.

## 6. Business Analysis Across the Change Lifecycle

Business analysis can take place before, during, and after a project.

### Before Implementation

* Identify the business problem.
* Understand the current state.
* Define desired outcomes.
* Explore possible approaches.
* Assess feasibility and expected value.

### During Implementation

* Clarify requirements.
* Support prioritization.
* Resolve requirement ambiguities.
* Assess proposed changes.
* Validate that the developing solution aligns with stakeholder needs.

### After Implementation

* Evaluate solution performance.
* Measure business outcomes.
* Identify gaps between expected and actual value.
* Recommend improvements.

Business analysis is not necessarily a linear process. Activities may repeat as new information becomes available.

## 7. Common Business Analysis Deliverables

A deliverable is an output produced through an activity or set of activities.

Examples include:

| Deliverable                      | Purpose                                                            |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Business needs statement         | Explains the reason for change                                     |
| Stakeholder register             | Identifies relevant stakeholders                                   |
| Business process model           | Represents activities and workflows                                |
| Requirements document            | Records requirements in an agreed format                           |
| User stories                     | Describe needs from a user's perspective                           |
| Use case                         | Describes interactions with a system                               |
| Requirements traceability matrix | Links requirements to related artifacts and outcomes               |
| Solution evaluation report       | Assesses solution performance                                      |
| Business case                    | Supports a decision by comparing costs, benefits, and alternatives |

Not every initiative requires every deliverable. The appropriate documentation depends on the context and agreed approach.

## 8. The IIBA and BABOK Guide

The International Institute of Business Analysis (IIBA) is a professional association supporting the business analysis profession.

The Business Analysis Body of Knowledge (BABOK Guide) describes generally recognized business analysis knowledge, tasks, techniques, and competencies.

The BABOK Guide organizes business analysis knowledge into six knowledge areas:

1. Business Analysis Planning and Monitoring.
2. Elicitation and Collaboration.
3. Requirements Life Cycle Management.
4. Strategy Analysis.
5. Requirements Analysis and Design Definition.
6. Solution Evaluation.

These knowledge areas are related and may be performed iteratively rather than in a fixed sequence.

The Entry Certificate in Business Analysis (ECBA) is an entry-level IIBA certification intended to demonstrate foundational business analysis knowledge.

**Exam preparation note:** Always compare study notes with the current official ECBA exam blueprint and IIBA guidance. Exam structures and eligibility requirements can change.

## 9. Essential Terminology

**Business need:** A problem or opportunity that motivates change.

**Stakeholder:** An individual, group, or organization that has a relationship with the change, needs, or solution.

**Requirement:** A usable representation of a need, capability, condition, or characteristic that a solution must satisfy.

**Solution:** A specific way of satisfying one or more needs in a particular context.

**Value:** The worth, importance, or usefulness of something to a stakeholder in a context.

**Change:** The transformation of an enterprise in response to a need.

**Enterprise:** An organizational system or collection of organizations pursuing shared goals.

**Context:** The circumstances influencing a change, including organizational structure, regulations, processes, technology, and culture.

## 10. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: University Registration Delays

A university receives complaints because students wait several hours to register for courses.

The university administration proposes purchasing a new registration platform.

A Business Analyst begins by investigating the problem.

Initial findings:

* Several departments use separate registration spreadsheets.
* Course capacity information is updated inconsistently.
* Students must visit multiple offices to resolve registration conflicts.
* Some approvals require manual signatures.
* The registration platform is outdated.

### Analysis

The stated solution is a new platform, but the underlying need is more reliable and efficient course registration.

Possible options include:

1. Standardizing registration procedures.
2. Centralizing course capacity data.
3. Automating approvals.
4. Improving the existing system.
5. Implementing a new integrated platform.

The analyst compares the options against business needs, stakeholder expectations, feasibility, cost, risk, and expected value.

### Success Measures

* Reduce average registration time from 120 minutes to 30 minutes.
* Reduce registration errors by 70%.
* Reduce unresolved registration conflicts by 50%.

These measures help determine whether the selected solution delivers the intended business value.

## 2. Introduction to the BACCM

The Business Analysis Core Concept Model (BACCM) is a conceptual framework defined by IIBA to explain the fundamental concepts that underpin business analysis.

It contains six core concepts:

1. Change
2. Need
3. Solution
4. Stakeholder
5. Value
6. Context

These concepts are interconnected. Understanding one often requires considering the others.

For example, an organization may need to change its customer service process because customer complaints are increasing. Stakeholders have different expectations, and the organization must select a solution that creates value within its operational and regulatory context.

## 3. The Six Core Concepts

### 3.1 Change

Change is the transformation of an enterprise in response to a need.

Change may involve:

* Introducing a new system.
* Improving a business process.
* Restructuring an organization.
* Changing policies or procedures.
* Launching a new product.
* Automating repetitive tasks.

Change can be planned or emerge in response to unexpected circumstances.

**Example:** A bank changes its loan approval process to reduce processing time and improve customer experience.

The Business Analyst helps understand the change, its objectives, the stakeholders it affects, and the outcomes it should produce.

### 3.2 Need

A need is a problem or opportunity that motivates a change.

Needs may arise from:

* Customer dissatisfaction.
* Operational inefficiency.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Market opportunities.
* Competitive pressure.
* New technologies.
* Financial performance issues.

A need is not necessarily the same as a stakeholder's proposed solution.

**Example:**

A department manager says, "We need a new CRM."

The analyst investigates and discovers that employees cannot access accurate customer information across departments.

The underlying need may be to improve customer information accessibility and consistency. A new CRM is only one possible solution.

### 3.3 Solution

A solution is a specific way of satisfying one or more needs in a particular context.

Solutions may include:

* Software applications.
* Manual procedures.
* Business process redesign.
* Organizational changes.
* Training programs.
* Outsourcing arrangements.
* Combinations of people, processes, information, and technology.

**Example:** To reduce invoice-processing errors, an organization might implement automated validation, standardize invoice templates, train employees, or combine these approaches.

The analyst should evaluate alternatives rather than assume that the most technologically advanced solution is automatically the best.

### 3.4 Stakeholder

A stakeholder is an individual, group, or organization that has a relationship with the change, need, or solution.

Common stakeholders include:

* Customers.
* End users.
* Business sponsors.
* Managers.
* Employees.
* Project team members.
* Suppliers.
* Regulators.
* Operations teams.
* Executives.
* Support personnel.

Stakeholders may have conflicting needs.

For example, customers may want faster approval, while a compliance team requires additional verification.

Business analysis must identify and understand these perspectives.

### 3.5 Value

Value is the worth, importance, or usefulness of something to a stakeholder in a context.

Value is not limited to financial benefits. It may include:

* Higher revenue.
* Lower costs.
* Reduced risk.
* Improved customer satisfaction.
* Better accessibility.
* Faster service.
* Improved compliance.
* Higher employee productivity.
* Better decision-making.

Value is contextual: the same solution can create value for one stakeholder while introducing costs or risks for another.

**Example:** Automation reduces processing time but increases the need for employee training. The organization should evaluate the overall benefits and trade-offs.

### 3.6 Context

Context consists of the circumstances influencing, or being influenced by, a change.

Context may include:

* Organizational culture.
* Business strategy.
* Existing processes.
* Technology infrastructure.
* Laws and regulations.
* Market conditions.
* Available budgets.
* Skills and staffing.
* Geographic considerations.
* Time constraints.

**Example:** A cloud-based system may be suitable for one company but inappropriate for another because of data residency requirements or infrastructure limitations.

The analyst must evaluate solutions in the actual context in which they will operate.

## 4. Understanding the Relationships Between Core Concepts

The six concepts should not be memorized as isolated definitions.

Consider this sequence:

1. A need creates a reason for change.
2. Stakeholders experience or influence the need and change.
3. A solution is developed or selected to address the need.
4. The solution is evaluated in its context.
5. Value is assessed based on stakeholder outcomes.
6. Evaluation may reveal additional needs and lead to further change.

This is a conceptual relationship, not a mandatory project sequence.

### Example: Online Appointment System

A medical clinic experiences overcrowding.

* **Need:** Reduce waiting time and improve appointment management.
* **Change:** Transform the appointment booking process.
* **Stakeholders:** Patients, receptionists, doctors, administrators, and IT support.
* **Solution:** Online booking, automated reminders, and improved scheduling rules.
* **Value:** Reduced waiting time, fewer missed appointments, and better staff utilization.
* **Context:** Privacy requirements, staff skills, patient accessibility, budget, and existing systems.

A solution that works technically but excludes patients who cannot use digital services may fail to deliver the expected overall value.

## 5. Requirements and Designs

Requirements and designs are related but distinct concepts.

### Requirements

A requirement represents a need, capability, condition, or characteristic that must be satisfied.

Requirements can describe:

* Business objectives.
* Stakeholder needs.
* Solution behavior.
* Quality expectations.
* Constraints.

### Designs

A design represents a possible solution approach or describes how a solution could satisfy requirements.

For example:

**Requirement:** Customers must be able to reset their passwords without contacting support.

**Design:** Provide a password-reset page that sends a time-limited verification link to the registered email address.

The requirement describes the expected capability. The design describes one implementation approach.

A single requirement may be satisfied by several different designs.

## 6. The Three Common Categories of Requirements

A useful classification distinguishes business, stakeholder, and solution requirements.

### Business Requirements

Describe the goals, objectives, and outcomes that explain why a change is needed.

Example: Reduce customer onboarding time by 40%.

### Stakeholder Requirements

Describe the needs of a stakeholder or stakeholder group that must be satisfied to achieve the business requirements.

Example: Customer service representatives need a consolidated view of each customer's onboarding status.

### Solution Requirements

Describe the capabilities and characteristics of a solution that will meet stakeholder requirements.

Solution requirements are commonly divided into:

* **Functional requirements:** What the solution must do.
* **Non-functional requirements:** How well the solution must perform or what qualities and constraints it must satisfy.

Example functional requirement: The system shall allow users to upload identification documents.

Example non-functional requirement: The system shall display the onboarding dashboard within two seconds under the agreed operating conditions.

### Transition Requirements

Transition requirements describe temporary capabilities needed to move from the current state to the future state.

Examples include:

* Migrating customer records.
* Training employees.
* Converting legacy data.
* Running old and new systems in parallel.
* Preparing operational support teams.

Transition requirements are necessary during implementation but may no longer be relevant once the transition is complete.

## 7. The Business Analysis Context

The same business problem may require different solutions in different environments.

Consider two organizations that want to automate invoice processing.

Organization A has:

* A modern cloud infrastructure.
* Structured digital invoices.
* An experienced IT team.
* A flexible budget.

Organization B has:

* Legacy software.
* Mostly paper invoices.
* Limited technical support.
* Strict infrastructure restrictions.

The best solution may differ because the context differs.

A Business Analyst should consider organizational capabilities, constraints, risks, stakeholder expectations, and external factors before recommending an approach.

## 8. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: Warehouse Order Errors

An e-commerce warehouse has experienced an increase in incorrect customer shipments.

Management proposes buying barcode scanners.

The analyst investigates and discovers:

* Product labels are sometimes incorrect.
* Employees use inconsistent picking procedures.
* Inventory records are updated late.
* Temporary employees receive limited training.
* Supervisors cannot easily identify error patterns.

### BACCM Analysis

**Need:** Reduce incorrect shipments and associated costs.

**Change:** Improve warehouse picking, verification, and inventory processes.

**Stakeholders:** Warehouse employees, supervisors, customers, inventory managers, IT staff, and finance teams.

**Possible solutions:** Barcode scanning, standardized procedures, improved labeling, training, and real-time inventory updates.

**Value:** Fewer returns, lower shipping costs, improved customer satisfaction, and more reliable inventory.

**Context:** Warehouse layout, equipment costs, staff turnover, system compatibility, and order volume.

### Conclusion

Barcode scanners may help, but they will not necessarily solve incorrect labeling or poor training. The analyst should assess the full business problem before recommending a solution.

## 1. Module Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain the purpose of Business Analysis Planning and Monitoring.
* Understand how a Business Analyst determines the appropriate approach to business analysis.
* Identify stakeholders and plan stakeholder engagement.
* Understand business analysis governance.
* Explain information management planning.
* Identify how business analysis performance is assessed.
* Recognize how plans are monitored and adjusted throughout an initiative.

## 2. Introduction

Business Analysis Planning and Monitoring determines how business analysis work will be performed, managed, coordinated, and evaluated.

Planning helps ensure that analysis activities are appropriate for the initiative, stakeholders, organizational environment, and expected outcomes.

Monitoring helps determine whether the planned activities are effective and whether adjustments are necessary.

Planning is not simply producing a schedule. It involves making decisions about:

* What analysis work is required.
* Who should participate.
* Which techniques will be used.
* How requirements and information will be managed.
* How decisions and approvals will be governed.
* How performance will be evaluated.

Plans should be adapted as circumstances change.

## 3. The Planning and Monitoring Knowledge Area

The BABOK Guide identifies five tasks within Business Analysis Planning and Monitoring:

1. Plan Business Analysis Approach.
2. Plan Stakeholder Engagement.
3. Plan Business Analysis Governance.
4. Plan Business Analysis Information Management.
5. Identify Business Analysis Performance Improvements.

Each task addresses a different aspect of organizing and improving business analysis work.

## 4. Plan Business Analysis Approach

### 4.1 Purpose

This task determines the appropriate approach for performing business analysis.

The approach establishes how the analyst will work, which activities will be performed, and how those activities will be coordinated.

Possible approaches include predictive, adaptive, and hybrid approaches.

### 4.2 Predictive Approach

A predictive approach generally emphasizes planning and defining requirements earlier, with formal change management and sequential delivery stages.

Typical characteristics:

* Greater emphasis on upfront planning.
* More detailed documentation where appropriate.
* Formal reviews and approvals.
* Defined delivery stages.
* Changes assessed against agreed baselines.

Predictive approaches may be suitable when requirements are relatively stable, regulatory documentation is important, or changes are costly.

### 4.3 Adaptive Approach

An adaptive approach emphasizes iterative learning, frequent feedback, and evolving requirements.

Typical characteristics:

* Incremental delivery.
* Frequent stakeholder collaboration.
* Regular reprioritization.
* Short feedback cycles.
* Refinement of requirements as knowledge improves.

Adaptive approaches may be suitable when needs are uncertain or the solution must evolve through experimentation.

### 4.4 Hybrid Approach

A hybrid approach combines elements of predictive and adaptive approaches.

For example, an organization may establish a formal budget and compliance framework while delivering software functionality through short iterations.

The correct approach depends on the initiative's risks, complexity, stakeholder availability, uncertainty, organizational standards, and constraints.

### 4.5 Factors Influencing the Approach

The Business Analyst should consider:

* Business objectives.
* Project size and complexity.
* Requirement uncertainty.
* Regulatory constraints.
* Stakeholder availability.
* Delivery deadlines.
* Organizational culture.
* Available tools and resources.
* Risks associated with change.
* Experience with previous initiatives.

### Example

A hospital implementing a regulated patient-record system may require formal approvals, traceability, and extensive documentation.

A small team experimenting with a new customer-facing feature may benefit from iterative development and rapid feedback.

Neither approach is universally superior.

## 5. Plan Stakeholder Engagement

### 5.1 Purpose

Stakeholder engagement planning determines how stakeholders will participate in business analysis.

It identifies stakeholders, assesses their characteristics, and establishes appropriate communication and collaboration methods.

### 5.2 Stakeholder Identification

Potential stakeholders include:

* Business sponsors.
* Customers.
* End users.
* Subject-matter experts.
* Business managers.
* Developers.
* Testers.
* Operations teams.
* Suppliers.
* Regulators.
* Support personnel.

Stakeholder identification should continue throughout the initiative because new stakeholders may emerge.

### 5.3 Stakeholder Analysis

Stakeholder analysis examines factors such as:

* Roles and responsibilities.
* Authority and decision-making power.
* Influence over outcomes.
* Interests and expectations.
* Knowledge and expertise.
* Attitudes toward change.
* Availability.
* Communication preferences.
* Potential conflicts.

Understanding these factors helps the analyst select suitable engagement strategies.

### 5.4 Stakeholder Engagement Methods

| Method                  | Appropriate Use                                               |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Individual interviews   | Exploring detailed needs or sensitive concerns                |
| Workshops               | Building shared understanding among multiple stakeholders     |
| Surveys                 | Collecting input from a large population                      |
| Focus groups            | Exploring opinions from a selected group                      |
| Demonstrations          | Obtaining feedback on a proposed solution                     |
| Document reviews        | Learning from existing policies, procedures, and requirements |
| Regular status meetings | Coordinating activities and resolving issues                  |

The choice depends on the information needed, stakeholder availability, complexity, and cost.

### Example

An analyst designing a new employee onboarding process interviews HR staff, holds a workshop with department managers, and surveys new employees.

These methods provide different perspectives and help reveal inconsistencies between documented procedures and actual experiences.

## 6. Plan Business Analysis Governance

### 6.1 Purpose

Governance defines how decisions about business analysis work and its outputs will be made.

It establishes decision-making responsibilities, review processes, approvals, and change-control arrangements.

Governance questions include:

* Who approves requirements?
* Who can change priorities?
* Who resolves conflicts between stakeholders?
* How are requirement changes evaluated?
* Who accepts the final deliverables?
* What happens when stakeholders disagree?

### 6.2 Governance Components

A governance plan may specify:

* Decision-making authority.
* Approval responsibilities.
* Review procedures.
* Change-control processes.
* Escalation paths.
* Prioritization authority.
* Documentation standards.
* Reporting requirements.

Governance should be clear enough to prevent confusion without creating unnecessary bureaucracy.

### 6.3 Example

A financial organization requires compliance approval for changes affecting customer data.

The governance arrangement may require:

1. The Business Analyst to document the proposed requirement.
2. The product owner to assess business priority.
3. The compliance team to evaluate regulatory implications.
4. The designated authority to approve the change.
5. The team to update affected requirements and traceability records.

The analyst supports the process but does not automatically possess approval authority.

## 7. Plan Business Analysis Information Management

### 7.1 Purpose

This task determines how business analysis information will be captured, stored, organized, maintained, accessed, and reused.

Business analysis information includes requirements, designs, stakeholder information, decisions, models, assumptions, and other analytical outputs.

### 7.2 Information Management Considerations

A plan may define:

* Documentation formats.
* Naming conventions.
* Version control.
* Storage locations.
* Access permissions.
* Confidentiality requirements.
* Traceability practices.
* Retention periods.
* Review and approval status.
* Reuse of existing information.

Information should remain accessible to authorized stakeholders and protected from unauthorized access.

### 7.3 Traceability

Traceability establishes relationships between related pieces of business analysis information.

For example:

Traceability supports:

* Impact analysis.
* Change assessment.

* Verification.

* Understanding why a requirement exists.

### 7.4 Example

A company maintains requirements in a shared repository with unique identifiers and version history.

When a regulation changes, the analyst can identify affected requirements, designs, tests, and processes.

This reduces the risk of overlooking related work.

## 8. Identify Business Analysis Performance Improvements

### 8.1 Purpose

This task assesses business analysis work to identify opportunities for improvement.

The focus is on how effectively and efficiently business analysis activities are performed.

It is different from evaluating whether the final business solution delivers value, which is addressed under Solution Evaluation.

### 8.2 Performance Measures

Possible measures include:

* Time required to obtain requirement approvals.
* Number of requirement-related defects.
* Frequency of rework caused by unclear requirements.
* Stakeholder satisfaction with analysis activities.
* Percentage of requirements with agreed acceptance criteria.
* Time required to resolve requirement conflicts.
* Completeness of traceability.
* Effectiveness of workshops.

Measures should be selected carefully. A high number of documents or meetings does not necessarily indicate effective business analysis.

### 8.3 Improvement Cycle

A practical improvement cycle is:

1. Define the desired performance.
2. Collect performance information.
3. Compare actual performance with expectations.
4. Identify causes of gaps.
5. Recommend improvements.
6. Implement changes.
7. Measure results.

### Example

A team repeatedly discovers missing requirements during testing.

The analyst reviews previous projects and finds that key operational stakeholders were not involved early enough.

The team changes its stakeholder engagement plan and introduces an operations review before requirement approval.

The improvement is evaluated by tracking requirement-related defects in subsequent releases.

## 9. Monitoring and Adapting the Plan

Business analysis plans should be reviewed as new information becomes available.

Reasons to adjust a plan include:

* A stakeholder becomes unavailable.
* New regulations introduce additional requirements.
* The delivery approach changes.
* The business priority changes.
* A major assumption proves incorrect.
* A technique produces insufficient information.
* Risks or constraints change.

The analyst should assess the impact of proposed adjustments and follow the applicable governance process.

A plan is a guide for effective work, not a guarantee that the original assumptions will remain valid.

## 1. Module Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain the purpose of elicitation and collaboration.
* Distinguish elicitation from simply collecting requirements.
* Identify common elicitation techniques.
* Prepare for and conduct elicitation activities.
* Confirm and communicate elicitation results.
* Understand stakeholder collaboration and communication.
* Manage conflicts, misunderstandings, and incomplete information.
* Recognize the importance of documenting and validating elicitation results.

## 2. Introduction to Elicitation and Collaboration

Elicitation is the work of drawing out, discovering, and confirming information relevant to business analysis.

Collaboration involves working with stakeholders and other participants to achieve shared objectives.

Elicitation and collaboration are closely related because understanding business needs usually requires active participation from the people who know the business, use its processes, or are affected by the proposed change.

Elicitation is not limited to asking questions. It may involve observing work, analyzing existing documents, conducting experiments, facilitating workshops, examining data, or reviewing a prototype.

### Why Elicitation Matters

Effective elicitation helps the Business Analyst:

* Understand the real business problem.
* Discover stakeholder needs and expectations.
* Identify existing processes and constraints.
* Reveal assumptions and dependencies.
* Resolve conflicting perspectives.
* Reduce missing or misunderstood requirements.
* Establish a shared understanding of the desired change.

Poor elicitation can lead to incorrect requirements, unnecessary features, project delays, rework, and solutions that fail to meet stakeholder needs.

## 3. The Elicitation and Collaboration Knowledge Area

The BABOK Guide identifies five tasks within this knowledge area:

1. Prepare for Elicitation.
2. Conduct Elicitation.
3. Confirm Elicitation Results.
4. Communicate Business Analysis Information.
5. Manage Stakeholder Collaboration.

These tasks support effective discovery, communication, and stakeholder participation.

## 4. Prepare for Elicitation

### 4.1 Purpose

Preparation ensures that elicitation activities are organized to produce useful information.

The analyst determines what information is needed, which stakeholders should participate, which techniques are appropriate, and what resources are required.

### 4.2 Define the Elicitation Objectives

Before an interview or workshop, clarify its purpose.

Examples:

* Understand why customers abandon checkout.
* Identify current inventory management procedures.
* Discover reporting requirements for department managers.
* Determine why employees experience repeated system failures.
* Clarify the acceptance criteria for a proposed feature.

A clear objective helps prevent discussions from becoming unfocused.

### 4.3 Identify Relevant Stakeholders

Determine who has the knowledge, experience, authority, or perspective needed for the activity.

For example, investigating delayed orders may require participation from:

* Warehouse employees.
* Inventory managers.
* Delivery coordinators.
* Customer service representatives.
* IT support.
* Customers.

Relying only on senior management may produce an incomplete understanding of operational reality.

### 4.4 Select Appropriate Elicitation Techniques

The choice depends on:

* The type of information required.
* Stakeholder availability.
* Complexity of the problem.
* Number of participants.
* Geographic distribution.
* Sensitivity of the information.
* Time and budget constraints.
* Whether stakeholders can explain the existing process accurately.

### 4.5 Prepare Supporting Materials

Preparation may include:

* Interview questions.
* Workshop agendas.
* Existing requirements.
* Process diagrams.
* Questionnaires.
* Sample reports.
* Prototypes.
* Observation checklists.
* Data extracts.

Existing documentation should be treated as a source of information, not automatically assumed to reflect current practice.

## 5. Conduct Elicitation

Conducting elicitation means performing the planned activity and capturing relevant information.

The analyst should create an environment in which participants can provide accurate information and clarify misunderstandings.

Typical activities include:

* Asking open-ended questions.
* Probing for specific examples.
* Observing real work.
* Recording important findings.
* Clarifying terminology.
* Identifying assumptions.
* Investigating inconsistencies.
* Confirming interpretations during the conversation.

The analyst should remain objective and avoid leading participants toward a preferred solution.

### Open-Ended Questions

Open-ended questions encourage detailed responses.

Examples:

* "Can you describe how an order is processed?"
* "What difficulties do you encounter during this process?"
* "What happens when the system is unavailable?"
* "How do you determine whether a report is accurate?"

### Closed-Ended Questions

Closed-ended questions seek specific answers.

Examples:

* "Does the system require manager approval?"
* "Is the report generated daily?"
* "Can customers cancel an order after dispatch?"

Both types are useful. Open-ended questions explore a topic, while closed-ended questions clarify particular facts.

### Probing Questions

Probing questions investigate an answer more deeply.

Examples:

* "Can you give me an example?"
* "Why does that happen?"
* "How often does this occur?"
* "What happens next?"
* "What is the business impact?"

Effective elicitation often combines all three question types.

## 6. Common Elicitation Techniques

### 6.1 Interviews

An interview is a structured or semi-structured conversation used to obtain information from one or more people.

**Best for:**

* Understanding individual responsibilities.
* Investigating detailed workflows.
* Exploring sensitive problems.
* Clarifying stakeholder expectations.
* Discovering exceptions and unusual cases.

**Advantages:**

* Allows follow-up questions.
* Provides detailed explanations.
* Helps clarify ambiguity.
* Can uncover information that stakeholders may not share in a group.

**Limitations:**

* Time-consuming.
* May reflect individual bias.
* Can be affected by poor questioning.
* Different stakeholders may provide contradictory accounts.

**Example:** Interview warehouse supervisors to understand why order-picking errors occur during busy periods.

### 6.2 Workshops

A workshop brings multiple participants together to collaborate on a defined topic.

**Best for:**

* Developing shared understanding.
* Resolving disagreements.
* Prioritizing requirements.
* Mapping business processes.
* Identifying opportunities for improvement.

**Advantages:**

* Multiple perspectives can be compared immediately.
* Conflicts can be addressed collaboratively.
* Decisions may be reached more quickly.
* Participants can build on one another's ideas.

**Limitations:**

* Dominant participants may influence the discussion.
* Scheduling can be difficult.
* Group pressure may discourage disagreement.
* Poor facilitation can reduce effectiveness.

**Example:** Organize a workshop involving finance, sales, and operations to agree on invoice approval requirements.

### 6.3 Surveys and Questionnaires

Surveys collect information from a relatively large number of participants using a consistent set of questions.

**Best for:**

* Measuring user satisfaction.
* Identifying common problems.
* Gathering feedback across departments.
* Collecting information from geographically distributed stakeholders.

**Advantages:**

* Can reach many participants.
* Standardized questions simplify comparisons.
* May require less time per participant.

**Limitations:**

* Responses may lack detail.
* Questions can be misunderstood.
* Response rates may be low.
* Poorly designed questions introduce bias.

**Example:** Survey 500 customers to determine which checkout problems occur most frequently.

### 6.4 Observation

Observation involves examining stakeholders as they perform actual tasks.

It is particularly useful when stakeholders struggle to explain their work or when documented procedures differ from reality.

**Best for:**

* Understanding real workflows.
* Identifying delays and bottlenecks.
* Discovering workarounds.
* Investigating repetitive tasks.
* Understanding environmental constraints.

**Advantages:**

* Reveals actual behavior.
* Can uncover undocumented procedures.
* Helps identify inefficiencies that interviews miss.

**Limitations:**

* People may change their behavior when observed.
* Observation takes time.
* Some tasks occur infrequently.
* The analyst may misinterpret what is observed.

**Example:** Observe customer service agents handling calls to identify repeated manual steps.

### 6.5 Document Analysis

Document analysis examines existing records to understand business operations, rules, and requirements.

Potential sources include:

* Policies.
* Procedures.
* Contracts.
* Existing requirements.
* Business reports.
* Forms.
* Training manuals.
* Regulations.
* Support tickets.
* Previous project documentation.

**Advantages:**

* Provides historical information.
* Helps identify existing rules.
* Supports preparation for interviews.
* May reveal inconsistencies between documents.

**Limitations:**

* Documents may be outdated.
* Some practices may never have been documented.
* Different documents may contradict one another.

**Example:** Review support tickets and service-level agreements to identify recurring customer service problems.

### 6.6 Brainstorming

Brainstorming generates ideas and possible solutions.

It is useful when exploring opportunities, investigating causes, or identifying alternative approaches.

Effective brainstorming separates idea generation from evaluation so participants are not discouraged from contributing.

**Example:** Generate possible explanations for declining customer retention before evaluating each hypothesis.

### 6.7 Prototyping

A prototype is an early representation of a potential solution used to obtain feedback.

Prototypes may be:

* Paper sketches.
* Wireframes.
* Clickable mockups.
* Interactive demonstrations.
* Limited working implementations.

**Best for:**

* Clarifying interface expectations.
* Discovering missing functionality.
* Testing user assumptions.
* Comparing design alternatives.

A prototype should not automatically be treated as an approved final design.

**Example:** Show customers a clickable checkout mockup and ask them to complete a purchase.

### 6.8 Focus Groups

A focus group involves a selected group of participants discussing a topic under facilitation.

It can help explore opinions, expectations, experiences, and reactions.

**Example:** Invite a small group of customers to discuss their experience with a delivery-tracking service.

Focus groups are useful for qualitative insight but should not automatically be treated as statistically representative of an entire population.

### 6.9 Interface Analysis

Interface analysis examines interactions between a solution and external users, systems, or other components.

Interfaces may involve:

* User interfaces.
* APIs.
* External applications.
* Payment providers.
* Hardware.
* Data exchange mechanisms.

**Example:** Analyze how an e-commerce platform exchanges payment status with an external payment gateway.

This helps identify integration requirements, constraints, and dependencies.

## 7. Choosing the Right Technique

Different techniques provide different types of information.

| Business Situation                              | Suitable Technique |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| Understand one expert's detailed workflow       | Interview          |
| Resolve conflicting departmental requirements   | Workshop           |
| Gather feedback from hundreds of users          | Survey             |
| Discover undocumented work practices            | Observation        |
| Understand existing policies and business rules | Document analysis  |
| Generate possible explanations or solutions     | Brainstorming      |
| Explore interface expectations                  | Prototyping        |
| Investigate system-to-system interactions       | Interface analysis |
| Explore opinions and reactions in a small group | Focus group        |

More than one technique may be needed to understand a complex problem.

For example, interviews may reveal reported issues, observation may show actual behavior, and document analysis may establish formal policies.

Using multiple independent sources can help verify findings.

## 8. Confirm Elicitation Results

### 8.1 Purpose

Confirmation checks that the information captured during elicitation accurately reflects what participants intended to communicate.

It helps prevent misunderstandings from becoming part of the business analysis information.

### 8.2 Confirmation Methods

The analyst may:

* Summarize findings to participants.
* Circulate meeting notes.
* Review process diagrams with stakeholders.
* Ask participants to confirm assumptions.
* Demonstrate a prototype.
* Clarify contradictory statements.
* Record unresolved questions.
* Request corrections to documented information.

Confirmation is not necessarily the same as formal approval.

A stakeholder may confirm that the analyst accurately recorded a discussion without having authority to approve the resulting requirements.

### Example

During an interview, a warehouse supervisor states that urgent orders should be processed first.

The analyst documents this rule and asks the supervisor to confirm whether urgency is determined by delivery deadline, customer category, or another factor.

The clarification prevents an ambiguous statement from becoming an incomplete requirement.

## 9. Communicate Business Analysis Information

### 9.1 Purpose

Business analysis information must be communicated in a way that stakeholders can understand and use.

Communication includes sharing information, explaining findings, and ensuring that relevant participants receive the appropriate information.

### 9.2 Effective Communication

The analyst should consider:

* Audience knowledge.
* Information complexity.
* Communication preferences.
* Confidentiality.
* Timing.
* Required level of detail.
* Purpose of the communication.
* Whether feedback is necessary.

Executives may need a concise summary of business impact and risks. Developers may need detailed requirements and interface behavior. Operational employees may need process diagrams and practical examples.

### 9.3 Common Communication Formats

* Reports.
* Presentations.
* Requirements documents.
* Process models.
* Email summaries.
* Workshops.
* Dashboards.
* Demonstrations.
* Shared repositories.
* Decision logs.

### Example

To explain a proposed warehouse change, the analyst prepares:

* An executive summary for management.
* A process diagram for warehouse supervisors.
* Detailed functional requirements for developers.
* Training materials for warehouse employees.

The underlying information is consistent, but its presentation is adapted to each audience.

## 10. Manage Stakeholder Collaboration

### 10.1 Purpose

Stakeholder collaboration maintains effective working relationships and encourages participants to contribute to the business analysis effort.

Collaboration includes managing expectations, resolving misunderstandings, addressing conflicts, and supporting timely decisions.

### 10.2 Common Collaboration Challenges

**Conflicting priorities:** Finance wants lower costs, while operations wants additional staffing.

**Limited availability:** Important subject-matter experts cannot attend workshops.

**Communication barriers:** Stakeholders use different terminology or have different technical backgrounds.

**Resistance to change:** Employees fear that automation will make their roles less secure.

**Unclear authority:** Participants disagree about who can approve requirements.

**Competing assumptions:** Stakeholders have different beliefs about the cause of a problem.

### 10.3 Managing Conflicts

A Business Analyst should investigate the source of disagreement rather than simply choose the loudest or most senior participant.

Useful approaches include:

1. Clarify each stakeholder's underlying need.
2. Separate facts from assumptions.
3. Identify shared objectives.
4. Evaluate constraints and trade-offs.
5. Compare alternatives using agreed criteria.
6. Facilitate a decision by the authorized person or group.
7. Document the outcome and any remaining disagreement.

The analyst facilitates understanding and informed decisions but does not necessarily have authority to impose the final decision.

### Example

Finance wants to minimize software costs. Customer service wants a feature-rich system.

The analyst compares alternative solutions against cost, customer impact, operational efficiency, risk, and strategic objectives.

The stakeholders can then make a more informed decision.

## 2. Introduction

Requirements Life Cycle Management focuses on managing requirements and designs from their initial development through changes, implementation, and eventual retirement.

Requirements do not remain static throughout an initiative. Stakeholder needs may change, new regulations may appear, and solution designs may evolve.

Effective requirements management helps ensure that requirements remain understandable, relevant, consistent, and aligned with business objectives.

The BABOK Guide identifies five tasks within this knowledge area:

1. Trace Requirements.
2. Maintain Requirements.
3. Prioritize Requirements.
4. Assess Requirements Changes.
5. Approve Requirements.

These tasks are related and may be performed iteratively.

## 3. Trace Requirements

### 3.1 Definition

Requirements traceability establishes and maintains relationships between requirements, designs, business objectives, stakeholders, and other relevant information.

Traceability helps explain why a requirement exists and what other elements may be affected by a change.

### 3.2 Types of Traceability

**Backward traceability:** Links a requirement to its origin, such as a business objective or stakeholder need.

**Forward traceability:** Links a requirement to downstream elements, such as designs, test cases, and implemented functionality.

**Bidirectional traceability:** Supports movement in both directions, from the requirement to its source and from the source to the requirement.

### 3.3 Benefits

Traceability supports:

* Impact analysis.
* Change management.
* Verification and validation.
* Compliance.
* Test coverage.
* Identification of unnecessary requirements.
* Understanding dependencies.
* Demonstrating alignment with business objectives.

### 3.4 Example

A retailer has the business objective of reducing fraudulent transactions.

A stakeholder requirement states that suspicious transactions must be reviewed.

A solution requirement specifies that transactions exceeding defined risk thresholds must be flagged.

A test case verifies that qualifying transactions are flagged correctly.

Traceability connects these elements and demonstrates how the solution requirement supports the business objective.

### 3.5 Traceability Matrix

A traceability matrix is one way to represent relationships between requirements and other artifacts.

| ID    | Requirement                  | Source             | Related Test | Status    |
| ----- | ---------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------ | --------- |
| BR-01 | Reduce order errors          | Business objective | KPI-01       | Approved  |
| SR-01 | Validate product identifiers | BR-01              | TC-01        | Approved  |
| SR-02 | Display validation errors    | SR-01              | TC-02        | In review |

A matrix is not the only way to manage traceability. A requirements management tool or repository may provide the same capability.

## 4. Maintain Requirements

### 4.1 Purpose

Requirements maintenance ensures that requirements and designs remain accurate, consistent, usable, and relevant throughout their life cycles.

Maintenance may involve:

* Updating requirement status.
* Correcting inconsistencies.
* Managing versions.
* Preserving relationships.
* Recording approved changes.
* Removing obsolete information according to governance rules.
* Keeping requirements accessible to authorized stakeholders.

### 4.2 Requirements Status

Common status labels include:

* Proposed.
* Under review.
* Approved.
* Implemented.
* Verified.
* Rejected.
* Deferred.
* Retired.

The precise status model depends on the organization's process.

A requirement's status should not be confused with its priority. A high-priority requirement can still be under review.

### 4.3 Version Management

Version management records changes to requirements and helps users identify the current approved version.

For example, a payment requirement may change from requiring one-time passwords to supporting an additional authentication method.

A controlled repository should preserve the history of the change, its approval, and its impact on related artifacts.

## 5. Prioritize Requirements

### 5.1 Purpose

Prioritization determines the relative importance of requirements so that stakeholders can allocate limited time, money, and resources effectively.

Not every requirement can necessarily be delivered immediately.

Prioritization considers business value, risk, cost, dependencies, urgency, and stakeholder needs.

### 5.2 Common Prioritization Factors

* Business benefit.
* Regulatory necessity.
* Customer impact.
* Implementation cost.
* Technical risk.
* Dependencies.
* Urgency.
* Operational impact.
* Availability of resources.
* Consequences of not implementing the requirement.

### 5.3 MoSCoW Technique

MoSCoW categorizes requirements into four groups:

**Must have:** Essential for the agreed scope or minimum acceptable outcome.

**Should have:** Important but not absolutely essential for the immediate release.

**Could have:** Desirable if resources and time permit.

**Won't have this time:** Explicitly excluded from the current scope or release.

The final category does not necessarily mean the requirement will never be implemented.

### 5.4 Example

An online store is launching a new checkout process.

| Requirement                 | Priority             | Reason                                    |
| --------------------------- | -------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Secure payment processing   | Must have            | Essential to safe transactions            |
| Order confirmation          | Must have            | Required for basic customer communication |
| Saved shopping lists        | Could have           | Useful but not essential to launch        |
| Custom animated transitions | Won't have this time | Excluded from the initial release         |

Priorities should be agreed with the appropriate stakeholders rather than assigned solely according to the analyst's personal preferences.

### 5.5 Priority Conflicts

Two stakeholders may assign different priorities to the same requirement.

The analyst should clarify the business outcomes, constraints, dependencies, and consequences of delay, then support a decision through the agreed governance process.

## 6. Assess Requirements Changes

### 6.1 Purpose

This task evaluates proposed changes to requirements or designs before decisions are made about whether and how to implement them.

A change may affect cost, schedule, scope, risk, quality, dependencies, and business value.

### 6.2 Change Assessment Process

A practical process includes:

1. Record the proposed change.
2. Understand the reason for the change.
3. Identify affected requirements and designs.
4. Assess business benefits and risks.
5. Evaluate cost, effort, and schedule implications.
6. Identify dependencies and downstream impacts.
7. Obtain the required decision.
8. Update affected information if approved.
9. Communicate the outcome.

The exact process depends on the initiative's governance and delivery approach.

### 6.3 Example

A stakeholder requests an additional identity-verification feature shortly before a release.

The analyst assesses:

* Whether the feature is required by a new regulation.
* Which existing requirements are affected.
* The implementation effort.
* Security and privacy implications.
* Testing requirements.
* Delivery risks.
* Whether the release should be adjusted.

The analyst provides the assessment to the authorized decision-makers.

A request should not automatically be accepted or rejected without considering its implications.

## 7. Approve Requirements

### 7.1 Purpose

Approval establishes that the appropriate stakeholders or authorized decision-makers accept requirements or designs for the next stage of work.

Approval may confirm agreement on scope, expectations, priorities, or readiness for implementation.

### 7.2 Approval Considerations

Before approval, stakeholders may need to determine whether requirements are:

* Understandable.
* Consistent.
* Feasible.
* Aligned with business needs.
* Sufficiently detailed for their intended use.
* Within the agreed scope.
* Acceptable in terms of risk and cost.

Different requirements may require approval from different authorities.

For example, business requirements may need sponsor approval, while security requirements may require review by an authorized security or compliance representative.

### 7.3 Approval Is Not the Same as Validation

Approval is a decision by an authorized person or group.

Validation evaluates whether requirements or designs support the business needs and desired outcomes.

A requirement can be formally approved and still be based on an incorrect assumption. Approval does not eliminate the need for continued analysis and validation.

## 8. Verification and Validation

These concepts are related but answer different questions.

**Verification:** Are the requirements or designs expressed appropriately and with sufficient quality?

Verification may examine clarity, consistency, completeness, feasibility, and testability.

**Validation:** Do the requirements or designs address the business need and deliver the intended value?

Validation focuses on alignment with the underlying need and desired outcomes.

### Example

Requirement: "The system should process orders quickly."

Verification identifies that "quickly" is ambiguous and not objectively testable.

The requirement is revised to specify an agreed processing-time threshold.

Validation then examines whether that threshold is appropriate for the business objective and stakeholder needs.

## 9. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: Banking Application

A bank is developing a mobile application.

Initial requirements include:

* Customers can view account balances.
* Customers can transfer money.
* Customers receive transaction notifications.

Later, the compliance team identifies an additional authentication requirement.

### Applying Requirements Life Cycle Management

**Trace requirements:** Link authentication requirements to relevant security and regulatory objectives.

**Maintain requirements:** Update the repository and preserve version history.

**Prioritize requirements:** Assess whether the authentication change is mandatory before launch.

**Assess changes:** Evaluate implementation cost, testing effort, schedule, and risk.

**Approve requirements:** Obtain the required approval from authorized stakeholders.

After implementation, verify that the requirement is correctly expressed and tested, and validate that the authentication approach satisfies the underlying security and business needs.

# 06 â€” Strategy Analysis

## 1. Module Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain the purpose of strategy analysis.
* Understand the current state and future state of an enterprise.
* Identify business needs and strategic objectives.
* Perform gap analysis.
* Understand risks associated with organizational change.
* Explain how a change strategy is developed.
* Apply strategy analysis concepts to business scenarios.

## 2. Introduction to Strategy Analysis

Strategy Analysis examines the business need, the current state of an enterprise, the desired future state, and the change required to move between them.

It helps organizations determine why change is necessary, what outcomes they want to achieve, and which approaches may deliver those outcomes.

Strategy analysis takes a broader perspective than documenting individual requirements. It considers business objectives, organizational capabilities, market conditions, risks, and long-term value.

The BABOK Guide identifies six tasks within Strategy Analysis:

1. Analyze Current State.
2. Define Future State.
3. Assess Risks.
4. Define Change Strategy.

The first three activities provide the foundation for defining a suitable change strategy. These tasks may be revisited as new information emerges.

## 3. Analyze Current State

### 3.1 Purpose

Analyzing the current state establishes how the enterprise operates today and why change may be required.

The current state may include:

* Business processes.
* Organizational structure.
* Technology systems.
* Employee capabilities.
* Information flows.
* Policies and regulations.
* Performance measures.
* Customer experiences.
* Existing problems and opportunities.

### 3.2 Identify Business Needs

Business needs may arise from internal or external factors.

Internal factors include:

* High operating costs.
* Inefficient processes.
* Frequent errors.
* Poor data quality.
* Limited employee capacity.
* Outdated systems.

External factors include:

* New regulations.
* Changing customer expectations.
* Competitor actions.
* Economic conditions.
* Technological developments.
* Supplier disruptions.

### 3.3 Analyze Capabilities

A capability is an ability that an enterprise possesses or needs to achieve a particular outcome.

Capabilities may involve people, processes, information, technology, and organizational resources.

For example, an online retailer may need capabilities in demand forecasting, inventory management, order fulfillment, and customer support.

The analyst examines whether existing capabilities are sufficient to meet business objectives.

### 3.4 Current-State Performance

Performance information can help establish why change is needed.

Examples include:

* Average processing time.
* Cost per transaction.
* Customer satisfaction.
* Defect rates.
* Revenue growth.
* Employee productivity.
* Service availability.

A baseline provides a reference point against which future performance can be evaluated.

### Example

A company processes customer refunds in an average of eight days. Customer complaints have increased, and employees manually check multiple systems.

The analyst documents the current process, identifies delays, interviews employees, and measures the frequency of errors.

These findings establish the current-state problems that a change initiative should address.

## 4. Define Future State

### 4.1 Purpose

Defining the future state describes the desired conditions after a change.

The future state should explain what the enterprise wants to achieve and how success will be recognized.

It may include:

* Desired business outcomes.
* Required capabilities.
* Target performance levels.
* New or improved processes.
* Technology changes.
* Organizational changes.
* Business constraints.
* Measures of success.

### 4.2 Define Measurable Objectives

Objectives should be specific enough to guide decisions and evaluate outcomes.

Weak objective: Improve customer service.

Stronger objective: Reduce average customer support response time from 12 hours to four hours within six months while maintaining customer satisfaction.

The stronger objective identifies a measurable outcome, baseline, target, and time frame.

### 4.3 Define Required Capabilities

The analyst identifies the capabilities needed to achieve the future state.

For example, a company seeking faster refunds may require:

* Automated refund eligibility checks.
* Accurate order-status information.
* Clear approval rules.
* Integration between payment and order systems.
* Staff training.
* Exception handling.

The future state describes required business capabilities without necessarily committing to one specific technical implementation.

### 4.4 Define Constraints

Constraints limit the possible future state or the methods available to achieve it.

Examples include:

* Budget limits.
* Legal obligations.
* Fixed deadlines.
* Existing infrastructure.
* Staff availability.
* Data residency rules.
* Contractual commitments.

A future state must be desirable while remaining realistic within the relevant constraints.

## 5. Perform Gap Analysis

Gap analysis compares the current state with the future state to identify what must change.

A gap may involve missing capabilities, insufficient performance, outdated processes, technology limitations, or organizational weaknesses.

### Example

| Area              | Current State       | Future State                 | Identified Gap                 |
| ----------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Refund processing | Manual checks       | Automated eligibility checks | Automation capability missing  |
| Customer data     | Distributed systems | Consistent customer record   | Data integration required      |
| Staff knowledge   | Informal procedures | Standardized training        | Training program needed        |
| Performance       | Eight-day average   | Two-day average              | Process efficiency improvement |

Gap analysis helps define the scope of change and identify possible initiatives.

### Important Distinction

A gap is the difference between the current and desired states. It is not automatically a specific solution.

For example, the gap may be slow processing. Automation could be one response, but process redesign or clearer approval rules might also address the problem.

## 6. Assess Risks

### 6.1 Purpose

Strategy analysis considers uncertainties that may affect the ability to achieve the future state.

A risk is an uncertain event or condition that could affect objectives.

Risks can have negative or positive effects, although change assessments frequently emphasize threats.

### 6.2 Common Business Risks

* Implementation costs exceed expectations.
* Employees resist new procedures.
* Data migration fails.
* A new system disrupts operations.
* Regulatory requirements are misunderstood.
* Expected benefits do not materialize.
* Key stakeholders become unavailable.
* A supplier fails to deliver.
* New technology introduces security weaknesses.

### 6.3 Risk Assessment

A practical risk assessment considers:

* The risk description.
* Its potential causes.
* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Existing controls.
* Possible responses.
* The person responsible for managing it.

Organizations may use qualitative scales, numerical scoring, or more formal risk analysis techniques.

### 6.4 Risk Responses

Possible responses include:

* Avoiding the risk by changing the approach.
* Reducing its likelihood or impact.
* Transferring certain consequences through contractual arrangements or insurance.
* Accepting the risk when appropriate.
* Exploiting or enhancing a positive opportunity.

The appropriate response depends on the risk, organizational tolerance, cost, and potential consequences.

### Example

A retailer plans to migrate millions of customer records.

Risk: Data migration errors could disrupt customer service.

Possible responses:

* Perform data profiling.
* Clean records before migration.
* Conduct test migrations.
* Reconcile record counts.
* Prepare a rollback plan.
* Monitor results after launch.

## 7. Define Change Strategy

### 7.1 Purpose

A change strategy describes the approach for moving from the current state to the future state.

It identifies the broad actions required to address the business need and achieve desired outcomes.

A change strategy may consider:

* Alternative solutions.
* Required capabilities.
* Costs and benefits.
* Risks.
* Dependencies.
* Organizational readiness.
* Implementation options.
* Transition arrangements.
* Expected business value.

### 7.2 Possible Change Strategies

A company may choose to:

* Improve an existing process.
* Purchase an off-the-shelf system.
* Develop a custom solution.
* Outsource selected activities.
* Introduce a new operating model.
* Train employees.
* Combine several changes.

### 7.3 Compare Alternatives

Alternatives should be evaluated against relevant criteria.

For example:

| Criterion             | Option A: Improve Existing System | Option B: Replace System  |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Initial cost          | Lower                             | Higher                    |
| Implementation time   | Shorter                           | Longer                    |
| Flexibility           | Limited                           | Potentially greater       |
| Migration risk        | Lower                             | Higher                    |
| Long-term maintenance | May remain costly                 | Depends on the new system |

These are illustrative comparisons. The actual assessment should use evidence from the organization.

### 7.4 Organizational Readiness

A technically feasible change may still fail if the organization is not prepared to adopt it.

Readiness considerations include:

* Leadership support.
* Employee skills.
* Availability of resources.
* Stakeholder commitment.
* Organizational culture.
* Training needs.
* Operational capacity.

A change strategy may therefore include communication, training, pilot deployments, and phased implementation.

## 8. Strategy Analysis and Business Value

Strategy analysis should connect the proposed change to business outcomes.

A solution may be delivered successfully from a technical perspective without achieving the expected business benefits.

For example, a new inventory system may launch on schedule but fail to reduce stockouts because purchasing policies remain unchanged.

The analyst should identify the capabilities and organizational changes needed to realize the intended value.

# 07 â€” Requirements Analysis and Design Definition

## 1. Module Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain requirements analysis and design definition.
* Understand how requirements are specified and modeled.
* Distinguish requirements from designs.
* Identify useful requirements analysis techniques.
* Understand requirement verification and validation.
* Explain requirements architecture and design options.
* Evaluate solution alternatives.
* Define requirements and designs that support business needs.

## 2. Introduction

Requirements Analysis and Design Definition focuses on analyzing, specifying, modeling, verifying, and validating requirements and designs.

The objective is to transform discovered information into a clear, usable representation of what stakeholders need and how a potential solution could satisfy those needs.

The BABOK Guide identifies six tasks within this knowledge area:

1. Specify and Model Requirements.
2. Verify Requirements.
3. Validate Requirements.
4. Define Requirements Architecture.
5. Define Design Options.
6. Analyze Potential Value and Recommend Solution.

These tasks are interconnected and may be repeated as requirements and designs evolve.

## 3. Specify and Model Requirements

### 3.1 Purpose

Specifying requirements means expressing them in a form that stakeholders and delivery teams can understand and use.

Modeling requirements represents information visually or structurally to clarify relationships, behavior, data, or processes.

Requirements may be documented using:

* Natural-language statements.
* User stories.
* Use cases.
* Process diagrams.
* Data models.
* Decision tables.
* State diagrams.
* Interface specifications.
* Prototypes.

The representation should match the complexity of the need and the audience using it.

### 3.2 Characteristics of Effective Requirements

Good requirements should be:

**Clear:** The intended meaning is understandable.

**Complete:** Necessary information is included for the requirement's intended purpose.

**Consistent:** The requirement does not conflict with related requirements.

**Feasible:** It can reasonably be satisfied within the applicable constraints.

**Testable:** Evidence can be collected to determine whether it has been met.

**Necessary:** It supports a business need, stakeholder need, or relevant constraint.

**Traceable:** Its origin and relationships can be identified when required.

**Unambiguous:** It has one clear interpretation within the intended context.

### 3.3 Example of an Unclear Requirement

Weak requirement:

"The system should load quickly."

Improved requirement:

"Under the agreed normal operating load, the customer dashboard shall load within two seconds for at least 95% of requests."

The improved version defines a measurable performance expectation. The actual threshold and operating conditions should be agreed with stakeholders.

### 3.4 User Stories

A user story commonly follows this format:

"As a [user], I want [capability], so that [benefit]."

Example:

"As a warehouse supervisor, I want to view low-stock products so that I can replenish inventory before stockouts occur."

A user story communicates the user's need and its benefit. Acceptance criteria provide additional detail about the conditions that must be satisfied.

Example acceptance criteria:

* The supervisor can view products below their defined reorder thresholds.
* The list displays the current available quantity.
* The list can be filtered by warehouse location.

User stories are commonly used in adaptive delivery approaches, but their usefulness depends on the context.

## 4. Modeling Requirements

### 4.1 Process Models

Process models describe activities, decisions, and flows of work.

They help identify:

* Process steps.
* Responsibilities.
* Decision points.
* Delays.
* Exceptions.
* Opportunities for improvement.

Example: A loan approval process may include application submission, identity verification, credit assessment, approval, and notification.

### 4.2 Data Models

Data models represent information entities, attributes, and relationships.

For example, an e-commerce system may contain:

* Customer.
* Order.
* Product.
* Payment.
* Shipment.

An order may contain multiple products, and each order may have an associated payment and shipment record, depending on the business rules.

Data modeling helps identify information requirements and potential inconsistencies.

### 4.3 Use Cases

A use case describes interactions between an actor and a system to achieve a goal.

Example: A customer submits an online order.

A use case may describe:

1. The customer selects products.
2. The system displays the cart.
3. The customer provides delivery details.
4. The system validates the order.
5. The payment is processed.
6. The customer receives confirmation.

Alternative flows may cover insufficient stock, invalid addresses, or declined payments.

### 4.4 Decision Tables

Decision tables represent combinations of conditions and their corresponding outcomes.

They are useful when business rules depend on several conditions.

Example: A discount may depend on membership status and order value.

| Member? | Order Value   | Discount |
| ------- | ------------- | -------- |
| Yes     | At least $100 | 10%      |
| Yes     | Below $100    | 5%       |
| No      | At least $100 | 3%       |
| No      | Below $100    | 0%       |

This is an illustrative rule set. Real business rules should be approved by the appropriate stakeholders.

## 5. Verify Requirements

Verification examines whether requirements and designs are expressed appropriately and meet the relevant quality expectations.

Typical verification questions include:

* Is the requirement clear?
* Is it complete enough for its intended purpose?
* Is it consistent with related requirements?
* Is it feasible?
* Is it testable?
* Does it contain ambiguous terms?
* Does it conflict with another requirement?

### Example

Requirement: "The system shall send reports regularly."

Verification identifies ambiguity in "regularly."

The analyst clarifies the intended frequency, recipient, format, and delivery conditions.

Verification improves the quality of the requirement representation.

## 6. Validate Requirements

Validation evaluates whether requirements and designs support the business need and desired outcomes.

A requirement may be clearly written and technically feasible but still fail to address the actual business problem.

### Example

A company requests an automated report showing the number of calls handled by each employee.

The requirement is clear and testable.

However, the underlying business need is to reduce customer waiting time.

The analyst investigates whether call volume alone supports this objective or whether waiting time, abandonment rates, staffing, and resolution quality are also relevant.

Validation helps ensure that the requirements remain connected to the intended business outcomes.

### Verification vs. Validation

| Verification                                      | Validation                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Examines the quality of requirements and designs  | Examines alignment with business needs                      |
| Focuses on clarity, consistency, and completeness | Focuses on relevance and expected value                     |
| Identifies ambiguous or contradictory statements  | Identifies requirements that do not solve the right problem |

Both activities are important and may occur repeatedly.

## 7. Define Requirements Architecture

Requirements architecture describes the structure and relationships among requirements and designs.

It helps organize complex requirements into meaningful groups and shows how different components relate to one another.

### Benefits

* Improves understanding of complex solutions.
* Supports traceability.
* Reveals dependencies.
* Identifies inconsistencies.
* Supports impact analysis.
* Helps stakeholders understand how capabilities fit together.

### Example

A banking application may contain requirements related to:

* Authentication.
* Account management.
* Payments.
* Notifications.
* Reporting.
* Audit logging.

Requirements architecture helps explain the relationships between these areas. For example, payment processing may depend on authentication, account validation, transaction limits, and audit logging.

## 8. Define Design Options

### 8.1 Purpose

Design options describe possible ways to satisfy requirements and achieve the desired future state.

A design option may involve changes to:

* Software.
* Business processes.
* Organizational structure.
* Technology infrastructure.
* Roles and responsibilities.
* Training.
* External service arrangements.

### 8.2 Solution Options

Common options include:

**Build:** Develop a custom solution.

**Buy:** Purchase an existing product.

**Configure:** Adapt an existing product to meet requirements.

**Outsource:** Obtain services from an external provider.

**Improve existing capabilities:** Modify existing processes or systems.

**Do nothing:** Retain the current state when the costs, risks, or lack of value justify that decision.

The do-nothing option should be evaluated realistically, including the consequences of leaving the existing problem unresolved.

### 8.3 Compare Design Options

Relevant criteria may include:

* Cost.
* Business value.
* Technical feasibility.
* Implementation time.
* Operational impact.
* Risk.
* Security.
* Maintainability.
* Scalability.
* User experience.
* Regulatory compliance.

No single criterion automatically determines the best option.

## 9. Analyze Potential Value and Recommend a Solution

This task evaluates the potential value of design options and supports the recommendation of a suitable solution.

The analyst considers how well each option addresses the business need and the trade-offs associated with it.

### Example

A company wants to reduce customer service response time.

Three alternatives are considered:

1. Hire additional agents.
2. Improve the existing customer service process.
3. Implement a chatbot for common questions.

The analyst compares costs, expected response-time improvements, customer experience, implementation effort, risks, and operational implications.

A chatbot may reduce repetitive inquiries but may not address complex cases. Additional agents may improve capacity but increase recurring costs.

The best recommendation depends on evidence and the organization's objectives.

### Cost-Benefit Analysis

A simple financial assessment compares expected benefits with expected costs.

Net benefit can be represented as:

Net Benefit = Total Expected Benefits âˆ’ Total Expected Costs

For example:

* Expected benefits: $120,000.
* Expected costs: $80,000.
* Net benefit: $40,000.

This simple calculation does not capture every consideration. Time value of money, uncertainty, operational risks, and non-financial benefits may also matter.

# 08 â€” Solution Evaluation

## 1. Module Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain the purpose of solution evaluation.
* Understand how solution performance is measured.
* Distinguish actual performance from expected performance.
* Identify limitations and enterprise constraints.
* Explain how solution value is assessed.
* Understand how evaluation findings support recommendations.
* Identify opportunities to improve solution performance.
* Apply solution evaluation concepts to business scenarios.

## 2. Introduction to Solution Evaluation

Solution Evaluation assesses the performance and value of a solution after it has been implemented or while it is being considered for implementation.

It helps determine whether a solution satisfies business needs, delivers expected benefits, and performs adequately within its operating environment.

A solution may be technically functional but still fail to produce the intended business outcomes.

For example, an organization may successfully deploy a new customer relationship management system, yet customer satisfaction may remain unchanged because employees do not use the system consistently.

Solution evaluation helps identify these gaps and recommend appropriate actions.

The BABOK Guide identifies six tasks within Solution Evaluation:

1. Measure Solution Performance.
2. Analyze Performance Measures.
3. Assess Solution Limitations.
4. Assess Enterprise Limitations.
5. Recommend Actions to Increase Solution Value.
6. Evaluate Solution Performance.

## 3. Measure Solution Performance

### 3.1 Purpose

Measuring solution performance involves collecting information about how effectively a solution operates.

Measurements should be linked to the business objectives and performance expectations established for the initiative.

Examples include:

* Processing time.
* Transaction success rate.
* Error rate.
* Customer satisfaction.
* Operating cost.
* System availability.
* Employee productivity.
* Conversion rate.
* Revenue.
* Compliance outcomes.

### 3.2 Key Performance Indicators

A Key Performance Indicator (KPI) is a measure used to monitor performance against an objective.

Examples:

| Business Objective            | Possible KPI                           |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Reduce customer waiting time  | Average waiting time                   |
| Improve order accuracy        | Percentage of orders shipped correctly |
| Reduce operating costs        | Cost per transaction                   |
| Improve website reliability   | Service availability                   |
| Increase customer retention   | Customer retention rate                |
| Improve processing efficiency | Average processing time                |

A KPI should measure something relevant to the desired business outcome.

### 3.3 Establish Baselines and Targets

A baseline represents the starting level of performance.

A target represents the desired performance level.

Example:

* Baseline average processing time: 10 minutes.
* Target average processing time: 6 minutes.
* Actual average processing time after implementation: 7 minutes.

The solution improved processing time by three minutes but did not achieve the six-minute target.

This difference requires further analysis.

### 3.4 Data Collection

Performance information may come from:

* System logs.
* Operational reports.
* Surveys.
* Financial records.
* User feedback.
* Observations.
* Analytics platforms.
* Support tickets.
* Service-level reports.

Data quality matters. Incomplete, inconsistent, or biased data can produce misleading conclusions.

The analyst should consider whether the data is reliable, representative, and collected under appropriate conditions.

## 4. Analyze Performance Measures

### 4.1 Purpose

Analyzing performance measures involves interpreting the collected data to understand whether the solution is meeting expectations.

The analyst compares actual results against:

* Business objectives.
* Baseline measurements.
* Target values.
* Stakeholder expectations.
* Operational requirements.
* Applicable standards.

### 4.2 Identify Performance Gaps

A performance gap exists when actual performance differs from the desired or expected level.

Example:

A company wants to reduce delivery delays from 15% to 5%.

After implementation, the delay rate decreases to 9%.

The solution produces an improvement, but the target has not been reached.

The analyst should investigate the causes rather than assume that the solution has failed completely.

### 4.3 Investigate Root Causes

Possible reasons for a performance gap include:

* Incorrect assumptions.
* Poor data quality.
* Inadequate training.
* Process bottlenecks.
* System defects.
* Missing capabilities.
* External factors.
* Inappropriate performance targets.
* Low stakeholder adoption.

Root-cause analysis helps distinguish symptoms from underlying causes.

For example, slow order processing may be caused by approval bottlenecks rather than application performance.

### 4.4 Consider Context

Performance can be affected by factors outside the solution itself.

A customer service system may handle normal traffic effectively but experience delays during seasonal peaks.

The analyst should consider the operating conditions when interpreting performance measures.

## 5. Assess Solution Limitations

### 5.1 Definition

Solution limitations are characteristics of a solution that prevent it from fully satisfying business needs or achieving desired outcomes.

Limitations may be related to:

* Functionality.
* Performance.
* Usability.
* Reliability.
* Security.
* Accessibility.
* Scalability.
* Maintainability.
* Integration.
* Data quality.

### 5.2 Example

An inventory management system accurately tracks stock in one warehouse but cannot consolidate stock levels across multiple locations.

The limitation prevents the organization from obtaining a complete view of available inventory.

Potential responses include configuring the system, integrating additional systems, improving data processes, or replacing the solution.

### 5.3 Workarounds

A workaround is an alternative procedure used to overcome a limitation.

Examples include:

* Maintaining a separate spreadsheet.
* Manually reconciling records.
* Asking a supervisor to approve exceptions.
* Re-entering data into another system.

Workarounds may temporarily support operations, but they can introduce errors, extra costs, and control weaknesses.

The analyst should assess their impact and determine whether a more sustainable response is required.

## 6. Assess Enterprise Limitations

### 6.1 Definition

Enterprise limitations are organizational conditions that constrain the ability to achieve desired outcomes.

They differ from limitations inherent in the solution.

Examples include:

* Limited employee skills.
* Insufficient budget.
* Outdated organizational policies.
* Resistance to change.
* Inadequate staffing.
* Weak data governance.
* Unclear responsibilities.
* Organizational culture.
* Limited training capacity.

### 6.2 Solution Limitation vs. Enterprise Limitation

Consider a company that implements a reporting platform.

The platform can generate accurate reports, but managers continue to make decisions using outdated spreadsheets.

The platform may be functioning correctly. The main limitation could be employee adoption, training, or organizational procedures.

Replacing the software might not solve the underlying problem.

### 6.3 Example

A warehouse system provides real-time inventory information, but employees update stock records only at the end of each shift.

The resulting inventory information is outdated.

The problem may require improved procedures, employee training, accountability, and operational controls rather than a different software product.

## 7. Recommend Actions to Increase Solution Value

### 7.1 Purpose

This task identifies actions that may improve the value delivered by a solution.

Possible actions include:

* Fixing defects.
* Improving processes.
* Enhancing functionality.
* Providing training.
* Updating business rules.
* Improving data quality.
* Integrating systems.
* Changing operating procedures.
* Reconfiguring an existing solution.
* Replacing an inadequate solution.

Recommendations should be supported by evidence and assessed against business objectives, cost, risk, feasibility, and expected benefits.

### 7.2 Prioritize Improvement Actions

Not every identified problem requires immediate action.

The analyst may consider:

* Business impact.
* Urgency.
* Implementation cost.
* Risk reduction.
* Expected return.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Dependencies.
* Resource availability.

A critical security issue may require immediate attention, while a minor interface enhancement may be deferred.

### 7.3 Example

A customer support chatbot answers common questions accurately but performs poorly on complex inquiries.

Potential actions include:

1. Improve the knowledge base.
2. Clarify the chatbot's capabilities.
3. Add an escalation path to human agents.
4. Improve training data and evaluation.
5. Monitor resolution rates and customer satisfaction.

The best combination depends on the causes of poor performance and the desired business outcomes.

## 8. Evaluate Solution Performance

### 8.1 Purpose

Evaluating solution performance brings together evidence about solution effectiveness, limitations, and opportunities to increase value.

The analyst determines whether the solution meets business needs and whether further action is necessary.

Evaluation may consider:

* Actual benefits versus expected benefits.
* Performance against targets.
* Stakeholder satisfaction.
* Operational effectiveness.
* Financial outcomes.
* Risk exposure.
* Remaining limitations.
* Opportunities for improvement.

### 8.2 Financial and Non-Financial Value

Solution value is not limited to direct financial returns.

Financial measures may include:

* Cost savings.
* Increased revenue.
* Reduced operational expenses.
* Lower cost per transaction.

Non-financial measures may include:

* Improved customer experience.
* Better compliance.
* Reduced employee frustration.
* Improved accessibility.
* Better decision-making.
* Increased reliability.

A complete evaluation considers the forms of value relevant to the organization and its stakeholders.

### 8.3 Continuous Evaluation

Solution evaluation may continue after initial implementation.

A solution that performs well at launch may become less effective as:

* Customer needs change.
* Transaction volumes increase.
* Regulations evolve.
* Technology becomes outdated.
* Business processes change.
* New competitors emerge.

Ongoing measurement helps organizations determine when additional changes are needed.

## 9. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: Customer Service Platform

A telecommunications company implements a new customer service platform.

The original objectives are:

* Reduce average response time from 12 hours to 4 hours.
* Improve customer satisfaction from 70% to 85%.
* Reduce repeated customer contacts by 30%.

### Performance Results After Three Months

* Average response time: 6 hours.
* Customer satisfaction: 78%.
* Repeated contacts: Reduced by 15%.

The solution improves all three measures but does not achieve any of the original targets.

### Analysis

The analyst investigates performance data and discovers:

* Agents are not consistently using the knowledge base.
* Some customer records are incomplete.
* Complex cases are routed incorrectly.
* Training was limited.
* The platform performs well under normal conditions.

### Solution Limitations

The routing configuration does not support several complex case categories.

### Enterprise Limitations

Employees lack sufficient training, and customer-record maintenance procedures are inconsistent.

### Recommended Actions

1. Improve routing rules.
2. Provide targeted employee training.
3. Establish customer-data quality controls.
4. Improve the knowledge base.
5. Monitor response time and repeat-contact rates monthly.

### Evaluation

The analyst measures performance after the improvements and compares results with the original baselines and targets.

This approach identifies whether the recommended actions increase solution value.

# 09 â€” Business Analysis Techniques

## 1. Module Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain the purpose of business analysis techniques.
* Select appropriate techniques for different situations.
* Understand stakeholder analysis, interviews, workshops, and observation.
* Apply SWOT, PESTLE, and root-cause analysis.
* Understand process modeling, data analysis, and decision analysis.
* Explain prioritization, estimation, and risk analysis techniques.
* Understand the role of metrics, benchmarking, and business cases.

## 2. Introduction

Business analysis techniques are methods used to investigate business needs, understand stakeholders, analyze information, evaluate alternatives, and support decisions.

A Business Analyst selects techniques according to the business context, available information, stakeholder needs, constraints, and expected results.

No single technique is appropriate for every situation. Interviews may help explore individual stakeholder needs, while workshops may be more suitable for resolving disagreements among several stakeholders.

## 3. Stakeholder Analysis

### 3.1 Purpose

Stakeholder analysis identifies individuals, groups, or organizations that influence a change, are affected by it, or have relevant interests in its outcome.

Typical stakeholders include:

* Customers.
* Business sponsors.
* End users.
* Managers.
* Developers.
* Testers.
* Operations teams.
* Suppliers.
* Regulators.
* Security and compliance representatives.

### 3.2 Power-Interest Grid

A power-interest grid classifies stakeholders according to their influence and interest.

| Stakeholder Category      | Typical Approach      |
| ------------------------- | --------------------- |
| High power, high interest | Manage closely        |
| High power, low interest  | Keep satisfied        |
| Low power, high interest  | Keep informed         |
| Low power, low interest   | Monitor appropriately |

This classification is a planning aid, not a substitute for understanding individual stakeholder needs.

### 3.3 Example

A bank is developing a mobile application.

* Executive sponsor: High power, high interest.
* Customer using the application: May have low organizational power but high interest.
* Regulatory authority: High influence over compliance requirements.
* Employee from an unrelated department: May have limited interest and influence.

The analyst should adjust engagement to reflect each stakeholder's responsibilities and concerns.

## 4. Interviews

### 4.1 Definition

An interview is a structured or semi-structured conversation used to collect information from a stakeholder.

Interviews are useful for exploring experiences, expectations, problems, assumptions, and requirements.

### 4.2 Interview Types

**Structured interview:** Uses a predefined set of questions.

**Semi-structured interview:** Uses planned questions with flexibility to explore important responses.

**Unstructured interview:** Uses a flexible conversational approach.

### 4.3 Good Interview Practices

1. Define the interview objective.
2. Select suitable participants.
3. Prepare open-ended questions.
4. Avoid leading questions.
5. Listen actively.
6. Ask follow-up questions.
7. Record important findings appropriately.
8. Confirm understanding.
9. Document and communicate results.

### 4.4 Example Questions

For an inventory-management project:

* How do you currently record incoming stock?
* What problems occur during stock reconciliation?
* Which decisions require information that is difficult to obtain?
* What exceptions occur most frequently?
* What would a successful process look like?

Open-ended questions often reveal problems that predefined multiple-choice questions miss.

## 5. Workshops

A workshop brings stakeholders together to discuss a defined subject and achieve a shared outcome.

Workshops can be used for:

* Requirements elicitation.
* Process improvement.
* Prioritization.
* Conflict resolution.
* Business rule definition.
* Solution evaluation.

### Effective Workshop Structure

1. Define objectives and scope.
2. Identify participants.
3. Prepare an agenda.
4. Establish discussion rules.
5. Facilitate participation.
6. Record decisions and unresolved questions.
7. Assign actions.
8. Distribute results.

A facilitator should encourage balanced participation and prevent one participant from dominating the discussion.

## 6. Observation

Observation involves watching people perform their actual work.

It is useful when stakeholders have difficulty describing a process accurately or when informal practices differ from documented procedures.

### Passive Observation

The analyst observes without directly participating in the activity.

### Active Observation

The analyst participates in the activity to understand its practical details.

### Example

Employees report that invoice processing takes ten minutes. Observation reveals that they spend additional time switching between systems and waiting for approvals.

Observation uncovers operational details that interviews alone may not reveal.

## 7. Document Analysis

Document analysis examines existing information to understand business processes, rules, constraints, and requirements.

Potential sources include:

* Policies.
* Procedures.
* Contracts.
* Reports.
* Existing requirements.
* System manuals.
* Regulations.
* Forms.
* Process documentation.
* Customer complaints.

Documents may be incomplete, outdated, contradictory, or inconsistent with actual practice. Findings should therefore be validated with relevant stakeholders.

## 8. Brainstorming

Brainstorming generates a range of ideas or possible solutions.

A useful brainstorming session separates idea generation from evaluation.

Participants first produce ideas without prematurely rejecting them. The ideas are then clarified, grouped, assessed, and prioritized.

Brainstorming supports creativity but does not independently establish whether an idea is feasible or valuable.

## 9. SWOT Analysis

SWOT examines internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats.

| Category      | Meaning                       | Example               |
| ------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Strengths     | Internal advantages           | Experienced staff     |
| Weaknesses    | Internal disadvantages        | Manual reporting      |
| Opportunities | External favorable conditions | Growing market demand |
| Threats       | External risks                | New competitors       |

SWOT helps organizations understand their strategic position and identify areas requiring investigation.

## 10. PESTLE Analysis

PESTLE examines external environmental factors.

* **Political:** Government priorities and political stability.
* **Economic:** Inflation, interest rates, and economic growth.
* **Social:** Demographics, customer behavior, and social expectations.
* **Technological:** Emerging technology and infrastructure.
* **Legal:** Laws, regulations, and contractual obligations.
* **Environmental:** Climate, sustainability, and environmental requirements.

Example: A logistics company evaluating expansion may use PESTLE to understand fuel prices, transportation regulations, technology adoption, and environmental restrictions.

## 11. Root-Cause Analysis

Root-cause analysis investigates the underlying causes of a problem rather than treating only its symptoms.

### Five Whys

The Five Whys technique repeatedly asks why a problem occurred.

Example:

**Problem:** Customer orders are frequently delayed.

1. Why? Orders wait for inventory confirmation.
2. Why? Inventory records are often outdated.
3. Why? Stock updates are entered manually.
4. Why? Warehouse systems are not integrated.
5. Why? Integration was excluded from the original system design.

The investigation suggests a potential underlying cause. Further evidence is needed before deciding on the final solution.

### Fishbone Diagram

A fishbone, or cause-and-effect, diagram organizes possible causes into categories.

Common categories include people, processes, technology, materials, measurement, and environment.

It helps teams investigate a problem systematically without assuming that the first explanation is correct.

## 12. Process Modeling

Process modeling represents activities, decisions, events, and information flows.

Common approaches include flowcharts and Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN).

Process models can help analysts:

* Understand existing workflows.
* Identify bottlenecks.
* Clarify responsibilities.
* Discover redundant activities.
* Document exceptions.
* Compare current and future processes.

A model should be detailed enough for its intended audience without introducing unnecessary complexity.

## 13. Data Analysis

Data analysis examines information to identify patterns, trends, relationships, and performance problems.

Examples include:

* Descriptive statistics.
* Trend analysis.
* Segmentation.
* Frequency analysis.
* Correlation analysis.
* Outlier detection.
* KPI comparisons.

Example: A retailer analyzes order cancellations by product category and delivery region to identify where operational improvements may have the greatest impact.

Correlation does not necessarily establish causation. Additional investigation may be required to understand why a pattern occurs.

## 14. Decision Analysis

Decision analysis compares alternatives using defined criteria.

Criteria may include:

* Cost.
* Expected benefits.
* Risk.
* Time.
* Feasibility.
* Compliance.
* Customer impact.

A weighted decision matrix assigns importance weights to criteria and scores alternatives against them.

The scores help structure a decision but should not replace informed judgment or stakeholder approval.

## 15. Prioritization Techniques

Common approaches include:

**MoSCoW:** Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have this time.

**Ranking:** Orders requirements from highest to lowest priority.

**Timeboxing:** Limits the time allocated to an activity or delivery period.

**Weighted scoring:** Evaluates alternatives against weighted criteria.

**Value versus effort:** Compares expected benefits with implementation effort.

Prioritization should reflect business objectives, constraints, dependencies, and stakeholder needs.

## 16. Estimation and Risk Analysis

### Estimation

Estimation predicts the effort, cost, duration, or resources required for an activity.

Techniques include expert judgment, analogous estimation, and parametric estimation.

Estimates involve uncertainty and should be refined as additional information becomes available.

### Risk Analysis

Risk analysis considers the likelihood and consequences of uncertain events.

A qualitative risk matrix may classify risks by likelihood and impact.

A high-impact risk with a high likelihood usually requires more attention than a low-impact, low-likelihood risk, subject to organizational risk policies.

## 17. Benchmarking and Metrics

Benchmarking compares performance against a reference point, such as:

* Historical performance.
* Internal departments.
* Industry standards.
* Competitors, where reliable information is available.
* Recognized best practices.

Metrics provide evidence for evaluating performance.

For example, a support department may compare its average resolution time with its previous quarter and an appropriate industry benchmark.

Comparisons should account for differences in operating conditions and measurement definitions.

## 18. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: Improving a Warehouse

A distribution company experiences frequent picking errors and delayed shipments.

The Business Analyst:

1. Interviews warehouse employees.
2. Observes the picking process.
3. Reviews error reports and operating procedures.
4. Maps the current process.
5. Uses root-cause analysis to investigate errors.
6. Analyzes errors by product and shift.
7. Conducts a workshop to generate improvements.
8. Prioritizes changes by value, effort, and risk.
9. Defines performance measures.
10. Evaluates results after implementation.

Potential recommendations might include barcode scanning, improved product labeling, employee training, or process redesign.

The final recommendation should follow evidence-based analysis rather than an assumption that technology alone will solve the problem.

# 10 â€” Business Analysis Perspectives

## 1. Module Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain the meaning of a business analysis perspective.
* Understand how context affects business analysis work.
* Distinguish common business analysis perspectives.
* Understand Agile, Business Intelligence, Information Technology, Business Architecture, and Business Process Management perspectives.
* Identify how techniques and deliverables vary across contexts.
* Apply perspective-based thinking to business scenarios.

## 2. Introduction

A business analysis perspective is a particular way of viewing business analysis work based on the context in which it is performed.

The business need, organizational environment, delivery approach, stakeholders, and solution type influence how an analyst works.

For example, a Business Analyst working on a data warehouse may focus on data quality, integration, reporting, and analytical needs. An analyst working on an Agile software team may focus on iterative delivery, backlog refinement, user stories, and frequent stakeholder feedback.

The underlying goal remains consistent: understand needs, facilitate change, and help deliver value.

The BABOK Guide describes several perspectives that provide specialized context for business analysis:

1. Agile.
2. Business Intelligence.
3. Information Technology.
4. Business Architecture.
5. Business Process Management.

These perspectives are not mutually exclusive. One initiative may involve several of them.

## 3. Agile Perspective

### 3.1 Definition

Agile is an approach to delivering value through iterative or incremental work, collaboration, feedback, and adaptation.

In an Agile environment, requirements may evolve as stakeholders learn more about the business problem and evaluate working solutions.

The Business Analyst helps the team understand needs, clarify requirements, manage priorities, and maintain alignment with business objectives.

### 3.2 Common Characteristics

* Frequent stakeholder collaboration.
* Incremental delivery.
* Iterative refinement of requirements.
* Continuous feedback.
* Adaptation to changing needs.
* Emphasis on delivering value.
* Close cooperation between business and delivery teams.

Agile does not mean working without planning or documentation. The appropriate level of documentation depends on the context.

### 3.3 Common Techniques

* User stories.
* Product backlog refinement.
* User story mapping.
* Workshops.
* Prototypes.
* Acceptance criteria.
* Iteration reviews.
* Retrospectives.
* Prioritization.

### 3.4 User Stories

A user story commonly describes a capability from a user's perspective.

Format:

"As a [user], I want [capability], so that [benefit]."

Example:

"As a customer, I want to track my order so that I know when it will arrive."

Acceptance criteria clarify the conditions that must be satisfied.

For example:

* The customer can access the current order status.
* The displayed status reflects the latest available information.
* The customer receives an understandable message when tracking information is unavailable.

### 3.5 Backlog Prioritization

A product backlog contains work items that may be considered for delivery.

Prioritization may consider:

* Business value.
* Customer impact.
* Risk.
* Dependencies.
* Urgency.
* Effort.
* Learning opportunities.

The person accountable for product value, often the Product Owner in Scrum, is responsible for effective product backlog management. The Business Analyst may support this work depending on the organization's roles.

### 3.6 Example

A company develops a mobile banking application.

Instead of attempting to deliver every feature at once, the team releases an initial version supporting secure login and balance viewing.

Customer feedback then informs the prioritization of transfers, notifications, and additional functionality.

The analyst helps clarify requirements and assess feedback as the product evolves.

## 4. Business Intelligence Perspective

### 4.1 Definition

Business Intelligence (BI) focuses on transforming data into information that supports reporting, analysis, monitoring, and business decision-making.

Business analysis in a BI context often involves understanding data sources, information needs, metrics, reporting requirements, and analytical capabilities.

### 4.2 Common Activities

* Identifying decision-making needs.
* Defining KPIs.
* Analyzing existing reports.
* Documenting data requirements.
* Assessing data quality.
* Understanding data sources.
* Defining reporting requirements.
* Supporting dashboard design.
* Validating analytical outputs.

### 4.3 Data Quality Dimensions

Useful data quality dimensions include:

**Accuracy:** Does the data correctly represent what it is intended to represent?

**Completeness:** Are required values present?

**Consistency:** Is information represented consistently across systems?

**Timeliness:** Is the data available when needed?

**Validity:** Does the data conform to defined rules and formats?

**Uniqueness:** Are duplicate records appropriately controlled?

### 4.4 Example

A retail manager wants to understand declining sales.

The analyst identifies relevant measures, including revenue, units sold, returns, discounts, customer segments, and product categories.

The analyst then investigates data sources, defines metric calculations, and documents reporting requirements.

A dashboard may reveal that the decline is concentrated in specific product categories or customer segments.

### 4.5 Reporting Requirements

A useful reporting requirement specifies:

* Intended audience.
* Business question.
* Required measures.
* Data sources.
* Filters and dimensions.
* Reporting frequency.
* Time period.
* Access restrictions.
* Accuracy and timeliness expectations.

Weak requirement: "Create a sales dashboard."

Improved requirement: "Provide regional sales managers with weekly revenue, order volume, and return-rate measures, filterable by region and product category."

## 5. Information Technology Perspective

### 5.1 Definition

The Information Technology (IT) perspective addresses business analysis in technology-related initiatives.

These initiatives may involve application development, infrastructure, system integration, cloud migration, cybersecurity controls, or technology replacement.

The analyst connects business needs with technical possibilities and constraints.

### 5.2 Common Activities

* Identifying business and technical requirements.
* Understanding system interfaces.
* Documenting functional and non-functional requirements.
* Analyzing data flows.
* Identifying integration dependencies.
* Supporting solution evaluation.
* Clarifying operational requirements.
* Coordinating business and technical stakeholders.

### 5.3 Functional Requirements

Functional requirements describe what a solution must do.

Examples:

* Users can reset their passwords.
* The system generates monthly invoices.
* Managers can approve purchase requests.
* The application records completed transactions.

### 5.4 Non-Functional Requirements

Non-functional requirements describe qualities, constraints, or performance expectations.

Examples include:

* Performance.
* Availability.
* Security.
* Accessibility.
* Reliability.
* Maintainability.
* Scalability.
* Usability.

Example:

"The service shall maintain 99.9% monthly availability, measured according to the agreed monitoring and exclusion rules."

Non-functional requirements should be defined in measurable terms whenever appropriate.

### 5.5 System Integration

System integration connects applications, services, or data sources.

An analyst may need to identify:

* Source and destination systems.
* Data exchanged.
* Transfer frequency.
* Error handling.
* Authentication and authorization.
* Data transformation rules.
* Ownership of information.
* Monitoring requirements.

### 5.6 Example

A company moves an application to a cloud platform.

The analyst documents business continuity requirements, user access needs, data migration requirements, availability expectations, and integration dependencies.

Technical specialists determine implementation details while the analyst helps maintain alignment with business needs.

## 6. Business Architecture Perspective

### 6.1 Definition

Business Architecture provides a structured view of an organization and how its parts work together to deliver value.

It helps connect strategy with business capabilities, value streams, information, organizational structure, and initiatives.

### 6.2 Common Elements

**Business capabilities:** What the organization must be able to do.

**Value streams:** How value is created and delivered through a sequence of activities.

**Organizational structure:** How responsibilities and authority are arranged.

**Information concepts:** Important business information and relationships.

**Business strategy:** The organization's objectives and direction.

### 6.3 Capability Analysis

Capability analysis identifies what an organization can do today and what it must be able to do in the future.

Example: A logistics company may have capabilities in:

* Order management.
* Transportation planning.
* Warehouse operations.
* Shipment tracking.
* Customer service.
* Billing.

A strategic objective to provide real-time shipment visibility may require improvements in tracking, data integration, and customer communication capabilities.

### 6.4 Capability Maps

A capability map organizes an organization's capabilities into a structured view.

It can help stakeholders identify:

* Weak capabilities.
* Duplicate capabilities.
* Investment priorities.
* Strategic dependencies.
* Areas for improvement.

A capability map describes what the organization needs to do rather than necessarily specifying the detailed sequence of activities.

### 6.5 Example

A bank wants to expand its digital services.

Its strategy requires stronger digital identity verification, payment processing, fraud detection, and customer support capabilities.

Business architecture helps connect the strategic objective with these capabilities and identify which initiatives are needed.

## 7. Business Process Management Perspective

### 7.1 Definition

Business Process Management (BPM) focuses on understanding, analyzing, improving, and managing business processes.

A business process is a set of related activities that produces an outcome for a customer or another stakeholder.

BPM considers process performance, consistency, efficiency, quality, and alignment with organizational goals.

### 7.2 Process Lifecycle

A typical process improvement cycle may include:

1. Identify the process and its objectives.
2. Document the current process.
3. Measure performance.
4. Analyze bottlenecks and causes.
5. Design the improved process.
6. Implement the changes.
7. Monitor results.
8. Refine the process when necessary.

This is a practical lifecycle model; organizations may use different frameworks.

### 7.3 Common Techniques

* Process mapping.
* BPMN.
* Value stream mapping.
* Root-cause analysis.
* Process simulation.
* Performance measurement.
* Gap analysis.
* Workflow analysis.

### 7.4 Process Metrics

Common measures include:

* Cycle time.
* Throughput.
* Error rate.
* Cost per transaction.
* First-pass yield.
* Rework rate.
* Waiting time.
* Customer satisfaction.

### 7.5 Example

A company processes supplier invoices manually.

The analyst maps the process and discovers duplicate data entry, unclear approval responsibilities, and delays caused by missing information.

Potential improvements include standardized invoice submission, automated validation, clearer approval rules, and exception handling.

The analyst compares performance before and after implementation.

## 8. Comparing the Five Perspectives

| Perspective                 | Primary Focus                                       | Example Deliverable                           |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Agile                       | Iterative delivery and evolving requirements        | User stories and acceptance criteria          |
| Business Intelligence       | Data, reporting, and decision support               | KPI definitions and dashboard requirements    |
| Information Technology      | Technology-enabled change                           | Functional and non-functional requirements    |
| Business Architecture       | Strategic capabilities and organizational structure | Capability map                                |
| Business Process Management | Process performance and improvement                 | Current-state and future-state process models |

The deliverables shown are examples, not exclusive outputs. A single perspective may use many techniques and deliverables.

## 9. Combining Perspectives

Real-world initiatives often involve multiple perspectives.

Consider a manufacturer introducing predictive maintenance.

**Business Architecture:** Identifies the capability needed to improve equipment reliability.

**Business Process Management:** Examines the maintenance workflow and current sources of delay.

**Business Intelligence:** Defines equipment-performance measures and reporting requirements.

**Information Technology:** Defines sensor integration, data storage, security, and application requirements.

**Agile:** Supports iterative delivery and feedback as the solution is developed.

The analyst selects techniques according to the needs of the initiative rather than treating perspectives as isolated disciplines.

## 1. Purpose

## 2. Business Analysis Fundamentals

### 2.1 What Is Business Analysis?

Business analysis enables change in an enterprise by defining needs and recommending solutions that deliver value to stakeholders.

A Business Analyst investigates problems, identifies opportunities, facilitates communication, analyzes requirements, and supports decisions.

Business analysis is not limited to software development. It can support operational improvement, organizational change, strategic planning, compliance, and technology initiatives.

### 2.2 Essential Terms

**Business need:** A problem or opportunity that motivates change.

**Stakeholder:** An individual, group, or organization with a relationship to the change, its needs, or its outcomes.

**Requirement:** A usable representation of a need.

**Design:** A representation of a solution.

**Solution:** A specific way of satisfying one or more needs within a context.

**Value:** The worth, importance, or usefulness of something to a stakeholder in a context.

**Context:** The circumstances influencing a change, including organizational, technological, regulatory, and environmental factors.

**Change:** The transformation of an enterprise in response to a need.

### 2.3 Example

A delivery company experiences frequent shipment delays.

* Business need: Improve delivery reliability.
* Stakeholders: Customers, dispatchers, drivers, managers, and suppliers.
* Requirement: Customers need access to current shipment status.
* Design: A tracking interface integrated with shipment data.
* Solution: The implemented tracking capability and associated processes.
* Value: Better customer visibility and potentially fewer status inquiries.

The terms are related but are not interchangeable.

## 3. Core Concepts Review

### 3.1 Need, Requirement, and Design

A need explains why change may be necessary.

A requirement describes something needed to address the need.

A design represents a possible way to satisfy requirements.

Example:

* Need: Reduce fraudulent transactions.
* Requirement: Suspicious transactions must be flagged for review.
* Design: A transaction-monitoring component evaluates defined risk indicators.

### 3.2 Stakeholders

Stakeholders may have different goals, authority, expectations, and concerns.

The analyst identifies relevant stakeholders and supports communication and decision-making.

Stakeholder disagreement should be investigated rather than automatically resolved in favor of the most vocal participant.

### 3.3 Value

Value depends on stakeholder priorities and context.

It may include:

* Revenue growth.
* Cost reduction.
* Better customer experience.
* Improved safety.
* Regulatory compliance.
* Reduced risk.
* Better information.
* Operational reliability.

A solution should be evaluated against the outcomes it is intended to achieve.

## 4. Planning and Monitoring Review

Business analysis planning establishes how the analysis work will be performed.

Monitoring examines progress and whether the approach remains appropriate.

Governance establishes decision-making responsibilities, authority, and controls.

Information management addresses how business analysis information is organized, stored, maintained, accessed, and communicated.

Stakeholder engagement planning determines how stakeholders will participate and receive information.

### Key Distinction

A project plan and a business analysis approach are related but different.

A project plan typically coordinates the overall initiative, including schedule, resources, and delivery activities.

A business analysis approach explains how the analysis work will be performed in its context.

## 5. Elicitation and Collaboration Review

Elicitation is the work of drawing out, discovering, and confirming information relevant to business analysis.

Collaboration involves working with stakeholders and other participants to develop shared understanding and achieve outcomes.

Common elicitation techniques include:

* Interviews.
* Workshops.
* Observation.
* Document analysis.
* Surveys.
* Brainstorming.
* Prototyping.

### Confirming Results

Elicitation does not end when a meeting finishes.

The analyst may need to document findings, confirm understanding, resolve inconsistencies, and follow up on unanswered questions.

### Example

An interview reveals that employees regularly correct customer addresses manually.

The analyst should investigate the cause, determine the business impact, and confirm whether the issue is related to data quality, process design, or system functionality.

The analyst should not immediately assume that a new application is required.

## 6. Requirements Life Cycle Management Review

The five tasks are:

1. Trace Requirements.
2. Maintain Requirements.
3. Prioritize Requirements.
4. Assess Requirements Changes.
5. Approve Requirements.

### Common Exam Distinctions

**Traceability:** Establishes relationships between requirements and related information.

**Maintenance:** Keeps requirements and designs accurate, usable, and relevant.

**Prioritization:** Determines relative importance.

**Change assessment:** Evaluates the implications of a proposed change.

**Approval:** Establishes acceptance by the appropriate authority.

### Example

A stakeholder requests a new reporting feature shortly before a release.

The analyst investigates the reason for the request, identifies affected requirements, assesses cost and schedule impacts, and presents the findings to the appropriate decision-makers.

The analyst should not confuse assessing a change with approving it.

## 7. Strategy Analysis Review

Strategy analysis connects the business need with the current state, desired future state, risks, and change strategy.

### Essential Questions

* What problem or opportunity exists?
* What is happening today?
* What outcome is desired?
* What gaps prevent that outcome?
* What risks could affect the change?
* What approach is most suitable?

### Example

A warehouse experiences a high stockout rate.

The current-state analysis identifies inaccurate inventory records.

The future state defines an acceptable inventory accuracy target.

Gap analysis identifies missing data controls and process weaknesses.

Risk assessment examines migration, operational disruption, and adoption risks.

The change strategy compares process improvements, system integration, training, and alternative solutions.

## 8. Requirements Analysis and Design Definition Review

The six tasks are:

1. Specify and Model Requirements.
2. Verify Requirements.
3. Validate Requirements.
4. Define Requirements Architecture.
5. Define Design Options.
6. Analyze Potential Value and Recommend Solution.

### Verification vs. Validation

Verification asks whether requirements and designs are appropriately expressed.

Validation asks whether they address the underlying business need.

### Example

Requirement: "The system should respond quickly."

Verification identifies ambiguity.

After clarification, the requirement specifies an agreed response-time threshold.

Validation then examines whether that threshold is appropriate for the intended users and business objective.

Both activities contribute to requirement quality and relevance.

## 9. Solution Evaluation Review

Solution evaluation assesses actual performance, limitations, and opportunities to improve value.

Important concepts include:

* Performance measures.
* Baselines.
* Targets.
* Actual results.
* Solution limitations.
* Enterprise limitations.
* Improvement recommendations.
* Financial and non-financial value.

### Example

A company introduces automated invoice processing.

The system functions correctly, but invoices still experience delays because managers approve them infrequently.

The analyst should investigate organizational workflow and approval constraints rather than automatically assuming that the software is defective.

## 1. Module Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain the characteristics of quality requirements.
* Identify ambiguity, inconsistency, and incompleteness.
* Understand requirements verification and validation.
* Explain requirements traceability and relationships.
* Understand requirement versioning and status management.
* Apply quality and traceability concepts to practical scenarios.

## 2. Introduction

Requirements provide a shared understanding of what stakeholders need from a change. Poor requirements can cause rework, delays, incorrect implementations, and failure to deliver expected business value.

Requirements quality concerns whether requirements are expressed in a way that supports understanding, analysis, implementation, and evaluation.

Traceability concerns the relationships between requirements and other relevant information.

Together, quality management and traceability help teams understand what must be delivered, why it is needed, how it relates to other elements, and whether the intended outcomes have been achieved.

## 3. Characteristics of Quality Requirements

Requirements should be suitable for their intended purpose and audience.

Important characteristics include:

### 3.1 Clear

A requirement should have an understandable meaning.

Poor: "The system should process requests quickly."

Improved: "The system shall display the request confirmation within two seconds for at least 95% of eligible requests under the agreed normal operating load."

The improved requirement provides a measurable expectation. The actual threshold should be agreed with stakeholders.

### 3.2 Complete

A requirement should contain the information necessary for its intended use.

Poor: "The system shall notify customers."

Questions remain: Which customers? Under what conditions? Through which channel? What information must be included?

Completeness is assessed in context. A requirement need not contain every implementation detail, but missing information must not prevent correct interpretation or use.

### 3.3 Consistent

Requirements should not contradict one another.

Example:

* Requirement A: Customers can cancel orders until shipment dispatch.
* Requirement B: Customers cannot cancel orders after payment.

If payment normally occurs before dispatch, these statements may conflict. The analyst must clarify the intended business rules.

### 3.4 Testable

A requirement should support a method for determining whether it has been satisfied.

Poor: "The application shall be user-friendly."

Improved: "At least 90% of representative first-time users shall complete the defined checkout task without assistance during the agreed usability test."

Testability depends on an agreed measurement method and acceptance criteria.

### 3.5 Feasible

A requirement should be achievable within applicable technical, financial, legal, operational, and time constraints.

A requirement that exceeds available infrastructure or budget may require negotiation, alternative designs, or a change in scope.

### 3.6 Necessary

A requirement should contribute to a genuine need, objective, obligation, or justified constraint.

Unnecessary requirements can increase costs and complexity without delivering meaningful value.

### 3.7 Traceable

A requirement should be capable of being linked to relevant sources, objectives, designs, tests, and other artifacts when appropriate.

Traceability supports change assessment and demonstrates why a requirement exists.

### 3.8 Unambiguous

A requirement should not reasonably support multiple conflicting interpretations.

Words such as "fast," "simple," "appropriate," and "efficient" may need clarification.

## 4. Requirements Verification

Verification examines whether requirements and designs are expressed appropriately and meet relevant quality standards.

Verification questions include:

* Is the requirement clear?
* Is it consistent with related requirements?
* Is it sufficiently complete?
* Can it be tested or otherwise assessed?
* Is the terminology used consistently?
* Does it follow applicable documentation standards?

### Example

Requirement: "The system must respond quickly."

A reviewer identifies that "quickly" is subjective.

The requirement is revised to specify a response-time threshold, workload conditions, and measurement method.

This improves its quality and testability.

## 5. Requirements Validation

Validation examines whether requirements and designs address the actual business need and contribute to desired outcomes.

Validation questions include:

* Does this requirement support a business objective?
* Does it address the stakeholder's actual need?
* Is the proposed capability appropriate?
* Does it create unnecessary complexity?
* Will satisfying it contribute to the desired future state?

### Example

A company asks for a sophisticated dashboard.

Investigation reveals that managers primarily need a daily exception report to identify delayed orders.

A simpler reporting capability may satisfy the business need more effectively.

The distinction is important: a requirement can be written perfectly yet still be the wrong requirement.

## 6. Verification Versus Validation

| Aspect           | Verification                        | Validation                                               |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Main concern     | Quality of requirements and designs | Relevance to business needs                              |
| Typical question | Is it expressed correctly?          | Is it the right thing to address the need?               |
| Example          | Clarifying an ambiguous threshold   | Confirming the threshold supports the business objective |
| Potential result | Improved requirement quality        | Revised scope or requirements                            |

Both activities are important and may occur iteratively throughout an initiative.

## 7. Requirements Traceability

### 7.1 Definition

Traceability establishes and maintains relationships between requirements and relevant elements.

Depending on the initiative, these relationships may connect requirements to:

* Business objectives.
* Stakeholder needs.
* Sources.
* Other requirements.
* Designs.
* Development work items.
* Test cases.
* Solution components.
* Business rules.
* Delivered capabilities.

### 7.2 Forward Traceability

Forward traceability follows a requirement toward related downstream elements.

Example:

Business requirement â†’ Detailed requirement â†’ Design element â†’ Test case.

It can help determine whether requirements have been addressed during delivery.

### 7.3 Backward Traceability

Backward traceability follows an element back to its source or justification.

Example:

Test case â†’ Requirement â†’ Stakeholder need â†’ Business objective.

It can help determine why a particular feature or test exists.

### 7.4 Bidirectional Traceability

Bidirectional traceability supports tracing in both directions.

It helps analysts understand both the origins and downstream implications of requirements.

The precise terminology and implementation depend on the organization's traceability approach.

## 8. Traceability Matrix

A traceability matrix records relationships between relevant elements.

Example:

| Requirement ID | Business Objective | Design  | Test Case | Status       |
| -------------- | ------------------ | ------- | --------- | ------------ |
| REQ-001        | OBJ-01             | DES-001 | TC-001    | Approved     |
| REQ-002        | OBJ-01             | DES-002 | TC-002    | In progress  |
| REQ-003        | OBJ-02             | DES-003 | TC-003    | Under review |

The matrix can reveal missing relationships.

For example, an approved requirement without an associated test or evaluation method may require investigation, depending on the requirement type.

A matrix is only useful when its information is accurate and maintained.

## 9. Requirements Versioning

Versioning records changes to requirements over time.

It helps teams understand:

* What changed.
* When it changed.
* Why it changed.
* Who authorized or made the change.
* Which version is current.
* Which related elements may be affected.

Example:

* Version 1.0: Password must contain at least eight characters.
* Version 1.1: Password must contain at least twelve characters.
* Change reason: Updated security policy.

The team should identify affected designs, tests, user guidance, and other dependent elements.

## 10. Requirements Status Management

A requirement may have a status such as:

* Proposed.
* Under review.
* Approved.
* In progress.
* Implemented.
* Verified.
* Rejected.
* Deferred.

These labels are illustrative. Organizations may define different status models.

Status indicates the requirement's current management state; it does not automatically indicate its priority or business value.

A high-priority requirement may still be under review.

## 11. Impact Analysis

Impact analysis examines the consequences of a proposed change.

Traceability helps identify affected requirements, designs, tests, processes, stakeholders, and solution components.

### Example

A company changes the definition of an eligible customer.

The analyst investigates the effects on:

1. Customer eligibility rules.
2. Registration requirements.
3. Existing customer records.
4. Reports.
5. Automated tests.
6. Training materials.
7. Regulatory controls.

This reduces the likelihood of inconsistent changes.

## 12. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: Online Ordering Platform

A retailer wants to introduce order cancellation.

The analyst documents the business need, identifies stakeholder requirements, and creates relationships between requirements and business objectives.

A requirement states:

"Customers can cancel eligible orders before dispatch."

During verification, the team identifies ambiguity around the term "eligible."

During validation, stakeholders confirm that the cancellation capability must reduce customer support requests without creating unnecessary warehouse disruption.

The analyst clarifies the eligibility rules and links them to design elements and test cases.

Later, the business changes its cancellation policy.

Traceability helps identify affected requirements, application behavior, customer communications, and tests.

The team assesses the impact before implementing the change.

# 14 â€” ECBA Stakeholder Engagement and Communication

## 1. Module Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain stakeholder engagement.
* Identify stakeholder roles and concerns.
* Understand communication planning.
* Select appropriate communication methods.
* Manage disagreements and expectations.
* Recognize barriers to effective collaboration.
* Apply stakeholder engagement techniques to business scenarios.

## 2. Introduction

Stakeholders influence business needs, requirements, decisions, implementation, and outcomes.

Effective stakeholder engagement helps establish shared understanding, gather reliable information, resolve conflicts, and maintain alignment throughout a change initiative.

Communication is not simply sending information. It also involves confirming understanding, receiving feedback, addressing concerns, and adapting messages to different audiences.

A technically successful solution may still fail if relevant stakeholders do not understand it, support it, or use it appropriately.

## 3. Identifying Stakeholders

Stakeholders may include:

* Business sponsors.
* Customers.
* End users.
* Business managers.
* Subject-matter experts.
* Business Analysts.
* Project managers.
* Developers.
* Testers.
* Operations teams.
* Vendors.
* Regulators.
* Compliance representatives.

The relevant stakeholders depend on the nature and scope of the change.

### 3.1 Stakeholder Identification Questions

Ask:

1. Who experiences the problem?
2. Who benefits from the change?
3. Who makes decisions?
4. Who supplies relevant information?
5. Who implements the solution?
6. Who operates or maintains the solution?
7. Who may be negatively affected?
8. Who establishes applicable policies or regulations?

Stakeholder identification should be revisited when the initiative or its context changes.

## 4. Stakeholder Roles and Responsibilities

Different stakeholders contribute different perspectives.

### Business Sponsor

Typically supports the initiative, helps establish business objectives, and may provide funding or organizational authority.

### End User

Uses the solution or participates in the affected business process.

### Subject-Matter Expert

Provides specialized knowledge about a business domain, process, rule, or technical subject.

### Business Analyst

Investigates needs, facilitates analysis, communicates findings, and supports requirements and solution evaluation.

### Project Manager

Coordinates project delivery activities, which may include schedule, resources, risks, and dependencies.

### Developer or Technical Specialist

Helps determine how technical requirements and solution designs can be implemented.

Roles may overlap, and responsibilities vary by organization.

## 5. Stakeholder Analysis

Stakeholder analysis examines stakeholder characteristics relevant to engagement.

Consider:

* Authority.
* Influence.
* Interest.
* Knowledge.
* Expectations.
* Concerns.
* Attitudes toward change.
* Availability.
* Communication preferences.

### Power-Interest Grid

| Category                  | Typical Engagement    |
| ------------------------- | --------------------- |
| High power, high interest | Manage closely        |
| High power, low interest  | Keep satisfied        |
| Low power, high interest  | Keep informed         |
| Low power, low interest   | Monitor appropriately |

This is a useful planning model, not a rigid rule. Regulatory obligations, accessibility needs, and the impact of the change may require additional engagement regardless of a stakeholder's apparent power.

## 6. Communication Planning

A communication plan identifies how relevant information will be shared with stakeholders.

It may specify:

* Audience.
* Purpose.
* Message.
* Communication method.
* Frequency.
* Timing.
* Owner.
* Required feedback.
* Recordkeeping requirements.

### Example

A company introduces a new inventory platform.

| Audience        | Information               | Method                       | Frequency      |
| --------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------- |
| Executives      | Progress, risks, benefits | Summary report               | Monthly        |
| Warehouse staff | Workflow changes          | Workshops and demonstrations | During rollout |
| Technical team  | Interfaces and defects    | Technical meetings           | As needed      |
| Customers       | Changes to order tracking | Email or website notice      | Before launch  |

The communication approach should be adapted to the audience and context.

## 7. Communication Methods

### Written Communication

Examples include reports, emails, requirements documents, meeting notes, and policies.

Advantages:

* Provides a record.
* Supports detailed information.
* Enables later review.
* Helps communicate decisions consistently.

Limitations:

* May be misunderstood.
* Can be ignored or overlooked.
* Does not automatically confirm understanding.

### Verbal Communication

Examples include interviews, calls, and discussions.

Advantages:

* Allows immediate clarification.
* Supports follow-up questions.
* Helps explore complex topics.

Limitations:

* Important information may be forgotten.
* Different participants may remember discussions differently.
* Decisions may need written confirmation.

### Visual Communication

Examples include process models, diagrams, prototypes, dashboards, and presentations.

Visual methods help stakeholders understand relationships, workflows, and proposed changes.

They should be accompanied by explanations when necessary.

### Collaborative Communication

Workshops and facilitated sessions allow stakeholders to exchange perspectives, identify disagreements, and develop shared understanding.

## 8. Active Listening

Active listening involves concentrating on what a stakeholder communicates and checking that the intended meaning has been understood.

Useful practices include:

* Avoiding unnecessary interruptions.
* Asking open-ended questions.
* Paraphrasing important statements.
* Clarifying unfamiliar terminology.
* Asking follow-up questions.
* Separating facts from assumptions.
* Confirming conclusions.

### Example

Stakeholder: "The system is too slow."

Analyst: "Which activities take longer than expected, and under what conditions?"

The follow-up question converts a general complaint into information that can be investigated.

## 9. Managing Stakeholder Expectations

Expectations may concern:

* Scope.
* Schedule.
* Cost.
* Functionality.
* Performance.
* Benefits.
* Responsibilities.
* Change impacts.

The analyst should communicate what is known, what remains uncertain, and which decisions are still pending.

Unrealistic expectations should be addressed through evidence, clear constraints, and appropriate decision-making processes.

The analyst should not promise benefits or delivery dates that have not been established.

## 10. Conflict Management

Stakeholders may disagree because of competing priorities, different information, resource limitations, or incompatible objectives.

### Common Sources of Conflict

* Budget constraints.
* Different interpretations of requirements.
* Conflicting business objectives.
* Competing deadlines.
* Different risk tolerances.
* Organizational politics.
* Unclear responsibilities.

### Effective Response

1. Clarify the disagreement.
2. Identify the underlying interests.
3. Separate evidence from assumptions.
4. Document relevant constraints.
5. Compare possible alternatives.
6. Facilitate discussion.
7. Escalate decisions when required authority is unavailable.
8. Communicate the decision and its rationale.

The analyst should not automatically favor the most senior or most vocal stakeholder.

## 11. Stakeholder Resistance

Resistance may arise from:

* Fear of losing responsibilities.
* Insufficient training.
* Poorly communicated changes.
* Previous negative experiences.
* Increased workload.
* Lack of trust.
* Concerns about job security.
* Disagreement with the proposed approach.

Resistance may reveal important risks or legitimate concerns.

The analyst should investigate the reasons and involve appropriate stakeholders rather than dismissing all resistance as uncooperative behavior.

## 12. Collaboration Tools

Common collaboration tools include:

* Shared documents.
* Requirements repositories.
* Issue trackers.
* Video meetings.
* Messaging platforms.
* Collaborative whiteboards.
* Process modeling tools.
* Version-control systems.

Tool selection should consider accessibility, confidentiality, security, usability, organizational policies, and stakeholder needs.

A tool does not replace clear communication or accountable decision-making.

## 13. Meeting Facilitation

Effective meetings require preparation and clear objectives.

### Before the Meeting

* Define the purpose.
* Identify participants.
* Prepare an agenda.
* Distribute relevant information.
* Identify decisions required.

### During the Meeting

* Keep discussion focused.
* Encourage balanced participation.
* Clarify terminology.
* Record decisions.
* Identify unresolved issues.
* Assign actions and owners.

### After the Meeting

* Distribute notes or outcomes.
* Confirm decisions and action items.
* Follow up on unresolved questions.
* Update relevant requirements or records.

## 14. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: New Hospital Appointment System

A hospital introduces a new appointment-management system.

Stakeholders include doctors, receptionists, patients, administrators, and IT support.

Doctors want efficient schedules. Receptionists need clear booking and cancellation procedures. Patients need accessible appointment information. Administrators want reliable reporting.

The analyst identifies the stakeholders, assesses their concerns, and develops a communication plan.

Interviews reveal that patients often miss appointments because reminder messages are unclear.

Workshops clarify the requirements for reminders, cancellations, and rescheduling.

A prototype is demonstrated to representative users, and feedback is collected.

The analyst documents decisions, resolves conflicting requirements through the agreed governance process, and ensures that affected stakeholders understand the changes.

This approach improves the likelihood that the solution will meet stakeholder needs.

# 15 â€” ECBA Business Process Modeling and Improvement

## 1. Module Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain business processes and process modeling.
* Distinguish current-state and future-state processes.
* Understand process boundaries, activities, events, and gateways.
* Interpret basic flowcharts and BPMN concepts.
* Identify bottlenecks, waste, and rework.
* Apply root-cause analysis and process improvement.
* Define process performance measures.
* Evaluate improvements using business outcomes.

## 2. Introduction

A business process is a set of related activities performed to achieve an outcome.

Examples include:

* Processing customer orders.
* Recruiting employees.
* Approving invoices.
* Handling support requests.
* Managing inventory.
* Delivering products.
* Resolving customer complaints.

Business process modeling provides a structured representation of how work is performed.

Process improvement examines how a process can better achieve its objectives through changes to activities, responsibilities, decisions, information, or technology.

## 3. Why Model Business Processes?

Process models help stakeholders:

* Understand existing work.
* Identify duplicated activities.
* Discover bottlenecks.
* Clarify responsibilities.
* Identify decision points.
* Understand information flows.
* Document exceptions.
* Communicate proposed changes.
* Establish performance measures.

A process model should be appropriate for its audience and purpose.

An executive overview may require only major activities, while an operational model may need detailed decisions and exception paths.

## 4. Process Boundaries

A process model should identify where the process begins and ends.

### Start

The event or condition that initiates the process.

### End

The condition or outcome that marks completion of the process.

### Inputs

Information, materials, requests, or other resources needed to perform activities.

### Outputs

Products, services, decisions, or information produced by the process.

### Example

For invoice processing:

* Start: An invoice is received.
* Inputs: Invoice details, purchase order, receipt information.
* Activities: Validate, match, review, approve, and record.
* Output: An approved invoice ready for payment.
* End: The invoice enters the payment process or is rejected with a reason.

Clear boundaries prevent the analyst from unintentionally expanding the scope.

## 5. Process Activities and Decisions

### Activities

Activities represent work performed within a process.

Examples:

* Validate customer details.
* Check inventory availability.
* Approve a purchase.
* Dispatch an order.

### Decisions

Decision points determine which path the process follows.

Example:

"Is the requested item available?"

* Yes: Prepare the order.
* No: Notify the customer or initiate replenishment.

Decision conditions should be clear enough for stakeholders to understand the process logic.

## 6. Flowcharts

Flowcharts represent the sequence of activities and decisions.

Common symbols include:

* Oval or rounded shape: Start or end.
* Rectangle: Activity or process step.
* Diamond: Decision.
* Arrow: Direction of flow.
* Parallelogram: Input or output in many traditional flowchart conventions.

Organizations may use different notation conventions, so a model should include a legend when needed.

### Example

<escape>
Start
  |
Receive Order
  |
Validate Order
  |
Is Information Complete?
  | Yes                 | No
Check Inventory      Request Missing Data
  |                      |
Is Stock Available?  Receive Updated Data
  |                      |
Prepare Shipment <------+
  |
Dispatch Order
  |
End
</escape>

This simplified example illustrates the basic flow of activities and decisions. A production model should ensure that every branch leads to an appropriate next step.

## 7. BPMN Fundamentals

Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN) is a standardized notation for representing business processes.

### 7.1 Events

Events represent something that happens during a process.

Examples include:

* A process starts.
* A message arrives.
* A timer expires.
* A process completes.

### 7.2 Activities

Activities represent work performed.

Examples include reviewing an application, validating an invoice, or dispatching an order.

### 7.3 Gateways

Gateways control branching, merging, and synchronization.

An exclusive gateway commonly represents a choice between alternative paths.

A parallel gateway represents paths that may proceed concurrently.

### 7.4 Sequence Flows

Sequence flows show the order in which activities and events occur within a process.

### 7.5 Pools and Lanes

Pools generally represent participants in a collaboration.

Lanes divide responsibilities within a participant, such as departments or roles.

### 7.6 Message Flows

Message flows represent communication between separate participants in a BPMN collaboration diagram.

They are distinct from sequence flows, which represent process order.

For ECBA-level study, focus on understanding why the notation is used and how its basic elements communicate process behavior.

## 8. Current-State Process Modeling

A current-state model represents how work is performed today.

It should capture actual practices, not merely the official procedure.

Useful information includes:

* Activities.
* Roles.
* Inputs and outputs.
* Decision rules.
* Systems used.
* Waiting times.
* Exceptions.
* Rework.
* Handoffs.
* Performance measures.

### Example

A purchase approval process officially requires one approval.

Observation reveals that employees frequently obtain additional informal approvals by email.

The current-state model should account for this practice when it materially affects process performance.

## 9. Future-State Process Modeling

A future-state model describes the desired process after improvement.

It may include:

* Eliminated redundant steps.
* Automated validation.
* Clearer responsibilities.
* Improved information flow.
* Reduced manual data entry.
* New decision rules.
* Better exception handling.
* Additional controls.

The future state should address the underlying business need and respect organizational constraints.

It should not simply automate every existing activity without assessing whether the activity remains necessary.

## 10. Gap Analysis

Gap analysis compares the current and desired states.

Example:

| Measure                 | Current State | Future Target |                 Gap |
| ----------------------- | ------------: | ------------: | ------------------: |
| Invoice cycle time      |        8 days |        3 days |              5 days |
| Error rate              |            6% |            2% | 4 percentage points |
| Manual data-entry steps |            10 |             4 |             6 steps |

These targets are illustrative. The analyst should validate whether the future targets are realistic and meaningful.

Gap analysis helps identify the changes needed to move toward the desired process.

## 11. Bottleneck Analysis

A bottleneck is a point in a process that restricts overall throughput or creates significant delay.

Possible causes include:

* Limited staffing.
* Excessive approvals.
* Slow system performance.
* Incomplete information.
* Unbalanced workloads.
* Limited equipment capacity.
* Dependencies on external parties.

### Example

A company can prepare 100 orders per hour, but only 40 orders per hour can pass through final inspection.

Final inspection may be the process bottleneck.

Improving earlier steps may not increase overall throughput unless the inspection constraint is addressed.

## 12. Waste and Rework

Process improvement may identify work that consumes resources without producing sufficient value.

Examples include:

* Repeated data entry.
* Unnecessary movement.
* Waiting for approvals.
* Excessive inventory.
* Repeated corrections.
* Duplicate reports.
* Unnecessary handoffs.

Rework occurs when an activity must be repeated to correct an error or satisfy a requirement.

Reducing rework can improve cycle time, cost, quality, and employee productivity.

However, activities that appear non-value-adding may still be necessary for compliance, safety, or risk control. They should not be removed without appropriate analysis.

## 13. Root-Cause Analysis

Process problems should be investigated systematically.

### Five Whys

Repeatedly ask why the problem occurred until the team identifies a plausible underlying cause that can be investigated.

### Fishbone Diagram

Organizes possible causes into categories such as people, processes, technology, materials, measurement, and environment.

### Pareto Analysis

Uses frequency or impact data to identify the categories that contribute most to a problem.

The common 80/20 description is a heuristic, not a rule that every business problem must follow.

### Example

A warehouse experiences frequent shipping errors.

Analysis shows that a large proportion of errors occur in two product categories because the package labels are visually similar.

Potential actions include label redesign, barcode scanning, or improved verification procedures.

## 14. Process Performance Measures

### Cycle Time

The time required to complete a process or activity according to the agreed measurement definition.

### Throughput

The quantity of completed work over a specified period.

### Error Rate

The proportion of items containing defined errors.

### First-Pass Yield

The proportion of items completed correctly without requiring rework.

### Cost per Transaction

The cost associated with completing a defined transaction.

### Waiting Time

Time spent waiting rather than performing the relevant processing activity.

### Customer Satisfaction

A measure of how customers perceive their experience.

Definitions and measurement boundaries should be consistent when comparing performance over time.

## 15. Process Improvement Approaches

### Standardization

Establishing consistent procedures and rules.

### Automation

Using technology to perform or support activities.

### Simplification

Removing unnecessary complexity.

### Parallelization

Performing independent activities concurrently when appropriate.

### Error Prevention

Designing processes to prevent mistakes rather than merely detecting them later.

### Training

Improving the ability of employees to perform tasks correctly.

### Process Redesign

Changing the overall structure of a process when incremental improvements are insufficient.

The appropriate approach depends on root causes, risks, costs, and business objectives.

## 16. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: University Registration

A university experiences long registration queues at the beginning of each semester.

The analyst interviews students and administrative staff, observes the registration process, and reviews historical waiting-time data.

The current-state model identifies:

* Repeated manual entry.
* Separate verification steps.
* Missing documents discovered late.
* Unclear responsibilities.
* Delays caused by a single approval point.

The analyst investigates the causes and works with stakeholders to define a future-state process.

Potential improvements include:

1. Pre-registration document validation.
2. Clearer student instructions.
3. Shared access to verified information.
4. Standardized approval rules.
5. Better exception handling.
6. Online status tracking.

The analyst defines performance measures, such as average waiting time, registration completion rate, and error rate.

After implementation, the analyst compares actual performance with the baseline and target.

## 3. Essential Concepts to Review

### Business Analysis

Business analysis enables change by defining needs and recommending solutions that deliver value to stakeholders in a context.

### Core Terms

* **Need:** A problem or opportunity to address.
* **Change:** The transformation of an organization in response to a need.
* **Solution:** A specific way of satisfying one or more needs.
* **Stakeholder:** A person, group, or organization that relates to a change, need, or solution.
* **Value:** The worth, importance, or usefulness of something to a stakeholder in a context.
* **Requirement:** A usable representation of a need.
* **Design:** A representation of a solution.
* **Business requirement:** A goal, objective, or outcome that describes why a change is needed.
* **Stakeholder requirement:** A need of a stakeholder or stakeholder group.
* **Solution requirement:** A capability or quality that a solution must have to meet stakeholder requirements.
* **Transition requirement:** A temporary capability needed to move from the current state to a desired future state.

## 4. Practice Questions

### Question 1: Business Analysis Purpose

An organization experiences increasing customer complaints because its order-processing system is slow. What should the business analyst do first?

A. Purchase a faster server.

B. Investigate the problem, understand the business need, and establish the current state.

C. Replace the entire system.

D. Train all employees on the existing system.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The analyst must understand the need and current state before recommending a solution. The other options prematurely assume a cause or solution.

### Question 2: Stakeholder Identification

A new inventory system will affect warehouse staff, purchasing employees, suppliers, and finance. What is the best action?

A. Interview only the project sponsor.

B. Identify relevant stakeholders and analyze their roles, interests, and influence.

C. Invite every stakeholder to every meeting.

D. Allow the development team to determine stakeholder needs.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Stakeholder analysis identifies people affected by or able to influence the change. Engagement should then be tailored to their needs and responsibilities.

### Question 3: Requirement Classification

"The organization wants to reduce order-processing time by 30% within six months."

What type of requirement is this most likely to represent?

A. Transition requirement.

B. Solution requirement.

C. Business requirement.

D. Interface requirement.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** It states a business outcome and measurable objective rather than a specific system capability.

### Question 4: Elicitation

A stakeholder says, "The system is difficult to use." What should the analyst do?

A. Record the statement as a complete functional requirement.

B. Ask targeted questions and investigate specific usability problems.

C. Ignore the comment because it is subjective.

D. Immediately redesign the interface.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The statement is vague. Follow-up questions and observation can reveal concrete problems and requirements.

### Question 5: Requirements Validation

A requirement is clearly written and approved by the stakeholder, but it does not contribute to the business objective. What is the main concern?

A. The requirement lacks value or alignment with the need.

B. The requirement must be functional.

C. The requirement is automatically invalid because it is approved.

D. The requirement must be a transition requirement.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Approval and clarity do not guarantee that a requirement delivers value. Requirements should support the need and intended outcomes.

### Question 6: Requirements Verification

An analyst checks whether a requirement is unambiguous, complete, consistent, and testable. Which activity is being performed?

A. Requirements verification.

B. Solution implementation.

C. Benefits realization.

D. Stakeholder identification.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Verification examines the quality of the requirement itself. Validation examines whether it supports the need and expected value.

### Question 7: Traceability

A developer asks why a particular feature exists. Which artifact is most useful for showing its relationship to the original business objective?

A. Meeting attendance sheet.

B. Requirements traceability information.

C. Employee directory.

D. Project calendar alone.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Traceability connects requirements to their sources, objectives, related requirements, designs, and tests as appropriate.

### Question 8: Process Modeling

A business analyst wants to understand how an order moves from submission through payment and delivery. Which technique is most appropriate?

A. Stakeholder register only.

B. Process modeling.

C. Financial forecasting only.

D. Brainstorming without recording outcomes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Process models represent activities, decisions, flows, and responsibilities.

### Question 9: Change Control

After requirements approval, a stakeholder requests a new feature that may affect cost and schedule. What should the analyst do?

A. Accept it immediately.

B. Reject all changes automatically.

C. Assess the change, its impact, value, dependencies, and approval needs.

D. Ask the developer to add it without informing anyone.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Changes should be evaluated according to the project's governance and requirements management approach.

### Question 10: Communication

A technical team and business stakeholders repeatedly misunderstand one another. What is the best response?

A. Use technical terminology with everyone.

B. Tailor communication methods, terminology, and detail to each audience.

C. Stop stakeholder meetings.

D. Send the same lengthy technical document to everyone.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Effective communication considers audience, purpose, context, and preferred communication method.

### Question 11: Solution Evaluation

A new system has been implemented, but customer satisfaction has not improved. What should the analyst investigate?

A. Whether the solution delivers the expected value and addresses the original need.

B. Whether the project documents have attractive formatting.

C. Whether every stakeholder attended the launch.

D. Whether requirements can be deleted.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Solution evaluation compares actual performance and outcomes with expected value, considering solution limitations and external factors.

### Question 12: Prioritization

A team has limited resources and cannot implement every requested feature in the first release. What should guide prioritization?

A. The loudest stakeholder's opinion alone.

B. Business value, risk, dependencies, urgency, and stakeholder needs.

C. Alphabetical order.

D. The developer's personal preference alone.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritization balances value and constraints using an agreed approach.

### Question 13: Functional Requirement

Which statement is a functional requirement?

A. The system should be easy to use.

B. The system must be reliable.

C. The system shall allow authorized users to cancel an unshipped order.

D. The project should reduce operational costs.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** It specifies a behavior or capability that the solution must provide.

### Question 14: Non-Functional Requirement

Which statement is a non-functional requirement?

A. The system shall create an invoice.

B. The system shall allow a user to reset a password.

C. The system shall process 95% of standard searches within two seconds under the specified load.

D. The system shall approve a valid purchase order.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** It specifies a measurable performance characteristic. The other options primarily describe behaviors.

### Question 15: Root Cause

Employees report that they frequently enter incorrect product quantities. What is the best analytical approach?

A. Assume employees need punishment.

B. Investigate the process, interface, training, data quality, and underlying causes.

C. Replace every employee.

D. Immediately automate the entire process.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Symptoms do not establish root causes. Investigate evidence before selecting an intervention.

## 7. Scenario-Based Practice

### Scenario: Retail Inventory Transformation

A retail organization uses spreadsheets to manage inventory. Stock discrepancies occur frequently, purchasing is delayed, and managers lack reliable reports.

#### Tasks

1. Identify the likely business need.
2. List five stakeholders.
3. Write two measurable business objectives.
4. Identify three elicitation techniques.
5. Write two functional requirements.
6. Write two non-functional requirements.
7. Identify one transition requirement.
8. Suggest criteria for prioritizing requirements.
9. Describe how the organization could evaluate the solution.

#### Model Answer

**Business need:** Improve inventory accuracy and availability while reducing manual effort and purchasing delays.

**Stakeholders:** Warehouse staff, purchasing staff, finance, store managers, suppliers, and IT support.

**Business objectives:**

* Reduce inventory discrepancy rate from the measured baseline by an agreed percentage within six months.
* Reduce average purchase-order processing time by 25% within six months.

Targets should be confirmed against baseline data and business feasibility.

**Elicitation techniques:** Interviews, observation, and document analysis.

**Functional requirements:**

* The system shall allow authorized staff to record stock receipts and issues.
* The system shall generate a low-stock alert when available inventory falls below an approved reorder threshold.

**Non-functional requirements:**

* The system shall meet an agreed availability target during defined business hours.
* Standard inventory searches shall complete within an agreed response-time target under specified load.

**Transition requirement:** Existing product and inventory records must be cleansed and migrated before go-live.

**Prioritization criteria:** Business value, risk reduction, urgency, dependencies, implementation effort, and regulatory needs.

**Evaluation:** Compare post-implementation discrepancy rates, processing times, stockout frequency, user feedback, and total operating costs against baseline measures.
