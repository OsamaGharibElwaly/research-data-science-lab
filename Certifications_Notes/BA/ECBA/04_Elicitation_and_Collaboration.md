# 04 — Elicitation and Collaboration

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Identify common elicitation techniques.
* Prepare for and conduct elicitation activities.

## 2. Introduction to Elicitation and Collaboration

Elicitation is the work of drawing out, discovering, and confirming information relevant to business analysis.

Collaboration involves working with stakeholders and other participants to achieve shared objectives.

Elicitation and collaboration are closely related because understanding business needs usually requires active participation from the people who know the business, use its processes, or are affected by the proposed change.

Elicitation is not limited to asking questions. It may involve observing work, analyzing existing documents, conducting experiments, facilitating workshops, examining data, or reviewing a prototype.

### Why Elicitation Matters

Effective elicitation helps the Business Analyst:

* Understand the real business problem.
* Discover stakeholder needs and expectations.
* Identify existing processes and constraints.
* Reveal assumptions and dependencies.
* Resolve conflicting perspectives.
* Reduce missing or misunderstood requirements.
* Establish a shared understanding of the desired change.

Poor elicitation can lead to incorrect requirements, unnecessary features, project delays, rework, and solutions that fail to meet stakeholder needs.

## 3. The Elicitation and Collaboration Knowledge Area

The BABOK Guide identifies five tasks within this knowledge area:

1. Prepare for Elicitation.
2. Conduct Elicitation.
3. Confirm Elicitation Results.
4. Communicate Business Analysis Information.
5. Manage Stakeholder Collaboration.

These tasks support effective discovery, communication, and stakeholder participation.

## 4. Prepare for Elicitation

### 4.1 Purpose

Preparation ensures that elicitation activities are organized to produce useful information.

The analyst determines what information is needed, which stakeholders should participate, which techniques are appropriate, and what resources are required.

### 4.2 Define the Elicitation Objectives

Before an interview or workshop, clarify its purpose.

Examples:

* Understand why customers abandon checkout.
* Identify current inventory management procedures.
* Discover reporting requirements for department managers.
* Determine why employees experience repeated system failures.
* Clarify the acceptance criteria for a proposed feature.

A clear objective helps prevent discussions from becoming unfocused.

### 4.3 Identify Relevant Stakeholders

Determine who has the knowledge, experience, authority, or perspective needed for the activity.

For example, investigating delayed orders may require participation from:

* Warehouse employees.
* Inventory managers.
* Delivery coordinators.
* Customer service representatives.
* IT support.
* Customers.

Relying only on senior management may produce an incomplete understanding of operational reality.

### 4.4 Select Appropriate Elicitation Techniques

The choice depends on:

* The type of information required.
* Stakeholder availability.
* Complexity of the problem.
* Number of participants.
* Geographic distribution.
* Sensitivity of the information.
* Time and budget constraints.
* Whether stakeholders can explain the existing process accurately.

### 4.5 Prepare Supporting Materials

Preparation may include:

* Interview questions.
* Workshop agendas.
* Existing requirements.
* Process diagrams.
* Questionnaires.
* Sample reports.
* Prototypes.
* Observation checklists.
* Data extracts.

Existing documentation should be treated as a source of information, not automatically assumed to reflect current practice.

## 5. Conduct Elicitation

Conducting elicitation means performing the planned activity and capturing relevant information.

The analyst should create an environment in which participants can provide accurate information and clarify misunderstandings.

Typical activities include:

* Asking open-ended questions.
* Probing for specific examples.
* Observing real work.
* Recording important findings.
* Clarifying terminology.
* Identifying assumptions.
* Investigating inconsistencies.
* Confirming interpretations during the conversation.

The analyst should remain objective and avoid leading participants toward a preferred solution.

### Open-Ended Questions

Open-ended questions encourage detailed responses.

Examples:

* "Can you describe how an order is processed?"
* "What difficulties do you encounter during this process?"
* "What happens when the system is unavailable?"
* "How do you determine whether a report is accurate?"

### Closed-Ended Questions

Closed-ended questions seek specific answers.

Examples:

* "Does the system require manager approval?"
* "Is the report generated daily?"
* "Can customers cancel an order after dispatch?"

Both types are useful. Open-ended questions explore a topic, while closed-ended questions clarify particular facts.

### Probing Questions

Probing questions investigate an answer more deeply.

Examples:

* "Can you give me an example?"
* "Why does that happen?"
* "How often does this occur?"
* "What happens next?"
* "What is the business impact?"

Effective elicitation often combines all three question types.

## 6. Common Elicitation Techniques

### 6.1 Interviews

An interview is a structured or semi-structured conversation used to obtain information from one or more people.

**Best for:**

* Understanding individual responsibilities.
* Investigating detailed workflows.
* Exploring sensitive problems.
* Clarifying stakeholder expectations.
* Discovering exceptions and unusual cases.

**Advantages:**

* Allows follow-up questions.
* Provides detailed explanations.
* Helps clarify ambiguity.
* Can uncover information that stakeholders may not share in a group.

**Limitations:**

* Time-consuming.
* May reflect individual bias.
* Can be affected by poor questioning.
* Different stakeholders may provide contradictory accounts.

**Example:** Interview warehouse supervisors to understand why order-picking errors occur during busy periods.

### 6.2 Workshops

A workshop brings multiple participants together to collaborate on a defined topic.

**Best for:**

* Developing shared understanding.
* Resolving disagreements.
* Prioritizing requirements.
* Mapping business processes.
* Identifying opportunities for improvement.

**Advantages:**

* Multiple perspectives can be compared immediately.
* Conflicts can be addressed collaboratively.
* Decisions may be reached more quickly.
* Participants can build on one another's ideas.

**Limitations:**

* Dominant participants may influence the discussion.
* Scheduling can be difficult.
* Group pressure may discourage disagreement.
* Poor facilitation can reduce effectiveness.

**Example:** Organize a workshop involving finance, sales, and operations to agree on invoice approval requirements.

### 6.3 Surveys and Questionnaires

Surveys collect information from a relatively large number of participants using a consistent set of questions.

**Best for:**

* Measuring user satisfaction.
* Identifying common problems.
* Gathering feedback across departments.
* Collecting information from geographically distributed stakeholders.

**Advantages:**

* Can reach many participants.
* Standardized questions simplify comparisons.
* May require less time per participant.

**Limitations:**

* Responses may lack detail.
* Questions can be misunderstood.
* Response rates may be low.
* Poorly designed questions introduce bias.

**Example:** Survey 500 customers to determine which checkout problems occur most frequently.

### 6.4 Observation

Observation involves examining stakeholders as they perform actual tasks.

It is particularly useful when stakeholders struggle to explain their work or when documented procedures differ from reality.

**Best for:**

* Understanding real workflows.
* Identifying delays and bottlenecks.
* Discovering workarounds.
* Investigating repetitive tasks.
* Understanding environmental constraints.

**Advantages:**

* Reveals actual behavior.
* Can uncover undocumented procedures.
* Helps identify inefficiencies that interviews miss.

**Limitations:**

* People may change their behavior when observed.
* Observation takes time.
* Some tasks occur infrequently.
* The analyst may misinterpret what is observed.

**Example:** Observe customer service agents handling calls to identify repeated manual steps.

### 6.5 Document Analysis

Document analysis examines existing records to understand business operations, rules, and requirements.

Potential sources include:

* Policies.
* Procedures.
* Contracts.
* Existing requirements.
* Business reports.
* Forms.
* Training manuals.
* Regulations.
* Support tickets.
* Previous project documentation.

**Advantages:**

* Provides historical information.
* Helps identify existing rules.
* Supports preparation for interviews.
* May reveal inconsistencies between documents.

**Limitations:**

* Documents may be outdated.
* Some practices may never have been documented.
* Different documents may contradict one another.

**Example:** Review support tickets and service-level agreements to identify recurring customer service problems.

### 6.6 Brainstorming

Brainstorming generates ideas and possible solutions.

It is useful when exploring opportunities, investigating causes, or identifying alternative approaches.

Effective brainstorming separates idea generation from evaluation so participants are not discouraged from contributing.

**Example:** Generate possible explanations for declining customer retention before evaluating each hypothesis.

### 6.7 Prototyping

A prototype is an early representation of a potential solution used to obtain feedback.

Prototypes may be:

* Paper sketches.
* Wireframes.
* Clickable mockups.
* Interactive demonstrations.
* Limited working implementations.

**Best for:**

* Clarifying interface expectations.
* Discovering missing functionality.
* Testing user assumptions.
* Comparing design alternatives.

A prototype should not automatically be treated as an approved final design.

**Example:** Show customers a clickable checkout mockup and ask them to complete a purchase.

### 6.8 Focus Groups

A focus group involves a selected group of participants discussing a topic under facilitation.

It can help explore opinions, expectations, experiences, and reactions.

**Example:** Invite a small group of customers to discuss their experience with a delivery-tracking service.

Focus groups are useful for qualitative insight but should not automatically be treated as statistically representative of an entire population.

### 6.9 Interface Analysis

Interface analysis examines interactions between a solution and external users, systems, or other components.

Interfaces may involve:

* User interfaces.
* APIs.
* External applications.
* Payment providers.
* Hardware.
* Data exchange mechanisms.

**Example:** Analyze how an e-commerce platform exchanges payment status with an external payment gateway.

This helps identify integration requirements, constraints, and dependencies.

## 7. Choosing the Right Technique

Different techniques provide different types of information.

| Business Situation                              | Suitable Technique |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| Understand one expert's detailed workflow       | Interview          |
| Resolve conflicting departmental requirements   | Workshop           |
| Gather feedback from hundreds of users          | Survey             |
| Discover undocumented work practices            | Observation        |
| Understand existing policies and business rules | Document analysis  |
| Generate possible explanations or solutions     | Brainstorming      |
| Explore interface expectations                  | Prototyping        |
| Investigate system-to-system interactions       | Interface analysis |
| Explore opinions and reactions in a small group | Focus group        |

More than one technique may be needed to understand a complex problem.

For example, interviews may reveal reported issues, observation may show actual behavior, and document analysis may establish formal policies.

Using multiple independent sources can help verify findings.

## 8. Confirm Elicitation Results

### 8.1 Purpose

Confirmation checks that the information captured during elicitation accurately reflects what participants intended to communicate.

It helps prevent misunderstandings from becoming part of the business analysis information.

### 8.2 Confirmation Methods

The analyst may:

* Summarize findings to participants.
* Circulate meeting notes.
* Review process diagrams with stakeholders.
* Ask participants to confirm assumptions.
* Demonstrate a prototype.
* Clarify contradictory statements.
* Record unresolved questions.
* Request corrections to documented information.

Confirmation is not necessarily the same as formal approval.

A stakeholder may confirm that the analyst accurately recorded a discussion without having authority to approve the resulting requirements.

### Example

During an interview, a warehouse supervisor states that urgent orders should be processed first.

The analyst documents this rule and asks the supervisor to confirm whether urgency is determined by delivery deadline, customer category, or another factor.

The clarification prevents an ambiguous statement from becoming an incomplete requirement.

## 9. Communicate Business Analysis Information

### 9.1 Purpose

Business analysis information must be communicated in a way that stakeholders can understand and use.

Communication includes sharing information, explaining findings, and ensuring that relevant participants receive the appropriate information.

### 9.2 Effective Communication

The analyst should consider:

* Audience knowledge.
* Information complexity.
* Communication preferences.
* Confidentiality.
* Timing.
* Required level of detail.
* Purpose of the communication.
* Whether feedback is necessary.

Executives may need a concise summary of business impact and risks. Developers may need detailed requirements and interface behavior. Operational employees may need process diagrams and practical examples.

### 9.3 Common Communication Formats

* Reports.
* Presentations.
* Requirements documents.
* Process models.
* Email summaries.
* Workshops.
* Dashboards.
* Demonstrations.
* Shared repositories.
* Decision logs.

### Example

To explain a proposed warehouse change, the analyst prepares:

* An executive summary for management.
* A process diagram for warehouse supervisors.
* Detailed functional requirements for developers.
* Training materials for warehouse employees.

The underlying information is consistent, but its presentation is adapted to each audience.

## 10. Manage Stakeholder Collaboration

### 10.1 Purpose

Stakeholder collaboration maintains effective working relationships and encourages participants to contribute to the business analysis effort.

Collaboration includes managing expectations, resolving misunderstandings, addressing conflicts, and supporting timely decisions.

### 10.2 Common Collaboration Challenges

**Conflicting priorities:** Finance wants lower costs, while operations wants additional staffing.

**Limited availability:** Important subject-matter experts cannot attend workshops.

**Communication barriers:** Stakeholders use different terminology or have different technical backgrounds.

**Resistance to change:** Employees fear that automation will make their roles less secure.

**Unclear authority:** Participants disagree about who can approve requirements.

**Competing assumptions:** Stakeholders have different beliefs about the cause of a problem.

### 10.3 Managing Conflicts

A Business Analyst should investigate the source of disagreement rather than simply choose the loudest or most senior participant.

Useful approaches include:

1. Clarify each stakeholder's underlying need.
2. Separate facts from assumptions.
3. Identify shared objectives.
4. Evaluate constraints and trade-offs.
5. Compare alternatives using agreed criteria.
6. Facilitate a decision by the authorized person or group.
7. Document the outcome and any remaining disagreement.

The analyst facilitates understanding and informed decisions but does not necessarily have authority to impose the final decision.

### Example

Finance wants to minimize software costs. Customer service wants a feature-rich system.

The analyst compares alternative solutions against cost, customer impact, operational efficiency, risk, and strategic objectives.

The stakeholders can then make a more informed decision.

## 11. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: Hospital Appointment Delays

A hospital experiences long waiting times and frequent appointment conflicts.

Management asks for a new appointment booking system.

### Step 1: Prepare for Elicitation

Define objectives:

* Understand how appointments are currently scheduled.
* Identify causes of delays.
* Discover stakeholder needs.
* Identify constraints affecting scheduling.

Identify stakeholders:

* Patients.
* Receptionists.
* Doctors.
* Nurses.
* Hospital administrators.
* IT support.
* Compliance personnel.

Select interviews, observation, document analysis, and a workshop.

### Step 2: Conduct Elicitation

Interviews reveal that patients frequently arrive late.

Observation shows that appointments are sometimes overbooked.

Document analysis reveals that different departments use separate scheduling spreadsheets.

A workshop identifies inconsistent rules for emergency appointments.

### Step 3: Confirm Results

The analyst circulates a process diagram and summarizes the findings.

Receptionists confirm the current workflow, while doctors clarify how emergency appointments should be prioritized.

Unresolved questions are recorded for follow-up.

### Step 4: Communicate Information

The analyst prepares:

* A current-state process model.
* A summary of major causes of delays.
* A list of stakeholder needs.
* A record of assumptions and unresolved issues.

### Step 5: Manage Collaboration

The hospital administrator prioritizes reduced waiting times. Doctors emphasize emergency flexibility. Receptionists emphasize ease of use.

The analyst facilitates discussion to establish common objectives and identify workable scheduling rules.

### Possible Outcomes

The hospital may choose to standardize scheduling procedures, centralize appointment data, introduce automated reminders, or implement a new system.

The final solution should reflect the validated needs rather than the initial assumption that software alone will resolve every problem.

## 12. Practice Questions

### Question 1

What is the primary purpose of elicitation?

A. To write source code.

B. To discover, draw out, and confirm relevant information.

C. To approve the project budget.

D. To guarantee that every stakeholder agrees immediately.

**Correct answer: B**

Explanation: Elicitation helps the analyst discover information about needs, requirements, context, and stakeholder expectations.

### Question 2

A Business Analyst needs to understand how warehouse employees actually process orders. Existing procedures are outdated. Which technique is particularly useful?

A. Observation.

B. Executive presentation.

C. Budget approval.

D. Final solution evaluation only.

**Correct answer: A**

Explanation: Observation reveals actual work practices and can uncover undocumented procedures and workarounds.

### Question 3

Which technique is generally most suitable for gathering standardized feedback from a large number of customers?

A. Individual interviews with every customer.

B. Surveys or questionnaires.

C. A single executive workshop.

D. Reviewing source code only.

**Correct answer: B**

Explanation: Surveys can reach a large population using consistent questions, although they may provide less detailed explanations than interviews.

### Question 4

A workshop produces meeting notes, but participants disagree about whether the notes accurately represent their decisions. What should the analyst do?

A. Ignore the disagreement.

B. Confirm the elicitation results with the relevant participants and resolve ambiguities.

C. Assume the most senior participant is always correct.

D. Immediately implement every statement in the notes.

**Correct answer: B**

Explanation: Confirming elicitation results helps ensure that the captured information accurately reflects what stakeholders communicated.

### Question 5

A team wants to obtain early feedback on the layout of a proposed mobile application. Which technique is especially suitable?

A. Prototyping.

B. Financial auditing only.

C. Organizational restructuring.

D. Final production deployment.

**Correct answer: A**

Explanation: Prototypes help stakeholders explore proposed functionality and provide feedback before full implementation.

### Question 6

Two departments disagree about the priority of a requirement. What is the most appropriate response?

A. Automatically select the preference of the largest department.

B. Ignore the conflict.

C. Clarify underlying needs, assess trade-offs, and facilitate a decision through the appropriate governance process.

D. Remove both departments from the initiative.

**Correct answer: C**

Explanation: Effective collaboration makes competing needs visible and supports decisions based on business objectives, constraints, and authorized decision-making arrangements.

### Question 7

A stakeholder confirms that meeting notes accurately reflect a discussion. Does this necessarily mean the stakeholder has formally approved all requirements?

A. Yes, confirmation and approval always mean the same thing.

B. No, confirmation checks accuracy, while formal approval depends on the applicable governance arrangements.

C. Yes, if the meeting lasted more than one hour.

D. No, because elicitation results should never be documented.

**Correct answer: B**

Explanation: Confirming information and formally approving requirements are distinct activities.

### Question 8

A Business Analyst needs to understand the existing rules governing invoice approval. Which technique should be considered?

A. Document analysis.

B. Brainstorming alone.

C. Prototype deployment.

D. Informal guessing.

**Correct answer: A**

Explanation: Existing policies, procedures, and records can reveal formal business rules. Additional techniques may be required to confirm whether actual practice matches the documentation.

### Question 9

Which is the best example of an open-ended elicitation question?

A. "Does the system have a login page?"

B. "Is the process automated?"

C. "Can you describe how you currently handle customer complaints?"

D. "Is the report generated every Monday?"

**Correct answer: C**

Explanation: The question invites a detailed description. The other questions are primarily closed-ended.

### Question 10

An analyst prepares one detailed technical report for developers and a short summary of business impact for executives. What principle does this demonstrate?

A. Communication should be adapted to the audience and purpose.

B. All stakeholders must receive identical documents.

C. Technical details should never be documented.

D. Stakeholder collaboration is unnecessary.

**Correct answer: A**

Explanation: Effective communication considers the audience's needs, knowledge, and decision-making responsibilities.

* Need agreement among multiple stakeholders? Consider a workshop.
* Need broad standardized feedback? Consider a survey.
* Need to understand actual behavior? Consider observation.
* Need to understand existing documented rules? Consider document analysis.
