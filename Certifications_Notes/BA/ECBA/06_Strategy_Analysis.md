## 2. Introduction to Strategy Analysis

Strategy Analysis examines the business need, the current state of an enterprise, the desired future state, and the change required to move between them.

It helps organizations determine why change is necessary, what outcomes they want to achieve, and which approaches may deliver those outcomes.

Strategy analysis takes a broader perspective than documenting individual requirements. It considers business objectives, organizational capabilities, market conditions, risks, and long-term value.

The BABOK Guide identifies six tasks within Strategy Analysis:

1. Analyze Current State.
2. Define Future State.
3. Assess Risks.
4. Define Change Strategy.

The first three activities provide the foundation for defining a suitable change strategy. These tasks may be revisited as new information emerges.

## 3. Analyze Current State

### 3.1 Purpose

Analyzing the current state establishes how the enterprise operates today and why change may be required.

The current state may include:

* Business processes.
* Organizational structure.
* Technology systems.
* Employee capabilities.
* Information flows.
* Policies and regulations.
* Performance measures.
* Customer experiences.
* Existing problems and opportunities.

### 3.2 Identify Business Needs

Business needs may arise from internal or external factors.

Internal factors include:

* High operating costs.
* Inefficient processes.
* Frequent errors.
* Poor data quality.
* Limited employee capacity.
* Outdated systems.

External factors include:

* New regulations.
* Changing customer expectations.
* Competitor actions.
* Economic conditions.
* Technological developments.
* Supplier disruptions.

### 3.3 Analyze Capabilities

A capability is an ability that an enterprise possesses or needs to achieve a particular outcome.

Capabilities may involve people, processes, information, technology, and organizational resources.

For example, an online retailer may need capabilities in demand forecasting, inventory management, order fulfillment, and customer support.

The analyst examines whether existing capabilities are sufficient to meet business objectives.

### 3.4 Current-State Performance

Performance information can help establish why change is needed.

Examples include:

* Average processing time.
* Cost per transaction.
* Customer satisfaction.
* Defect rates.
* Revenue growth.
* Employee productivity.
* Service availability.

A baseline provides a reference point against which future performance can be evaluated.

### Example

A company processes customer refunds in an average of eight days. Customer complaints have increased, and employees manually check multiple systems.

The analyst documents the current process, identifies delays, interviews employees, and measures the frequency of errors.

These findings establish the current-state problems that a change initiative should address.

## 4. Define Future State

### 4.1 Purpose

Defining the future state describes the desired conditions after a change.

The future state should explain what the enterprise wants to achieve and how success will be recognized.

It may include:

* Desired business outcomes.
* Required capabilities.
* Target performance levels.
* New or improved processes.
* Technology changes.
* Organizational changes.
* Business constraints.
* Measures of success.

### 4.2 Define Measurable Objectives

Objectives should be specific enough to guide decisions and evaluate outcomes.

Weak objective: Improve customer service.

Stronger objective: Reduce average customer support response time from 12 hours to four hours within six months while maintaining customer satisfaction.

The stronger objective identifies a measurable outcome, baseline, target, and time frame.

### 4.3 Define Required Capabilities

The analyst identifies the capabilities needed to achieve the future state.

For example, a company seeking faster refunds may require:

* Automated refund eligibility checks.
* Accurate order-status information.
* Clear approval rules.
* Integration between payment and order systems.
* Staff training.
* Exception handling.

The future state describes required business capabilities without necessarily committing to one specific technical implementation.

### 4.4 Define Constraints

Constraints limit the possible future state or the methods available to achieve it.

Examples include:

* Budget limits.
* Legal obligations.
* Fixed deadlines.
* Existing infrastructure.
* Staff availability.
* Data residency rules.
* Contractual commitments.

A future state must be desirable while remaining realistic within the relevant constraints.

## 5. Perform Gap Analysis

Gap analysis compares the current state with the future state to identify what must change.

A gap may involve missing capabilities, insufficient performance, outdated processes, technology limitations, or organizational weaknesses.

### Example

| Area              | Current State       | Future State                 | Identified Gap                 |
| ----------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Refund processing | Manual checks       | Automated eligibility checks | Automation capability missing  |
| Customer data     | Distributed systems | Consistent customer record   | Data integration required      |
| Staff knowledge   | Informal procedures | Standardized training        | Training program needed        |
| Performance       | Eight-day average   | Two-day average              | Process efficiency improvement |

Gap analysis helps define the scope of change and identify possible initiatives.

### Important Distinction

A gap is the difference between the current and desired states. It is not automatically a specific solution.

For example, the gap may be slow processing. Automation could be one response, but process redesign or clearer approval rules might also address the problem.

## 6. Assess Risks

### 6.1 Purpose

Strategy analysis considers uncertainties that may affect the ability to achieve the future state.

A risk is an uncertain event or condition that could affect objectives.

Risks can have negative or positive effects, although change assessments frequently emphasize threats.

### 6.2 Common Business Risks

* Implementation costs exceed expectations.
* Employees resist new procedures.
* Data migration fails.
* A new system disrupts operations.
* Regulatory requirements are misunderstood.
* Expected benefits do not materialize.
* Key stakeholders become unavailable.
* A supplier fails to deliver.
* New technology introduces security weaknesses.

### 6.3 Risk Assessment

A practical risk assessment considers:

* The risk description.
* Its potential causes.
* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Existing controls.
* Possible responses.
* The person responsible for managing it.

Organizations may use qualitative scales, numerical scoring, or more formal risk analysis techniques.

### 6.4 Risk Responses

Possible responses include:

* Avoiding the risk by changing the approach.
* Reducing its likelihood or impact.
* Transferring certain consequences through contractual arrangements or insurance.
* Accepting the risk when appropriate.
* Exploiting or enhancing a positive opportunity.

The appropriate response depends on the risk, organizational tolerance, cost, and potential consequences.

### Example

A retailer plans to migrate millions of customer records.

Risk: Data migration errors could disrupt customer service.

Possible responses:

* Perform data profiling.
* Clean records before migration.
* Conduct test migrations.
* Reconcile record counts.
* Prepare a rollback plan.
* Monitor results after launch.

## 7. Define Change Strategy

### 7.1 Purpose

A change strategy describes the approach for moving from the current state to the future state.

It identifies the broad actions required to address the business need and achieve desired outcomes.

A change strategy may consider:

* Alternative solutions.
* Required capabilities.
* Costs and benefits.
* Risks.
* Dependencies.
* Organizational readiness.
* Implementation options.
* Transition arrangements.
* Expected business value.

### 7.2 Possible Change Strategies

A company may choose to:

* Improve an existing process.
* Purchase an off-the-shelf system.
* Develop a custom solution.
* Outsource selected activities.
* Introduce a new operating model.
* Train employees.
* Combine several changes.

### 7.3 Compare Alternatives

Alternatives should be evaluated against relevant criteria.

For example:

| Criterion             | Option A: Improve Existing System | Option B: Replace System  |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Initial cost          | Lower                             | Higher                    |
| Implementation time   | Shorter                           | Longer                    |
| Flexibility           | Limited                           | Potentially greater       |
| Migration risk        | Lower                             | Higher                    |
| Long-term maintenance | May remain costly                 | Depends on the new system |

These are illustrative comparisons. The actual assessment should use evidence from the organization.

### 7.4 Organizational Readiness

A technically feasible change may still fail if the organization is not prepared to adopt it.

Readiness considerations include:

* Leadership support.
* Employee skills.
* Availability of resources.
* Stakeholder commitment.
* Organizational culture.
* Training needs.
* Operational capacity.

A change strategy may therefore include communication, training, pilot deployments, and phased implementation.

## 8. Strategy Analysis and Business Value

Strategy analysis should connect the proposed change to business outcomes.

A solution may be delivered successfully from a technical perspective without achieving the expected business benefits.

For example, a new inventory system may launch on schedule but fail to reduce stockouts because purchasing policies remain unchanged.

The analyst should identify the capabilities and organizational changes needed to realize the intended value.

## 9. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: Manufacturing Production Delays

A manufacturing company experiences frequent delivery delays.

### Current State

* Production planning uses spreadsheets.
* Inventory data is updated manually.
* Machine downtime is recorded inconsistently.
* Management receives weekly performance reports.
* Delivery delays affect customer satisfaction.

### Future State

The company wants to:

* Reduce late deliveries from 20% to 8%.
* Improve inventory accuracy to 98%.
* Identify machine downtime within the same shift.
* Provide daily production performance information.

### Gap Analysis

The organization lacks timely production data, consistent inventory information, and reliable downtime reporting.

### Risk Assessment

Potential risks include employee resistance, inaccurate historical data, integration failures, and disruption during implementation.

### Change Strategy

Possible actions include:

1. Standardize inventory procedures.
2. Improve machine downtime recording.
3. Integrate production and inventory information.
4. Train employees.
5. Introduce a production dashboard.
6. Deploy improvements in stages.

The organization should evaluate the costs, risks, dependencies, and expected value before selecting the final approach.

## 10. Practice Questions

### Question 1

What is the primary purpose of analyzing the current state?

A. To select a programming language immediately.

B. To understand the existing enterprise, its problems, and its capabilities.

C. To approve all future requirements.

D. To eliminate the need for stakeholder engagement.

**Correct answer: B**

Explanation: Current-state analysis establishes the conditions and problems that motivate change.

### Question 2

What does gap analysis identify?

A. The difference between the current state and the desired future state.

B. Only the number of employees in an organization.

C. Only software defects.

D. The final project budget in every case.

**Correct answer: A**

Explanation: Gap analysis identifies differences that must be addressed to achieve the future state.

### Question 3

Which is the strongest example of a measurable future-state objective?

A. Improve the website.

B. Make employees happier.

C. Reduce average order processing time from 30 minutes to 15 minutes within four months.

D. Purchase modern software.

**Correct answer: C**

Explanation: It specifies a baseline, measurable target, and time frame.

### Question 4

A proposed system is technically feasible, but employees lack the skills to use it. What should the analyst consider?

A. Organizational readiness and training needs.

B. Ignoring the employees.

C. Removing all performance measures.

D. Assuming technical feasibility guarantees success.

**Correct answer: A**

Explanation: Organizational readiness affects whether a change can be adopted and whether it will deliver value.

### Question 5

A company wants to replace an existing system. What should it do before committing to the change strategy?

A. Compare relevant alternatives, benefits, costs, risks, and constraints.

B. Select the most expensive alternative.

C. Ignore the current state.

D. Begin migration without planning.

**Correct answer: A**

Explanation: Evaluating alternatives helps the organization select an approach appropriate to its needs and context.

### Question 6

Which is an example of a risk response?

A. Identifying a future-state objective.

B. Preparing a tested rollback plan to reduce the impact of migration failure.

C. Recording a stakeholder's job title.

D. Writing a general mission statement.

**Correct answer: B**

Explanation: A rollback plan is a risk response intended to reduce the consequences of a potential failure.

## 12. Final Exam Reminder

Distinguish the current state, future state, gap, risk, and change strategy:

* Current state: What exists today?
* Future state: What should exist?
* Gap: What differs between the two?
* Risk: What uncertainty could affect the change?
* Change strategy: How should the organization move toward the desired state?

Look for answers that connect business needs, measurable outcomes, organizational context, and value.
