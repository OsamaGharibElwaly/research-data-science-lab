## 2. Introduction

Business analysis techniques are methods used to investigate business needs, understand stakeholders, analyze information, evaluate alternatives, and support decisions.

A Business Analyst selects techniques according to the business context, available information, stakeholder needs, constraints, and expected results.

No single technique is appropriate for every situation. Interviews may help explore individual stakeholder needs, while workshops may be more suitable for resolving disagreements among several stakeholders.

## 3. Stakeholder Analysis

### 3.1 Purpose

Stakeholder analysis identifies individuals, groups, or organizations that influence a change, are affected by it, or have relevant interests in its outcome.

Typical stakeholders include:

* Customers.
* Business sponsors.
* End users.
* Managers.
* Developers.
* Testers.
* Operations teams.
* Suppliers.
* Regulators.
* Security and compliance representatives.

### 3.2 Power-Interest Grid

A power-interest grid classifies stakeholders according to their influence and interest.

| Stakeholder Category      | Typical Approach      |
| ------------------------- | --------------------- |
| High power, high interest | Manage closely        |
| High power, low interest  | Keep satisfied        |
| Low power, high interest  | Keep informed         |
| Low power, low interest   | Monitor appropriately |

This classification is a planning aid, not a substitute for understanding individual stakeholder needs.

### 3.3 Example

A bank is developing a mobile application.

* Executive sponsor: High power, high interest.
* Customer using the application: May have low organizational power but high interest.
* Regulatory authority: High influence over compliance requirements.
* Employee from an unrelated department: May have limited interest and influence.

The analyst should adjust engagement to reflect each stakeholder's responsibilities and concerns.

## 4. Interviews

### 4.1 Definition

An interview is a structured or semi-structured conversation used to collect information from a stakeholder.

Interviews are useful for exploring experiences, expectations, problems, assumptions, and requirements.

### 4.2 Interview Types

**Structured interview:** Uses a predefined set of questions.

**Semi-structured interview:** Uses planned questions with flexibility to explore important responses.

**Unstructured interview:** Uses a flexible conversational approach.

### 4.3 Good Interview Practices

1. Define the interview objective.
2. Select suitable participants.
3. Prepare open-ended questions.
4. Avoid leading questions.
5. Listen actively.
6. Ask follow-up questions.
7. Record important findings appropriately.
8. Confirm understanding.
9. Document and communicate results.

### 4.4 Example Questions

For an inventory-management project:

* How do you currently record incoming stock?
* What problems occur during stock reconciliation?
* Which decisions require information that is difficult to obtain?
* What exceptions occur most frequently?
* What would a successful process look like?

Open-ended questions often reveal problems that predefined multiple-choice questions miss.

## 5. Workshops

A workshop brings stakeholders together to discuss a defined subject and achieve a shared outcome.

Workshops can be used for:

* Requirements elicitation.
* Process improvement.
* Prioritization.
* Conflict resolution.
* Business rule definition.
* Solution evaluation.

### Effective Workshop Structure

1. Define objectives and scope.
2. Identify participants.
3. Prepare an agenda.
4. Establish discussion rules.
5. Facilitate participation.
6. Record decisions and unresolved questions.
7. Assign actions.
8. Distribute results.

A facilitator should encourage balanced participation and prevent one participant from dominating the discussion.

## 6. Observation

Observation involves watching people perform their actual work.

It is useful when stakeholders have difficulty describing a process accurately or when informal practices differ from documented procedures.

### Passive Observation

The analyst observes without directly participating in the activity.

### Active Observation

The analyst participates in the activity to understand its practical details.

### Example

Employees report that invoice processing takes ten minutes. Observation reveals that they spend additional time switching between systems and waiting for approvals.

Observation uncovers operational details that interviews alone may not reveal.

## 7. Document Analysis

Document analysis examines existing information to understand business processes, rules, constraints, and requirements.

Potential sources include:

* Policies.
* Procedures.
* Contracts.
* Reports.
* Existing requirements.
* System manuals.
* Regulations.
* Forms.
* Process documentation.
* Customer complaints.

Documents may be incomplete, outdated, contradictory, or inconsistent with actual practice. Findings should therefore be validated with relevant stakeholders.

## 8. Brainstorming

Brainstorming generates a range of ideas or possible solutions.

A useful brainstorming session separates idea generation from evaluation.

Participants first produce ideas without prematurely rejecting them. The ideas are then clarified, grouped, assessed, and prioritized.

Brainstorming supports creativity but does not independently establish whether an idea is feasible or valuable.

## 9. SWOT Analysis

SWOT examines internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats.

| Category      | Meaning                       | Example               |
| ------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Strengths     | Internal advantages           | Experienced staff     |
| Weaknesses    | Internal disadvantages        | Manual reporting      |
| Opportunities | External favorable conditions | Growing market demand |
| Threats       | External risks                | New competitors       |

SWOT helps organizations understand their strategic position and identify areas requiring investigation.

## 10. PESTLE Analysis

PESTLE examines external environmental factors.

* **Political:** Government priorities and political stability.
* **Economic:** Inflation, interest rates, and economic growth.
* **Social:** Demographics, customer behavior, and social expectations.
* **Technological:** Emerging technology and infrastructure.
* **Legal:** Laws, regulations, and contractual obligations.
* **Environmental:** Climate, sustainability, and environmental requirements.

Example: A logistics company evaluating expansion may use PESTLE to understand fuel prices, transportation regulations, technology adoption, and environmental restrictions.

## 11. Root-Cause Analysis

Root-cause analysis investigates the underlying causes of a problem rather than treating only its symptoms.

### Five Whys

The Five Whys technique repeatedly asks why a problem occurred.

Example:

**Problem:** Customer orders are frequently delayed.

1. Why? Orders wait for inventory confirmation.
2. Why? Inventory records are often outdated.
3. Why? Stock updates are entered manually.
4. Why? Warehouse systems are not integrated.
5. Why? Integration was excluded from the original system design.

The investigation suggests a potential underlying cause. Further evidence is needed before deciding on the final solution.

### Fishbone Diagram

A fishbone, or cause-and-effect, diagram organizes possible causes into categories.

Common categories include people, processes, technology, materials, measurement, and environment.

It helps teams investigate a problem systematically without assuming that the first explanation is correct.

## 12. Process Modeling

Process modeling represents activities, decisions, events, and information flows.

Common approaches include flowcharts and Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN).

Process models can help analysts:

* Understand existing workflows.
* Identify bottlenecks.
* Clarify responsibilities.
* Discover redundant activities.
* Document exceptions.
* Compare current and future processes.

A model should be detailed enough for its intended audience without introducing unnecessary complexity.

## 13. Data Analysis

Data analysis examines information to identify patterns, trends, relationships, and performance problems.

Examples include:

* Descriptive statistics.
* Trend analysis.
* Segmentation.
* Frequency analysis.
* Correlation analysis.
* Outlier detection.
* KPI comparisons.

Example: A retailer analyzes order cancellations by product category and delivery region to identify where operational improvements may have the greatest impact.

Correlation does not necessarily establish causation. Additional investigation may be required to understand why a pattern occurs.

## 14. Decision Analysis

Decision analysis compares alternatives using defined criteria.

Criteria may include:

* Cost.
* Expected benefits.
* Risk.
* Time.
* Feasibility.
* Compliance.
* Customer impact.

A weighted decision matrix assigns importance weights to criteria and scores alternatives against them.

The scores help structure a decision but should not replace informed judgment or stakeholder approval.

## 15. Prioritization Techniques

Common approaches include:

**MoSCoW:** Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have this time.

**Ranking:** Orders requirements from highest to lowest priority.

**Timeboxing:** Limits the time allocated to an activity or delivery period.

**Weighted scoring:** Evaluates alternatives against weighted criteria.

**Value versus effort:** Compares expected benefits with implementation effort.

Prioritization should reflect business objectives, constraints, dependencies, and stakeholder needs.

## 16. Estimation and Risk Analysis

### Estimation

Estimation predicts the effort, cost, duration, or resources required for an activity.

Techniques include expert judgment, analogous estimation, and parametric estimation.

Estimates involve uncertainty and should be refined as additional information becomes available.

### Risk Analysis

Risk analysis considers the likelihood and consequences of uncertain events.

A qualitative risk matrix may classify risks by likelihood and impact.

A high-impact risk with a high likelihood usually requires more attention than a low-impact, low-likelihood risk, subject to organizational risk policies.

## 17. Benchmarking and Metrics

Benchmarking compares performance against a reference point, such as:

* Historical performance.
* Internal departments.
* Industry standards.
* Competitors, where reliable information is available.
* Recognized best practices.

Metrics provide evidence for evaluating performance.

For example, a support department may compare its average resolution time with its previous quarter and an appropriate industry benchmark.

Comparisons should account for differences in operating conditions and measurement definitions.

## 18. Practical Case Study

### Scenario: Improving a Warehouse

A distribution company experiences frequent picking errors and delayed shipments.

The Business Analyst:

1. Interviews warehouse employees.
2. Observes the picking process.
3. Reviews error reports and operating procedures.
4. Maps the current process.
5. Uses root-cause analysis to investigate errors.
6. Analyzes errors by product and shift.
7. Conducts a workshop to generate improvements.
8. Prioritizes changes by value, effort, and risk.
9. Defines performance measures.
10. Evaluates results after implementation.

Potential recommendations might include barcode scanning, improved product labeling, employee training, or process redesign.

The final recommendation should follow evidence-based analysis rather than an assumption that technology alone will solve the problem.

## 19. Practice Questions

### Question 1

Which technique is especially useful for understanding how employees actually perform a task?

A. Observation.

B. Financial forecasting only.

C. Organizational branding.

D. Market segmentation only.

**Correct answer: A**

Explanation: Observation reveals actual behavior, workarounds, and process details.

### Question 2

Which technique examines strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats?

A. PESTLE.

B. SWOT.

C. MoSCoW.

D. Five Whys.

**Correct answer: B**

Explanation: SWOT examines internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats.

### Question 3

Which technique is most suitable for examining political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental factors?

A. PESTLE.

B. Fishbone only.

C. User story mapping only.

D. Traceability matrix.

**Correct answer: A**

Explanation: PESTLE provides a framework for analyzing the external environment.

### Question 4

Why should an analyst avoid immediately accepting the first proposed cause of a problem?

A. The first explanation is always correct.

B. The underlying cause may differ from the visible symptom.

C. Root-cause analysis is unnecessary.

D. Problems never have multiple causes.

**Correct answer: B**

Explanation: Root-cause analysis investigates the reasons a problem occurs.

### Question 5

When is a workshop especially useful?

A. When stakeholders need to collaborate on a shared outcome.

B. When no objective exists.

C. When decisions must never be documented.

D. When all participants must work independently without discussion.

**Correct answer: A**

Explanation: Workshops support collaboration, shared understanding, prioritization, and decision-making.

### Question 6

What is an important limitation of a weighted decision matrix?

A. It cannot compare alternatives.

B. Its scores depend on the selected criteria, weights, and judgments.

C. It always produces a perfect decision.

D. It eliminates uncertainty.

**Correct answer: B**

Explanation: A decision matrix structures comparison but depends on the quality of its assumptions and inputs.

### Question 7

A retailer discovers that cancellations are concentrated in one delivery region. What should the analyst do next?

A. Assume the delivery team is responsible.

B. Investigate relevant causes and validate the pattern with additional evidence.

C. Delete the cancellation data.

D. Immediately replace every delivery system.

**Correct answer: B**

Explanation: Data patterns guide investigation but do not, by themselves, establish causation.
