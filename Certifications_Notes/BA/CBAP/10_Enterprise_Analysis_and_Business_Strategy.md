# 10 — Enterprise Analysis and Business Strategy

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise Analysis examines business needs, organizational capabilities, strategic opportunities, and potential initiatives at the enterprise level.

For a CBAP-level analyst, enterprise thinking means understanding how individual projects, processes, systems, and investments contribute to broader organizational objectives.

This module covers enterprise strategy, capability assessment, business architecture, portfolio alignment, investment evaluation, and strategic governance.

## 2. Enterprise Analysis Fundamentals

Enterprise Analysis considers the organization as an interconnected system rather than a collection of isolated projects.

It helps determine:

* Why change is necessary.
* Which business needs deserve attention.
* What strategic outcomes are expected.
* Which capabilities must change.
* What initiatives could address the need.
* Whether the organization can support the change.
* How expected value will be measured.

Enterprise analysis can occur before a project is formally initiated and may influence multiple projects or programs.

## 3. Business Strategy

Business strategy describes how an organization intends to achieve its objectives and create value.

Strategic choices may involve:

* Market positioning.
* Customer segments.
* Products and services.
* Operational efficiency.
* Innovation.
* Partnerships.
* Geographic expansion.
* Digital transformation.
* Cost leadership or differentiation.

A business analyst should understand strategic priorities sufficiently to assess whether proposed changes contribute to them.

### 3.1 Strategic Alignment

Strategic alignment connects initiatives to organizational goals.

Example:

Enterprise goal: Improve customer retention.

Supporting objectives:

* Reduce service resolution time.
* Improve product reliability.
* Increase customer satisfaction.

Potential initiatives:

* Redesign customer support processes.
* Improve defect tracking.
* Introduce customer analytics.

Each initiative should have a credible relationship to the intended outcome.

## 4. Business Architecture

Business architecture represents the structure and relationships of important business elements.

It may include:

* Capabilities.
* Value streams.
* Business processes.
* Organizational units.
* Information concepts.
* Business services.
* Stakeholders.
* Business rules.

### 4.1 Capability-Based Planning

A business capability describes what an organization must be able to do to achieve its objectives.

Examples:

* Manage customer relationships.
* Fulfill orders.
* Forecast demand.
* Manage supplier contracts.
* Analyze operational performance.

Capabilities are relatively stable compared with the processes or technologies used to implement them.

### 4.2 Capability Maps

A capability map organizes capabilities into a structured representation.

A retail capability map might include:

**Customer management**

* Customer onboarding.
* Customer service.
* Loyalty management.

**Supply chain**

* Demand planning.
* Procurement.
* Inventory management.
* Distribution.

**Enterprise management**

* Financial planning.
* Human resources.
* Risk management.
* Performance reporting.

Capability maps help identify duplication, missing capabilities, and areas where investment may generate strategic value.

## 5. Value Streams

A value stream describes the stages through which an organization delivers value to a stakeholder.

Example: Order-to-delivery value stream.

1. Customer discovers a product.
2. Customer places an order.
3. Payment is authorized.
4. Inventory is allocated.
5. Order is fulfilled.
6. Delivery is completed.
7. Customer receives support when needed.

Value-stream analysis identifies delays, handoffs, waste, and opportunities to improve the overall outcome rather than optimize only one department.

## 6. Enterprise Capability Assessment

Capability assessment determines the organization's current ability to perform an activity and compares it with the desired future state.

### 6.1 Assessment Dimensions

Possible dimensions include:

* People and skills.
* Processes.
* Technology.
* Information.
* Governance.
* Performance.
* Organizational maturity.

### 6.2 Capability Maturity

An organization may assess maturity using a defined scale, such as:

1. Initial.
2. Developing.
3. Defined.
4. Managed.
5. Optimized.

These labels are illustrative, not a universal BABOK maturity scale. Organizations should define observable criteria for each level.

For example, a developing analytics capability may rely on disconnected spreadsheets, while a managed capability may use governed data sources, standardized definitions, and monitored reporting processes.

### 6.3 Capability Heat Maps

A capability heat map visualizes areas of strength and weakness.

It can combine:

* Current maturity.
* Strategic importance.
* Performance gaps.
* Risk.
* Investment priority.

A strategically important capability with low maturity may deserve attention, but investment decisions should also consider dependencies, costs, and alternative approaches.

## 7. Business Cases and Investment Decisions

A business case justifies an initiative by explaining its rationale, expected value, costs, risks, alternatives, and feasibility.

### 7.1 Business Case Components

A comprehensive business case may include:

* Executive summary.
* Business need.
* Strategic alignment.
* Current-state findings.
* Proposed outcomes.
* Options considered.
* Cost estimates.
* Expected benefits.
* Risks and assumptions.
* Feasibility.
* Implementation approach.
* Dependencies.
* Success measures.
* Recommendation.

### 7.2 Options Analysis

Common options include:

* Do nothing.
* Improve the current state.
* Buy a solution.
* Build a solution.
* Outsource.
* Partner with another organization.
* Deliver a phased transformation.

The "do nothing" option provides a useful reference for understanding the cost and consequences of inaction.

### 7.3 Investment Prioritization

An organization may rank initiatives by:

* Strategic contribution.
* Expected return.
* Risk reduction.
* Regulatory necessity.
* Urgency.
* Resource demand.
* Interdependencies.
* Time to benefit.

Portfolio decisions should reflect enterprise priorities rather than simply approving the projects with the most enthusiastic sponsors.

## 8. Portfolio, Program, and Project Relationships

These concepts operate at different levels.

**Portfolio:** A collection of investments or initiatives managed to achieve strategic objectives.

**Program:** Related projects and activities coordinated to achieve benefits that may not be available through independent management.

**Project:** A temporary initiative that delivers a defined product, service, or result.

Enterprise analysis helps establish the rationale and strategic fit of initiatives before detailed delivery planning.

The business analyst may contribute at all three levels, depending on organizational responsibilities.

## 9. Enterprise Risk and Dependencies

Enterprise-level risks may affect several business units or initiatives simultaneously.

Examples:

* Major supplier failure.
* Regulatory changes.
* Cybersecurity incidents.
* Workforce shortages.
* Data governance weaknesses.
* Legacy-system constraints.
* Inconsistent enterprise standards.

Dependencies may include shared platforms, common data, specialist teams, and prerequisite capabilities.

An enterprise view helps avoid local decisions that create broader operational problems.

## 10. Organizational Change and Governance

Enterprise change requires coordinated decision-making across affected groups.

Governance should clarify:

* Decision authority.
* Accountability.
* Approval criteria.
* Risk ownership.
* Funding responsibilities.
* Escalation routes.
* Performance reporting.
* Benefits ownership.

### 10.1 Decision Rights

Not every decision belongs to the business analyst.

The analyst investigates needs, develops alternatives, evaluates evidence, and recommends actions. Authorized stakeholders make decisions within their governance responsibilities.

This distinction is important for CBAP-level judgment questions.

## 11. Measuring Strategic Performance

Strategic performance indicators connect enterprise objectives to measurable outcomes.

Examples include:

* Revenue growth.
* Customer retention.
* Operating margin.
* Delivery reliability.
* Product quality.
* Employee retention.
* Compliance performance.
* Innovation cycle time.

### 11.1 Balanced Scorecard

The Balanced Scorecard commonly examines four perspectives:

1. Financial.
2. Customer.
3. Internal business processes.
4. Learning and growth.

This helps avoid judging strategy solely through short-term financial measures.

For example, a process improvement may increase short-term costs while building employee capabilities that support future growth.

## 12. Advanced Case Study: Enterprise Digital Transformation

### Scenario

A regional distribution company operates separate systems for sales, inventory, purchasing, and finance.

Management wants a unified digital platform.

### Enterprise Analysis

**Business need:** Improve operational visibility and reduce duplicated work.

**Strategic alignment:** Support reliable delivery, improved margins, and scalable growth.

**Capability assessment:** Inventory visibility and cross-functional reporting are weak; financial control capabilities are relatively mature.

**Value-stream analysis:** Order processing includes duplicated data entry and repeated manual checks.

**Options:** Integrate current systems, implement a commercial enterprise platform, or replace systems in phases.

**Risks:** Data migration, operational disruption, employee resistance, and supplier integration.

**Governance:** Define executive sponsorship, data ownership, business decision rights, and benefits accountability.

**Measures:** Order cycle time, inventory accuracy, reporting effort, delivery reliability, and total operating cost.

### Recommendation

Develop a phased transformation roadmap, standardize key data definitions, prioritize high-value integration points, and validate benefits before expanding to later stages.

### Key Lesson

Enterprise analysis aligns individual initiatives with organizational strategy, capabilities, risks, and measurable outcomes.

## 16. Module Summary

Enterprise Analysis connects business needs, strategic objectives, capabilities, value streams, investment decisions, and governance. It enables analysts to assess change at the organizational level, prioritize initiatives based on strategic value, and ensure that projects contribute to sustainable enterprise outcomes.
