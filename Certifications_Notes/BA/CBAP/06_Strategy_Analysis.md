# 06 — Strategy Analysis

## 1. Introduction

Strategy Analysis examines the business need, defines the future state required to address it, assesses risks, and develops a change strategy that enables the organization to transition from its current state to its desired future state.

For advanced business analysts, strategy analysis connects organizational objectives with practical change initiatives. It helps decision-makers understand why change is necessary, what capabilities must evolve, which alternatives are feasible, and how expected value can be achieved.

This module follows the Strategy Analysis knowledge area of the BABOK® Guide, with additional advanced applications for CBAP preparation.

## 2. Understanding the Business Need

A business need is a problem or opportunity that justifies organizational action.

### 2.1 Problem-Based Needs

Examples include:

* Declining revenue.
* Increasing operating costs.
* High customer churn.
* Regulatory non-compliance.
* Inefficient workflows.
* Poor product quality.
* Limited visibility into operational performance.

### 2.2 Opportunity-Based Needs

Examples include:

* Entering a new market.
* Introducing a new product.
* Expanding digital services.
* Automating repetitive work.
* Using predictive analytics.
* Developing strategic partnerships.

### 2.3 Business Need Assessment

The analyst should determine:

* What is happening?
* Why does it matter?
* Who is affected?
* What evidence supports the need?
* What happens if no action is taken?
* What outcomes are desired?
* Which strategic objectives are involved?
* What constraints affect possible responses?

A business need should be distinguished from a preferred solution. For example, a request to implement artificial intelligence may actually reflect a need to reduce forecasting errors.

## 3. Analyzing the Current State

Current-state analysis establishes the organization's existing position and capabilities.

### 3.1 Areas of Investigation

Examine:

* Business processes.
* Organizational structure.
* Products and services.
* Information and data.
* Technology architecture.
* People and skills.
* Policies and governance.
* Performance measures.
* Financial resources.
* External relationships.

### 3.2 Current-State Performance

Useful evidence includes:

* Revenue and profitability.
* Cost per transaction.
* Cycle time.
* Defect rates.
* Customer satisfaction.
* Employee productivity.
* Compliance incidents.
* Service availability.
* Market share.

The analyst should establish reliable baselines and understand the factors influencing performance.

### 3.3 Current-State Limitations

Limitations may be caused by:

* Legacy technology.
* Fragmented information.
* Organizational silos.
* Skills shortages.
* Ineffective incentives.
* Poor process design.
* Regulatory restrictions.
* Limited investment capacity.

Identifying the underlying constraints prevents a strategy from relying on capabilities the organization does not possess.

## 4. Environmental Analysis

External conditions influence the organization's opportunities, threats, and strategic choices.

### 4.1 PESTLE Analysis

PESTLE examines six environmental dimensions.

| Dimension     | Examples                                                       |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Political     | Government policies, trade restrictions, political stability   |
| Economic      | Inflation, interest rates, purchasing power, economic growth   |
| Social        | Demographics, customer expectations, cultural changes          |
| Technological | AI adoption, automation, cybersecurity, digital infrastructure |
| Legal         | Employment law, privacy regulations, contractual obligations   |
| Environmental | Energy use, climate exposure, sustainability requirements      |

PESTLE helps identify external factors that may affect the business need or proposed change.

### 4.2 SWOT Analysis

SWOT organizes internal and external factors.

* **Strengths:** Internal advantages.
* **Weaknesses:** Internal limitations.
* **Opportunities:** Favorable external conditions.
* **Threats:** External risks or pressures.

Example for a logistics company:

* Strength: Established regional distribution network.
* Weakness: Limited demand forecasting.
* Opportunity: Growing e-commerce demand.
* Threat: Increasing fuel costs and competition.

SWOT becomes more useful when findings are supported by evidence and translated into strategic actions.

### 4.3 Porter's Five Forces

Porter's Five Forces examines competitive pressures through:

1. Competitive rivalry.
2. Threat of new entrants.
3. Threat of substitutes.
4. Bargaining power of buyers.
5. Bargaining power of suppliers.

This framework can help determine whether a market opportunity is attractive and what capabilities may be needed to compete.

## 5. Defining the Future State

The future state describes the desired condition after the proposed change.

It should explain:

* What the organization intends to achieve.
* Which capabilities will be required.
* What business processes will change.
* Which stakeholders will be affected.
* What performance improvements are expected.
* What constraints remain.
* How success will be measured.

### 5.1 Strategic Objectives and Outcomes

A strategic objective should be specific enough to guide decisions.

Weak objective:

"Improve customer service."

Stronger objective:

"Increase customer satisfaction from the established baseline to at least 90% within 12 months while maintaining the approved service cost per customer."

The target and measurement method should be validated with decision-makers.

### 5.2 Future-State Capabilities

A future state may require new or improved capabilities in:

* Data analytics.
* Customer relationship management.
* Supply chain planning.
* Digital service delivery.
* Risk management.
* Quality assurance.
* Workforce development.

Capability-based analysis focuses on what the organization must be able to do, rather than immediately selecting specific technologies.

## 6. Gap Analysis

Gap analysis identifies differences between the current and future states.

| Capability         | Current State                | Future State                    | Required Change                         |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Forecasting        | Manual spreadsheet estimates | Evidence-based demand forecasts | Analytics and process redesign          |
| Reporting          | Monthly reporting            | Timely operational reporting    | Data integration and automation         |
| Quality management | Reactive defect handling     | Preventive quality controls     | Process and governance improvements     |
| Customer support   | Fragmented customer records  | Integrated customer information | Systems integration and data governance |

The gaps help determine the scope, cost, dependencies, and risks of the change.

### 6.1 Gap Prioritization

Not every gap needs to be addressed immediately.

Prioritize based on:

* Strategic importance.
* Business value.
* Risk.
* Dependencies.
* Cost.
* Feasibility.
* Time sensitivity.
* Organizational readiness.

## 7. Assessing Risks

Strategy Analysis evaluates uncertainty that may affect the desired future state or the change strategy.

### 7.1 Strategic Risks

Examples include:

* Market demand does not develop as expected.
* Competitors respond aggressively.
* Regulations change.
* Critical skills are unavailable.
* Technology investments become obsolete.
* Expected benefits fail to materialize.
* Stakeholders resist the transformation.

### 7.2 Risk Assessment

For each major risk, identify:

* Cause.
* Potential event.
* Consequence.
* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Existing controls.
* Proposed response.
* Owner.
* Monitoring indicators.

Risk ratings should follow the organization's approved criteria rather than arbitrary labels.

### 7.3 Risk Responses

Typical responses include:

* Avoiding the risk.
* Reducing likelihood or impact.
* Transferring or sharing exposure where appropriate.
* Accepting the risk with informed authorization.
* Exploiting an opportunity when the risk represents potential upside.

The selected response should be proportionate to the risk and the organization's risk tolerance.

## 8. Change Strategy

A change strategy describes how the organization will move from its current state toward the desired future state.

It may define:

* Scope and boundaries.
* Major initiatives.
* Delivery approach.
* Required capabilities.
* Resource needs.
* Dependencies.
* Implementation sequence.
* Governance.
* Risk responses.
* Communication and training.
* Performance measures.

### 8.1 Strategic Alternatives

Possible alternatives include:

* Maintaining the current state.
* Improving existing processes.
* Purchasing a commercial solution.
* Developing a custom solution.
* Outsourcing selected activities.
* Forming a partnership.
* Conducting a limited pilot.
* Combining multiple interventions.

The analyst should compare viable alternatives rather than assuming that a single proposed option is the only possibility.

### 8.2 Transition States

Large transformations may require intermediate states.

For example:

Current state: Manual inventory planning.

Transition state 1: Standardized inventory data and reporting.

Transition state 2: Automated replenishment recommendations.

Future state: Integrated demand forecasting and inventory optimization.

Transition states allow benefits to emerge incrementally while reducing implementation risk.

## 9. Feasibility Assessment

Feasibility determines whether a proposed change is practical and justified.

### 9.1 Common Feasibility Dimensions

* Technical feasibility.
* Financial feasibility.
* Operational feasibility.
* Schedule feasibility.
* Legal and regulatory feasibility.
* Organizational feasibility.
* Resource feasibility.

### 9.2 Cost-Benefit Analysis

A cost-benefit analysis compares expected costs with expected benefits.

Suppose a proposed system has:

* Initial implementation cost: 500,000 monetary units.
* Annual operating cost: 80,000.
* Expected annual financial benefits: 240,000.

Annual net benefit after operating costs is:

240,000 − 80,000 = 160,000.

A simple payback estimate based on the initial investment and annual net benefit is:

500,000 / 160,000 = 3.125 years.

This simplified calculation excludes discounting, changing cash flows, residual value, and other financial considerations. A full investment decision may require net present value, internal rate of return, sensitivity analysis, or other approved methods.

### 9.3 Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis examines how conclusions change when assumptions vary.

For example:

* What if adoption is 20% lower than expected?
* What if implementation costs rise by 15%?
* What if benefits arrive six months later?
* What if demand is substantially higher than forecast?

This helps decision-makers understand which assumptions matter most.

## 10. Organizational Readiness

Organizational readiness assesses whether the organization can adopt and sustain the proposed change.

Consider:

* Leadership support.
* Workforce skills.
* Stakeholder commitment.
* Available resources.
* Process maturity.
* Technology readiness.
* Cultural compatibility.
* Change fatigue.
* Communication effectiveness.

Readiness gaps may require training, leadership engagement, pilot programs, revised incentives, or a phased implementation.

## 11. Strategic Alignment and Portfolio Decisions

An organization may have more potential initiatives than available resources.

Strategy Analysis supports portfolio decisions by comparing initiatives against common criteria.

Possible criteria include:

* Strategic contribution.
* Expected value.
* Risk reduction.
* Regulatory necessity.
* Resource requirements.
* Dependencies.
* Time to benefit.
* Organizational capacity.

An initiative with a strong local business case may still be deprioritized if it conflicts with enterprise priorities or depends on unavailable capabilities.

## 12. Advanced Case Study: Manufacturing Digital Transformation

### Scenario

A manufacturer faces increasing production costs, inconsistent quality, and limited visibility into machine performance.

Management proposes implementing an AI-based predictive maintenance platform.

### Strategy Analysis

**Business need:** Reduce unplanned downtime and improve production reliability.

**Current state:** Maintenance is mainly reactive, equipment data is fragmented, and defect analysis is inconsistent.

**Environmental factors:** Competitive pressure, rising energy costs, supplier availability, and increasing automation opportunities.

**Future state:** Reliable equipment monitoring, improved maintenance planning, and measurable reductions in downtime.

**Gap analysis:** The organization lacks standardized sensor data, integrated maintenance records, and staff expertise in predictive analytics.

**Alternatives:**

1. Improve preventive maintenance procedures.
2. Standardize equipment and maintenance data.
3. Introduce condition monitoring.
4. Pilot predictive maintenance on selected machines.
5. Deploy an enterprise-wide platform.

**Risks:** Poor data quality, integration difficulties, model errors, workforce resistance, and uncertain financial benefits.

**Change strategy:** Begin with data standardization and a limited pilot, validate benefits, train relevant staff, and expand only if the evidence supports the investment.

**Measures:** Unplanned downtime, maintenance cost, equipment availability, false alarms, and production quality.

### Key Lesson

A strategic recommendation should address the underlying business need, fit the organization's capabilities, and demonstrate a credible path to measurable value.

## 13. Knowledge Check

### Questions

1. What is the purpose of Strategy Analysis?
2. How does current-state analysis support strategic decisions?
3. What does PESTLE examine?
4. How does SWOT differ from PESTLE?
5. Why is capability-based thinking useful?
6. What is the purpose of gap analysis?
7. Why should multiple strategic alternatives be evaluated?
8. What is the difference between a risk and an assumption?
9. How does sensitivity analysis support investment decisions?
10. Why is organizational readiness important?
11. What is the purpose of a transition state?
12. Why might a technically feasible solution still be unsuitable?

### Answers

1. To understand the business need, define the future state, assess risks, and develop a suitable change strategy.
2. It establishes evidence about current capabilities, performance, constraints, and problems.
3. Political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental factors.
4. PESTLE examines the external environment; SWOT organizes internal strengths and weaknesses alongside external opportunities and threats.
5. It identifies what the organization must be able to do without prematurely prescribing a particular solution.
6. It identifies the differences between current and desired future capabilities.
7. Alternatives may differ in value, cost, risk, feasibility, and strategic fit.
8. A risk is an uncertain event or condition that may affect objectives; an assumption is treated as true for analysis or planning but requires appropriate validation.
9. It reveals how changes in uncertain inputs affect expected outcomes.
10. The organization must have sufficient leadership support, skills, resources, and capacity to adopt the change.
11. It provides an intermediate condition that supports a controlled transition toward the future state.
12. It may be financially unjustified, operationally unsuitable, legally restricted, or inconsistent with strategic priorities.

## 16. Module Summary

Strategy Analysis connects business needs with organizational direction and practical change. It combines current-state assessment, environmental analysis, future-state definition, gap analysis, risk assessment, feasibility evaluation, and change strategy development. The objective is to recommend a viable path toward measurable business value while accounting for constraints, uncertainty, and organizational readiness.
