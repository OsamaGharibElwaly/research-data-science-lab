# Business Analysis Competencies and Ethics

## 1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain the competencies required for effective business analysis.
* Understand analytical thinking and problem-solving.
* Evaluate communication, collaboration, and facilitation skills.
* Explain business knowledge, systems thinking, and behavioral characteristics.
* Apply professional ethics to business analysis situations.
* Identify conflicts of interest and confidentiality risks.
* Understand professional responsibility and continuous development.
* Prepare for competency- and ethics-related CCBA questions.

## 2. Introduction

Business analysis requires more than writing requirements. A business analyst must understand business needs, investigate problems, work with stakeholders, evaluate alternatives, communicate findings, and support changes that create value.

Professional competence combines knowledge, practical skills, judgment, behavior, and experience.

An effective business analyst should be able to:

1. Understand a business problem.
2. Gather reliable evidence.
3. Analyze complex information.
4. Communicate findings clearly.
5. Work with stakeholders who have different priorities.
6. Make recommendations supported by evidence.
7. Recognize limitations and seek assistance when necessary.
8. Act ethically and protect confidential information.
9. Continue developing professional skills.

## 3. Analytical Thinking and Problem-Solving

Analytical thinking helps business analysts examine information, identify patterns, understand relationships, and draw justified conclusions.

### 3.1 Critical Thinking

Critical thinking involves evaluating claims, assumptions, evidence, and conclusions.

A critical thinker asks:

* What evidence supports this statement?
* Is the information reliable?
* Are there alternative explanations?
* What assumptions are being made?
* What evidence would contradict this conclusion?
* Are we confusing correlation with causation?

Example:

A manager believes that introducing a new CRM system will increase sales by 20%.

The analyst should investigate whether sales performance is actually limited by CRM functionality. Other causes may include pricing, product quality, market conditions, lead quality, or sales training.

### 3.2 Systems Thinking

Systems thinking examines how components interact within a larger system.

A business process may involve people, technology, policies, data, suppliers, customers, and organizational incentives.

Changing one component can affect others.

Example:

Automating invoice approval may reduce finance workload but increase supplier complaints if payment exceptions become harder to resolve.

Systems thinking helps identify dependencies, unintended consequences, and broader business impacts.

### 3.3 Problem-Solving

A structured problem-solving approach includes:

1. Define the problem.
2. Establish the current state.
3. Identify evidence and constraints.
4. Investigate root causes.
5. Develop alternatives.
6. Evaluate options.
7. Recommend an approach.
8. Measure the result.

Avoid selecting a solution before understanding the problem.

### 3.4 Decision-Making

Business analysts support decisions by presenting evidence, options, risks, and trade-offs.

The analyst may recommend an option, but the final decision may belong to a sponsor, product owner, executive, or other authorized stakeholder.

Good decision support includes:

* Decision criteria.
* Supporting evidence.
* Assumptions.
* Risks.
* Alternatives.
* Expected outcomes.
* Uncertainties.
* Required approvals.

## 4. Learning and Knowledge Acquisition

Business analysts frequently work in unfamiliar domains.

They may need to learn industry terminology, operational procedures, regulations, software systems, and stakeholder expectations.

Useful learning techniques include:

* Interviews with subject-matter experts.
* Document analysis.
* Direct observation.
* Reviewing historical reports.
* Studying existing systems.
* Researching relevant standards.
* Creating concept maps.
* Testing assumptions through small investigations.

A competent analyst distinguishes between established facts, stakeholder opinions, assumptions, and unresolved questions.

### Example

When joining a logistics project, an analyst should learn the meaning of terms such as shipment, consignment, delivery exception, warehouse receipt, and proof of delivery before modeling the process.

Misunderstanding domain terminology can produce incorrect requirements even when the analyst's technical work is strong.

## 5. Behavioral Characteristics

Professional behavior influences the quality of analysis and stakeholder relationships.

### 5.1 Integrity

Integrity means acting honestly and consistently with professional responsibilities.

Examples include:

* Reporting unfavorable findings accurately.
* Acknowledging mistakes.
* Avoiding misleading performance claims.
* Disclosing relevant conflicts of interest.
* Not manipulating evidence to support a preferred solution.

### 5.2 Adaptability

Adaptability is the ability to adjust to changing circumstances while preserving analytical quality.

Business analysts may need to change their techniques when:

* Stakeholders are unavailable.
* Requirements are uncertain.
* Regulations change.
* Project priorities shift.
* New evidence invalidates an assumption.

Adaptability does not mean accepting every change without evaluation.

### 5.3 Organization and Time Management

Business analysts often manage multiple deliverables and stakeholders.

Useful practices include:

* Maintaining a requirements register.
* Tracking decisions and actions.
* Recording open questions.
* Prioritizing high-impact issues.
* Scheduling stakeholder reviews.
* Managing dependencies.
* Keeping documentation current.

### 5.4 Emotional Intelligence

Emotional intelligence supports awareness of emotions, communication styles, and interpersonal dynamics.

It can help analysts:

* Recognize stakeholder concerns.
* Listen without becoming defensive.
* Respond constructively to criticism.
* Manage difficult discussions.
* Separate personal disagreement from the business issue.

### 5.5 Professional Confidence

Confidence means presenting evidence-based conclusions clearly while remaining open to correction.

An analyst should not pretend to know everything. When uncertain, the analyst should clarify the uncertainty, investigate, and seek suitable expertise.

## 6. Communication Skills

Business analysts communicate with technical and non-technical audiences.

Communication methods may include:

* Interviews.
* Workshops.
* Written requirements.
* Reports.
* Presentations.
* Process diagrams.
* Dashboards.
* Decision records.
* Informal discussions.

### 6.1 Active Listening

Active listening involves understanding the speaker's meaning rather than simply waiting to respond.

Useful behaviors include:

* Asking clarifying questions.
* Summarizing what was heard.
* Confirming important details.
* Identifying contradictions.
* Avoiding premature conclusions.
* Distinguishing facts from assumptions.

Example:

A stakeholder says, "The reporting system is useless."

A useful response is:

"Can you show me which reporting tasks are difficult and what information you cannot obtain?"

This converts a broad complaint into an investigable business need.

### 6.2 Audience Adaptation

Different stakeholders require different levels of detail.

Executives may need business value, risk, cost, and strategic implications.

Developers may need functional behavior, interfaces, data rules, and acceptance criteria.

End users may need process flows, examples, and explanations of how their work will change.

The content should remain accurate while its presentation changes for the audience.

### 6.3 Written Communication

Effective business analysis documents should be:

* Clear.
* Structured.
* Consistent.
* Relevant to their audience.
* Traceable where appropriate.
* Free from unnecessary ambiguity.
* Maintained as decisions change.

A lengthy document is not automatically a good document. The purpose is to support understanding, decisions, implementation, or evaluation.

## 7. Interaction and Collaboration

Business analysts often work with stakeholders who have conflicting interests.

Examples:

* Finance prioritizes cost control.
* Operations prioritizes efficiency.
* Compliance prioritizes regulatory adherence.
* Customers prioritize convenience.
* IT prioritizes maintainability and security.

The analyst should understand each perspective and help stakeholders make informed trade-offs.

### Facilitation

Facilitation helps a group accomplish a defined objective.

A useful workshop structure includes:

1. Define the purpose.
2. Establish participants and roles.
3. Share relevant context.
4. Agree on discussion rules.
5. Explore needs and alternatives.
6. Resolve or document disagreements.
7. Confirm decisions and actions.

The facilitator should manage the process without improperly imposing a personal preference on the outcome.

### Negotiation

Negotiation seeks an acceptable resolution between parties with different needs.

Effective negotiation involves:

* Understanding each party's underlying interests.
* Identifying shared objectives.
* Separating essential requirements from preferences.
* Comparing options.
* Making trade-offs explicit.
* Recording decisions.
* Escalating unresolved issues appropriately.

## 8. Business Knowledge

Business analysts need enough domain knowledge to understand the environment in which change occurs.

Relevant knowledge may include:

* Business processes.
* Organizational structure.
* Financial concepts.
* Industry regulations.
* Products and services.
* Customer behavior.
* Market conditions.
* Operational performance.
* Technology capabilities.

An analyst working on manufacturing may need to understand production yield, defects, inventory, capacity, and quality control.

An analyst working in banking may need to understand transactions, authorization, reconciliation, fraud controls, and applicable regulatory obligations.

The required depth depends on the role and the complexity of the initiative.

## 9. Tools and Technology Knowledge

Business analysts use tools to support communication, modeling, requirements management, and decision-making.

Examples include:

* Spreadsheets.
* SQL and reporting tools.
* Process-modeling software.
* Diagramming tools.
* Requirements-management platforms.
* Project-management tools.
* Collaboration software.
* Prototyping tools.
* Issue-tracking systems.

Tool proficiency is valuable, but tool selection should follow the business need.

A sophisticated modeling platform is not necessarily better than a simple diagram when the latter adequately communicates the process.

Analysts should also understand relevant technology constraints, integrations, data quality, access controls, and system limitations.

## 10. Professional Ethics

Ethics concerns the principles that guide responsible professional behavior.

Business analysis decisions can affect customers, employees, suppliers, shareholders, and the public.

Ethical practice requires the analyst to consider the consequences of recommendations and avoid misleading, unfair, or irresponsible behavior.

### 10.1 Honesty

Present findings accurately.

Do not:

* Invent stakeholder approval.
* Hide material risks.
* Misrepresent expected benefits.
* Manipulate measurements.
* Claim that a solution meets requirements when evidence shows otherwise.

### 10.2 Confidentiality

Protect information entrusted to you.

Examples include:

* Customer records.
* Employee information.
* Financial forecasts.
* Commercial contracts.
* Credentials.
* Internal system details.
* Unreleased business plans.

Share information only with authorized recipients and for legitimate purposes.

Follow applicable organizational policies and legal requirements.

### 10.3 Objectivity

Objectivity means evaluating evidence and alternatives fairly.

An analyst should not recommend a vendor merely because of a personal relationship or accept unsupported claims because they come from a senior executive.

Where complete neutrality is difficult, the analyst should disclose relevant interests and use transparent evaluation criteria.

### 10.4 Competence

Work within your capabilities and seek assistance when necessary.

For example, a business analyst identifying a significant cybersecurity weakness should document the concern and involve appropriately qualified security professionals rather than pretending to conduct a complete specialist assessment without the necessary expertise.

### 10.5 Accountability

Take responsibility for professional work, decisions within your authority, and the accuracy of deliverables.

Accountability includes correcting errors, communicating relevant limitations, and following through on agreed actions.

## 11. Conflicts of Interest

A conflict of interest occurs when personal, financial, or other interests may improperly influence professional judgment or create the appearance of compromised impartiality.

Example:

An analyst participates in selecting a software vendor while a close relative works for one of the competing vendors.

Appropriate actions may include:

1. Identify the conflict.
2. Disclose it to the appropriate authority.
3. Follow organizational policy.
4. Avoid participating in decisions where impartiality may be compromised.
5. Document relevant steps when required.

A conflict does not always mean misconduct has occurred. The concern is whether the conflict is managed transparently and appropriately.

## 12. Ethical Decision-Making Framework

When faced with an ethical dilemma:

1. **Identify the issue:** What action or decision raises concern?
2. **Gather facts:** What is known, and what remains uncertain?
3. **Identify stakeholders:** Who may be affected?
4. **Review obligations:** What laws, policies, contracts, and professional standards apply?
5. **Consider alternatives:** What actions are available?
6. **Assess consequences:** What harms, benefits, and risks may result?
7. **Seek guidance:** Consult appropriate experts or authorities when needed.
8. **Act responsibly:** Choose an appropriate course of action.
9. **Document and communicate:** Record material decisions where appropriate.
10. **Review the outcome:** Determine whether further action is necessary.

### Example

A project sponsor asks an analyst to remove an unfavorable performance metric from a report before presenting it to senior management.

The analyst should determine whether the metric is relevant, validate its accuracy, explain its significance, and communicate the results honestly.

The analyst should not knowingly mislead decision-makers to protect a project or individual.

## 13. Responsible Data Handling

Business analysis frequently involves collecting, reviewing, and sharing data.

Responsible handling includes:

* Collecting only data needed for legitimate purposes.
* Respecting access restrictions.
* Avoiding unnecessary exposure of personal information.
* Using secure storage and transfer methods.
* Maintaining appropriate data quality.
* Following retention and deletion policies.
* Avoiding unsupported conclusions.
* Considering bias and representativeness.

Example:

A customer analytics project should not expose identifiable customer records to every stakeholder when aggregated data would adequately support the decision.

## 14. Competency Development

Professional development helps analysts maintain and improve their capabilities.

A development plan can include:

* Technical learning.
* Domain education.
* Communication training.
* Professional certifications.
* Mentoring.
* Workshops.
* Reading professional standards.
* Practice projects.
* Feedback and self-assessment.

A useful development cycle is:

Assess current capability → Identify a gap → Set a learning objective → Practice → Seek feedback → Measure improvement → Update the plan.

### Example

A junior analyst struggles to facilitate workshops.

A development plan could include observing an experienced facilitator, preparing a workshop agenda, facilitating a small session, collecting participant feedback, and reviewing what worked.

## 15. Case Study: Biased Vendor Recommendation

### Background

A company is selecting a customer relationship management platform.

The analyst is asked to compare three vendors. One vendor has a lower score against the documented criteria, but a senior manager strongly prefers it.

The analyst also learns that a colleague has a personal relationship with the vendor's sales representative.

### Risks

* Biased evaluation.
* Undisclosed conflicts of interest.
* Inconsistent scoring.
* Inaccurate reporting.
* Loss of stakeholder trust.
* Procurement or governance concerns.

### Appropriate Actions

1. Confirm that the evaluation criteria are relevant and agreed.
2. Validate the evidence and scoring.
3. Disclose relevant conflicts through the appropriate process.
4. Record the reasons behind the recommendation.
5. Present strengths, weaknesses, costs, and risks fairly.
6. Allow authorized decision-makers to make the final choice.
7. Document any justified changes to criteria or weighting.
8. Follow procurement and governance requirements.

### Lessons

Ethical business analysis is not about preventing senior stakeholders from making decisions. It is about ensuring that decisions are informed by reliable evidence and made through appropriate processes.

## 16. Knowledge Check

### Questions

**1. Which behavior demonstrates critical thinking?**

A. Accepting every senior manager's statement.
B. Evaluating claims using evidence and alternative explanations.
C. Choosing the newest technology.
D. Avoiding difficult questions.

**2. What is systems thinking?**

A. Focusing only on individual software components.
B. Examining relationships and interactions within a larger system.
C. Avoiding stakeholder analysis.
D. Measuring only project cost.

**3. A stakeholder says a system is "terrible." What is the best initial response?**

A. Reject the complaint.
B. Immediately replace the system.
C. Ask for examples and clarify the underlying problem.
D. Record the statement as a complete requirement.

**4. What should an analyst do when a conflict of interest may exist?**

A. Ignore it if the project is important.
B. Conceal it until a decision is made.
C. Disclose it appropriately and follow relevant policies.
D. Automatically cancel the project.

**5. Which action violates professional honesty?**

A. Reporting uncertainty.
B. Correcting an error.
C. Hiding material unfavorable evidence to mislead decision-makers.
D. Requesting specialist advice.

**6. What is the purpose of facilitation?**

A. Force all stakeholders to agree with the analyst.
B. Help a group achieve a defined objective through a structured process.
C. Eliminate the need for decisions.
D. Replace business requirements.

**7. What should an analyst do when a task exceeds their competence?**

A. Pretend to be an expert.
B. Ignore the risk.
C. Recognize the limitation and seek appropriate assistance.
D. Remove the issue from the report.

**8. Which practice supports responsible data handling?**

A. Share identifiable data with everyone.
B. Collect all available information regardless of purpose.
C. Limit access to authorized users and legitimate needs.
D. Ignore retention policies.

### Answer Key

1. B — Critical thinking evaluates evidence and assumptions.
2. B — Systems thinking considers relationships among components.
3. C — Clarification converts a vague complaint into an investigable issue.
4. C — Disclosure and appropriate management protect professional integrity.
5. C — Knowingly misleading decision-makers is unethical.
6. B — Facilitation structures group work toward a defined outcome.
7. C — Competence includes recognizing limitations and obtaining suitable expertise.
8. C — Data access should be appropriate to the purpose and authorization.

## 17. Revision Summary

* Business analysis competence combines knowledge, skills, judgment, behavior, and experience.
* Critical thinking evaluates evidence, assumptions, and alternative explanations.
* Systems thinking examines interactions across people, processes, data, and technology.
* Problem-solving should begin with understanding the problem and its causes.
* Communication should be adapted to the audience without compromising accuracy.
* Facilitation and negotiation help stakeholders work through different priorities.
* Integrity, objectivity, confidentiality, competence, and accountability support ethical practice.
* Conflicts of interest should be identified, disclosed, and managed appropriately.
* Responsible data handling protects confidentiality and improves decision quality.
* Professional development should be planned, practiced, and reviewed.
* Ethical decisions should consider facts, stakeholders, obligations, alternatives, and consequences.
