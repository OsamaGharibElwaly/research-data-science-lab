# Business Analysis Strategy

## 1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

* Explain the purpose of business analysis strategy.
* Analyze business needs, organizational context, and desired outcomes.
* Understand current-state and future-state analysis.
* Define business objectives, capabilities, and strategic change.
* Conduct gap analysis and assess risks.
* Compare strategic options and recommend a change strategy.
* Develop a practical business analysis strategy using a case study.

## 2. Introduction to Business Analysis Strategy

Business analysis strategy determines how an organization can move from its current situation toward a desired future state.

It connects business needs with organizational goals and identifies the changes necessary to achieve measurable outcomes.

A business analysis strategy answers questions such as:

* Why is change necessary?
* What business problem or opportunity exists?
* What does success look like?
* What capabilities must the organization develop?
* Which strategic options are available?
* What risks and constraints affect the change?
* How should the organization transition to the future state?

### Example

A university experiences long registration queues and frequent student complaints.

A weak approach would be to immediately purchase a new registration system.

A stronger business analysis strategy would investigate the underlying problem, examine current processes, define measurable objectives, assess available solutions, and recommend an appropriate change.

The problem may involve outdated software, unclear policies, insufficient staff, or unnecessary approval steps. Technology might help, but it is not automatically the correct solution.

## 3. Business Needs, Problems, and Opportunities

### 3.1 Business Need

A business need is a problem or opportunity that motivates an organization to undertake change.

Examples include:

* Reducing operating costs.
* Improving customer satisfaction.
* Complying with new regulations.
* Entering a new market.
* Improving operational efficiency.
* Reducing error rates.
* Increasing revenue.
* Replacing unsupported technology.

A business need should be distinguished from a proposed solution.

| Business need                            | Proposed solution               |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Reduce customer waiting time             | Introduce an appointment system |
| Improve inventory accuracy               | Implement barcode scanning      |
| Reduce invoice errors                    | Automate invoice validation     |
| Increase student registration efficiency | Develop an online portal        |

The need explains why change is required. The solution describes one possible way to address it.

### 3.2 Problems and Opportunities

A problem represents an undesirable current condition.

An opportunity represents a potential improvement or benefit.

For example:

* Problem: 18% of invoices contain errors.
* Opportunity: Automating validation may reduce errors and processing time.

Both can justify business analysis, but they may require different investigation methods.

### 3.3 Root-Cause Analysis

A business analyst should avoid treating symptoms as root causes.

Common techniques include:

**Five Whys:** Repeatedly ask why a problem occurs until the underlying cause becomes clearer.

**Fishbone diagram:** Organize potential causes into categories such as people, processes, technology, policies, and environment.

**Pareto analysis:** Identify the categories responsible for a large proportion of observed problems.

**Process analysis:** Examine workflow steps, handoffs, bottlenecks, and rework.

Example:

Problem: Customer orders are frequently delayed.

1. Why? Orders remain pending.
2. Why? Payment confirmation is delayed.
3. Why? Payment data is imported manually.
4. Why? The systems do not exchange data.
5. Why? Integration requirements were never established.

The investigation suggests a systems integration problem rather than simply insufficient staff.

## 4. Business Goals and Objectives

A business goal describes a desired organizational outcome. An objective makes that outcome sufficiently specific to guide action and evaluation.

Examples:

**Goal:** Improve customer service.

**Objective:** Reduce average customer response time from 12 hours to 4 hours within six months.

A well-defined objective often includes:

* A clear outcome.
* A measurable indicator.
* A baseline.
* A target.
* A time frame.
* An accountable owner.

### SMART Objectives

SMART objectives are:

* Specific.
* Measurable.
* Achievable.
* Relevant.
* Time-bound.

Example:

"Reduce invoice processing time from 3 business days to 1 business day within four months while maintaining an error rate below 2%."

This is more useful than "Improve invoice processing."

## 5. Current-State Analysis

Current-state analysis examines how an organization operates today.

It may cover:

* Business processes.
* Organizational structure.
* Stakeholder responsibilities.
* Existing systems and applications.
* Data flows.
* Business rules.
* Performance metrics.
* Policies and regulations.
* Organizational capabilities.
* Operational constraints.

### Techniques

**Interviews:** Understand stakeholder experiences and responsibilities.

**Observation:** Identify how work is actually performed.

**Document analysis:** Review policies, reports, process manuals, and existing requirements.

**Process modeling:** Represent activities, decisions, and handoffs.

**Data analysis:** Examine volumes, error rates, costs, and performance.

**Capability analysis:** Determine what the organization can currently do and how effectively it performs.

### Current-State Example

An order-processing process has these characteristics:

* Orders arrive by email.
* Employees manually enter order information.
* Inventory is checked using a separate spreadsheet.
* Managers approve unusual orders by email.
* Customers receive status updates only when they request them.

Potential problems include duplicate data entry, inconsistent inventory records, delayed approvals, and poor visibility.

The current-state model should document these issues before a future-state solution is selected.

## 6. Future-State Analysis

The future state describes the desired condition after a successful change.

It should explain:

* What will be different?
* Which business outcomes are expected?
* Which capabilities will be introduced or improved?
* How will stakeholders work differently?
* Which performance measures will demonstrate success?
* What constraints must the future state respect?

### Example

A future-state order process could include:

1. Customers submit orders through a portal.
2. The system validates mandatory information.
3. Inventory availability is checked automatically.
4. Standard orders are processed without unnecessary approval.
5. Exceptions are routed to authorized managers.
6. Customers receive automated status notifications.

The future state should not simply describe software features. It must describe the business improvements those features support.

## 7. Gap Analysis

Gap analysis compares the current state with the future state to identify the changes required.

A gap may involve:

* Missing capabilities.
* Inefficient processes.
* Technology limitations.
* Data-quality problems.
* Skills shortages.
* Policy conflicts.
* Unclear responsibilities.
* Performance shortfalls.

### Example Gap Analysis

| Area             | Current state     | Future state                 | Identified gap              |
| ---------------- | ----------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Order entry      | Manual entry      | Validated online submission  | Digital ordering capability |
| Inventory        | Spreadsheet       | Integrated inventory records | System integration          |
| Approval         | Email-based       | Rule-based workflow          | Approval automation         |
| Customer updates | Manual responses  | Automated notifications      | Communication capability    |
| Performance      | Limited reporting | Real-time dashboard          | Monitoring and analytics    |

Gap analysis provides the basis for defining requirements, estimating change effort, and prioritizing initiatives.

## 8. Business Capabilities

A business capability describes what an organization must be able to do to achieve its objectives.

Examples include:

* Customer relationship management.
* Order fulfillment.
* Financial reporting.
* Supplier management.
* Data governance.
* Regulatory compliance.
* Product development.

A capability is not necessarily a software application or organizational department.

For example, inventory management is a capability that may involve people, processes, data, policies, and technology.

Capability analysis helps identify where investment is needed and prevents organizations from focusing exclusively on IT systems.

## 9. Strategic Options Analysis

After understanding the need and the gaps, the business analyst can help evaluate alternative approaches.

Possible options include:

1. Maintain the current state.
2. Improve the existing process.
3. Purchase a commercial solution.
4. Build a custom application.
5. Outsource part of the process.
6. Combine process improvement with technology implementation.

### Weighted Decision Matrix

Suppose three options are evaluated using four criteria.

Scores range from 1 to 5, where 5 is best.

| Criterion            | Weight | Improve existing system | Buy commercial system | Build custom system |
| -------------------- | -----: | ----------------------: | --------------------: | ------------------: |
| Cost                 |    30% |                       5 |                     3 |                   2 |
| Implementation speed |    25% |                       4 |                     4 |                   2 |
| Functional fit       |    30% |                       3 |                     4 |                   5 |
| Maintainability      |    15% |                       3 |                     4 |                   3 |

Weighted scores:

* Improve existing system: \(5(0.30)+4(0.25)+3(0.30)+3(0.15)=3.80\)
* Buy commercial system: \(3(0.30)+4(0.25)+4(0.30)+4(0.15)=3.65\)
* Build custom system: \(2(0.30)+2(0.25)+5(0.30)+3(0.15)=3.05\)

Under these assumptions, improving the existing system receives the highest score.

However, the result is only as reliable as the criteria, weights, evidence, and estimates. A weighted score supports judgment; it does not replace it.

## 10. Feasibility Analysis

Feasibility analysis assesses whether a proposed change is practical and worthwhile.

Common dimensions include:

**Technical feasibility:** Can the required technology be implemented and supported?

**Operational feasibility:** Can the organization use and sustain the proposed solution?

**Economic feasibility:** Do expected benefits justify the costs?

**Schedule feasibility:** Can the change be delivered within the required time?

**Legal and regulatory feasibility:** Does the option comply with applicable obligations?

**Organizational feasibility:** Is the organization prepared to adopt the change?

### Example

A custom analytics platform may be technically possible but economically impractical for a small business with limited data and budget.

A simpler reporting solution may deliver greater value with less risk.

## 11. Risk Analysis

A risk is an uncertain event or condition that may affect objectives.

Examples include:

* Integration failure.
* Poor data quality.
* Budget overruns.
* Delayed delivery.
* Resistance to change.
* Security vulnerabilities.
* Vendor dependency.
* Lack of user training.

A simple risk score can be calculated as:

Risk score = Probability × Impact

If probability and impact are each scored from 1 to 5, a risk with probability 4 and impact 5 receives a score of 20.

This is a prioritization aid, not a universally standardized measurement.

Risk responses may include:

* Avoiding the risk.
* Reducing its probability or impact.
* Transferring or sharing some exposure.
* Accepting the risk with appropriate authorization.
* Preparing a contingency plan.

## 12. Business Case and Expected Value

A business case explains why an investment or change is justified.

It may include:

* Business need.
* Strategic alignment.
* Alternatives considered.
* Estimated costs.
* Expected benefits.
* Risks and assumptions.
* Constraints.
* Implementation considerations.
* Success measures.

### Financial Measures

**Return on Investment (ROI)**

ROI = (Net Benefit / Investment Cost) × 100%

If an initiative costs $50,000 and produces $70,000 in net benefits:

ROI = ($70,000 / $50,000) × 100% = 140%.

Use the organization's defined accounting convention when distinguishing gross benefits, net benefits, and investment cost.

**Payback Period**

The payback period estimates how long it takes to recover the initial investment.

If a solution costs $60,000 and generates $15,000 in net cash benefits per quarter, the simple payback period is:

$60,000 / $15,000 = 4 quarters.

This calculation assumes consistent benefits and does not account for discounting or uneven cash flows.

## 13. Strategic Alignment

Strategic alignment ensures that a change contributes to organizational priorities.

A project may be technically impressive but strategically weak if it does not support a meaningful business need.

Alignment can be examined through:

* Organizational goals.
* Department objectives.
* Customer needs.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Budget priorities.
* Performance indicators.
* Dependencies with other initiatives.

A useful traceability chain is:

Business need → Business objective → Capability gap → Change initiative → Requirements → Solution → Measured outcome.

This chain helps explain why a requirement exists and how the resulting solution is expected to create value.

## 14. Case Study: University Registration Transformation

### Background

A university processes 12,000 student registrations per semester. Students report long queues, repeated document submissions, and delayed confirmation.

The university wants to improve the registration experience without significantly increasing operating costs.

### Current-State Findings

* Average registration completion time: 3 days.
* 20% of submissions require correction.
* Documents are stored across multiple systems.
* Students receive inconsistent status updates.
* Staff spend substantial time answering routine questions.

### Business Need

Reduce registration effort, improve data accuracy, and provide students with reliable status information.

### Objectives

1. Reduce average registration completion time from 3 days to 1 day within two semesters.
2. Reduce correction rates from 20% to below 5%.
3. Enable students to view registration status online.
4. Maintain compliance with student-data policies.
5. Avoid increasing routine administrative workload.

### Options

* Option A: Improve existing procedures and forms.
* Option B: Introduce an online registration portal.
* Option C: Implement an integrated student information platform.

### Analysis

Option A may be inexpensive but may not resolve fragmented data and status visibility.

Option B may improve submission quality and communication but requires integration planning.

Option C may address more structural problems but carries higher cost, complexity, and implementation risk.

The recommended approach depends on feasibility evidence, budget, architecture, stakeholder needs, and expected benefits. A phased portal implementation may be appropriate if integration and data governance risks can be managed.

### Success Measures

* Average completion time.
* Percentage of submissions requiring correction.
* Number of status-related inquiries.
* Student satisfaction.
* Staff processing hours.
* System availability.
* Compliance incidents.

### Lessons

* Start with the business need rather than a preferred product.
* Establish measurable objectives.
* Understand current-state problems.
* Compare credible alternatives.
* Connect requirements to expected benefits.
* Define how outcomes will be measured.

### Questions

**1. What is the primary purpose of business analysis strategy?**

A. Select software before investigating the problem.
B. Connect business needs, organizational objectives, and necessary change.

**2. Which statement is a business need rather than a solution?**

A. Install a cloud application.
B. Purchase a reporting license.
C. Reduce invoice-processing errors.
D. Develop a mobile application.

**3. What does current-state analysis examine?**

A. Only the desired future system.
B. How the organization operates today.
C. Only project costs.
D. Only software architecture.

**4. What is the main purpose of gap analysis?**

A. Compare current and desired states to identify required changes.
B. Approve all requirements automatically.
C. Replace feasibility analysis.
D. Eliminate business objectives.

**5. Which option best describes a business capability?**

A. A specific application screen.
B. An organizational ability to achieve a business outcome.
C. A project deadline.
D. A single stakeholder interview.

**6. What should guide the selection of a strategic option?**

A. The newest technology.
B. The preference of the loudest stakeholder.
C. Evidence, business value, feasibility, risks, and constraints.
D. The highest implementation cost.

**7. A change has a probability score of 3 and an impact score of 4. What is its simple risk score?**

A. 7
B. 12
C. 1
D. 34

**8. Why should success measures be defined early?**

A. To guarantee that the project cannot fail.
B. To establish how outcomes and benefits will be evaluated.
C. To avoid engaging stakeholders.
D. To remove the need for requirements.

### Answer Key

1. B — Strategy connects the business need to the change required to create value.
2. C — Reducing errors describes a desired business outcome, not a predetermined solution.
3. B — Current-state analysis documents the existing environment and its performance.
4. A — Gap analysis identifies differences that the change must address.
5. B — Capabilities describe what the organization must be able to do.
6. C — Strategic decisions should consider multiple criteria and supporting evidence.
7. B — 3 × 4 = 12.
8. B — Measures establish the basis for evaluating success.

## 16. Revision Summary

* Business analysis strategy links business needs to organizational goals.
* A business need explains why change is necessary.
* Root-cause analysis helps distinguish symptoms from underlying problems.
* Objectives should be measurable and connected to business outcomes.
* Current-state analysis documents existing processes, systems, capabilities, and constraints.
* Future-state analysis describes the desired condition.
* Gap analysis identifies the changes needed to reach the future state.
* Strategic options should be compared using evidence and explicit criteria.
* Feasibility analysis considers technical, operational, economic, schedule, and other relevant constraints.
* Risk analysis supports prioritization and response planning.
* A business case communicates expected value, costs, assumptions, and risks.
* Requirements should remain traceable to business needs and intended outcomes.
